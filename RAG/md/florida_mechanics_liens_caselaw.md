# Florida Construction Lien Compliance – Case Law Analysis and Guidance

## Strict Compliance vs. Substantial Compliance in Lien Law

Florida’s Mechanics’ Lien Law (Part I of Chapter 713, F.S.) is a
**creature of statute, strictly construed** by the
courts[law.justia.com](https://law.justia.com/cases/florida/supreme-court/1991/76234-0.html#:~:text=required%20time.%20Noting%20,its%20lien%20must%20be%20denied)[floridabar.org](https://www.floridabar.org/the-florida-bar-journal/floridas-unwieldy-but-effective-construction-lien-law/#:~:text=The%20lien%20law%E2%80%99s%20strictest%20application,80%C2%A0Determining%20exactly%20when).
A lienor must follow statutory requirements **to the letter** to obtain
and enforce a lien, except where the statute itself permits leniency.
Notably, Florida Statutes §713.37 abolishes any liberal construction in
favor of lien claimants. As the Florida Supreme Court put it,
*“mechanics' liens are purely creatures of statute…strict compliance
is an indispensable prerequisite”* to lien
enforcement[law.justia.com](https://law.justia.com/cases/florida/supreme-court/1991/76234-0.html#:~:text=required%20time.%20Noting%20,its%20lien%20must%20be%20denied).
This strict approach most strongly applies to **time deadlines and
formal notices**, while **minor errors in the lien document’s contents**
may be forgiven if they do not mislead or prejudice the other
party[law.justia.com](https://law.justia.com/cases/florida/supreme-court/1991/76234-0.html#:~:text=e,to%20contractors%2C%20notice%20of%20nonpayment)[descrybe.ai](https://descrybe.ai/case-details/c1930203#:~:text=Application%3A%20The%20court%20concluded%20that,necessitating%20further%20examination%20at%20trial).
The following sections examine key themes – timeliness, service of
notices, lien content defects, fraudulent liens, and enforcement
deadlines – through relevant Florida cases, and distill practical
guidance for contractors, subcontractors, and suppliers (“lienors”)
filing private (non-government) construction liens under today’s law.

## Timeliness of Recording: 90-Day Deadline & “Final Furnishing”

**Florida Statutes §713.08(5)** requires that a Claim of Lien be
recorded **within 90 days of the lienor’s final furnishing of labor,
services, or materials** to the
project[casemine.com](https://www.casemine.com/judgement/us/5914692eadd7b049342d2e6d#:~:text=Second%2C%20the%20lien%20was%20timely,the%20project%20until%20this%20electrical).
Florida courts strictly enforce this 90-day window – a late filing
results in an *invalid lien*, no matter how slight the delay. The
central question often becomes: *what counts as the date of “final
furnishing”*? Courts have clarified that **“final furnishing” means the
last date the lienor actually contributed to the improvement of the
property**[floridabar.org](https://www.floridabar.org/the-florida-bar-journal/floridas-unwieldy-but-effective-construction-lien-law/#:~:text=an%20NTO%2C76%C2%A0and%20final%20furnishing%20no,mine%20from%20which%20its%20raw).
In other words, the clock runs from the last **on-site, contract-related
work or delivery** that is *“necessary to a finished job,”* done in good
faith and within a reasonable
time[casemine.com](https://www.casemine.com/judgement/us/5914692eadd7b049342d2e6d#:~:text=Since%20J,s%20lien%20on%20this%20ground).

Crucially, **mere warranty service, punch-list touch-ups, or corrective
repairs** do *not* extend the deadline – they are not new improvements
but post-completion
tweaks[floridabar.org](https://www.floridabar.org/the-florida-bar-journal/floridas-unwieldy-but-effective-construction-lien-law/#:~:text=improvements%20thereto%2C%20the%20final%20furnishing,80%C2%A0Determining%20exactly%20when).
For example, trivial tasks like cleanup or returning to fix minor
defects after completion don’t count as “final furnishing” under Florida
law[floridabar.org](https://www.floridabar.org/the-florida-bar-journal/floridas-unwieldy-but-effective-construction-lien-law/#:~:text=an%20NTO%2C76%C2%A0and%20final%20furnishing%20no,mine%20from%20which%20its%20raw)[floridabar.org](https://www.floridabar.org/the-florida-bar-journal/floridas-unwieldy-but-effective-construction-lien-law/#:~:text=improvements%20thereto%2C%20the%20final%20furnishing,80%C2%A0Determining%20exactly%20when).
In contrast, work done pursuant to the contract’s scope, or to close out
permit requirements, can reset the 90-day clock. In **J.S.L.
Construction Co. v. Levy**, a contractor’s oversight of final electrical
work and a last walk-through in May 1999 were deemed part of its
contract obligations; thus a lien recorded in July 1999 was within 90
days and **held
timely**[casemine.com](https://www.casemine.com/judgement/us/5914692eadd7b049342d2e6d#:~:text=work%20or%20thereafter%20but%20not,through%20with%20the%20building%20inspector)[casemine.com](https://www.casemine.com/judgement/us/5914692eadd7b049342d2e6d#:~:text=Since%20J,s%20lien%20on%20this%20ground).
The court applied the test from *Aronson v. Keating*, asking whether the
later work was done in good faith, within a reasonable time, under the
contract, and necessary to finish the job – and answered
yes[casemine.com](https://www.casemine.com/judgement/us/5914692eadd7b049342d2e6d#:~:text=and%20all%20other%20subcontract%20work,s%20lien%20on%20this%20ground).

Florida’s courts have wrestled with edge cases on final furnishing. The
**Florida Supreme Court’s decision in** *Stunkel v. Gazebo Landscaping*
illustrates a *narrow* view: there, a subcontractor’s off-site selection
of materials (trees) before any work on the property did **not** count
as beginning to furnish, and the 45-day Notice to Owner period only
began once on-site work
commenced[law.justia.com](https://law.justia.com/cases/florida/supreme-court/1995/84075-0.html#:~:text=DOES%20A%20SUBCONTRACTOR%20BEGIN%20TO,INSTALLATION%20ON%20THE%20JOB%20SITE)[law.justia.com](https://law.justia.com/cases/florida/supreme-court/1995/84075-0.html#:~:text=We%20answer%20the%20certified%20question,materials%20at%20the%20job%20site).
By analogy, “furnishing” (whether first or last) requires actual
performance of work on the job site, under a binding
contract[law.justia.com](https://law.justia.com/cases/florida/supreme-court/1995/84075-0.html#:~:text=We%20answer%20the%20certified%20question,materials%20at%20the%20job%20site)[supremecourt.flcourts.gov](https://supremecourt.flcourts.gov/content/download/363102/file/03-1021_JurisIni.pdf#:~:text=In%20this%20case%2C%20the%204,%E2%80%9Cfurnishing%E2%80%9D%20for%20purposes%20of%20the).
In *Stunkel*, the Court emphasized certainty and strict adherence to the
statutory timeline, underscoring that lien rights cannot be based on
informal or off-site
activities[supremecourt.flcourts.gov](https://supremecourt.flcourts.gov/content/download/363102/file/03-1021_JurisIni.pdf#:~:text=In%20this%20case%2C%20the%204,%E2%80%9Cfurnishing%E2%80%9D%20for%20purposes%20of%20the).

However, a later Fourth DCA case, **Michnal v. Palm Coast Development,
Inc.**, took a slightly more liberal (but fact-specific) view: the
contractor’s *last activity* was sending a **written change-order
proposal** (a fax quoting extra costs) to the owner during a work
stoppage dispute, followed by the owner’s termination of the contract
the next
day[supremecourt.flcourts.gov](https://supremecourt.flcourts.gov/content/download/363102/file/03-1021_JurisIni.pdf#:~:text=match%20at%20L114%20construction%20lien,timely%20%E2%80%93%20that%20is%2C%20filed)[supremecourt.flcourts.gov](https://supremecourt.flcourts.gov/content/download/363102/file/03-1021_JurisIni.pdf#:~:text=Michnal%E2%80%99s%20request%20that%20the%204th,the%20general%20contractor%20to%20the).
Palm Coast recorded its lien within 90 days of that letter, arguing the
letter was part of its contract performance. The 4th DCA agreed – under
those unique circumstances – that the **July 21 fax constituted a
“furnishing” of labor or services**, making the October lien
timely[supremecourt.flcourts.gov](https://supremecourt.flcourts.gov/content/download/363102/file/03-1021_JurisIni.pdf#:~:text=DCA%20concluded%20that%20the%20fax,%28Opinion%20at%205%29%20Thereafter)[supremecourt.flcourts.gov](https://supremecourt.flcourts.gov/content/download/363102/file/03-1021_JurisIni.pdf#:~:text=In%20this%20case%2C%20the%204,%E2%80%9Cfurnishing%E2%80%9D%20for%20purposes%20of%20the).
The court reasoned that the contractor was still attempting to fulfill
the contract (trying to resolve a design impasse) when the letter was
sent, so it treated the correspondence as an integral step in the
work[supremecourt.flcourts.gov](https://supremecourt.flcourts.gov/content/download/363102/file/03-1021_JurisIni.pdf#:~:text=In%20this%20case%2C%20the%204,%E2%80%9Cfurnishing%E2%80%9D%20for%20purposes%20of%20the).
*Michnal* illustrates that courts may examine the **totality of
circumstances** – if work was ongoing or contractually required tasks
(even administrative or supervisory) continued, the final furnishing
date can be
extended[casemine.com](https://www.casemine.com/judgement/us/5914692eadd7b049342d2e6d#:~:text=was%20filed%20on%20July%208%2C,2d%20822%2C%20823)[casemine.com](https://www.casemine.com/judgement/us/5914692eadd7b049342d2e6d#:~:text=and%20all%20other%20subcontract%20work,s%20lien%20on%20this%20ground).
Nevertheless, after *Michnal* was decided, the Florida Supreme Court
declined
review[4dca.flcourts.gov](https://4dca.flcourts.gov/pre_opinion_content_download/164732#:~:text=,citing%20Aronson%20v),
leaving the 4th DCA’s decision intact but not broadly endorsed
statewide. The safer approach is to assume courts will **closely
scrutinize any claimed “last work”**; purely gratuitous or
after-the-fact efforts to extend the lien deadline (such as returning to
tighten a bolt or do “punch list” fixes long after substantial
completion) will likely be
rejected[floridabar.org](https://www.floridabar.org/the-florida-bar-journal/floridas-unwieldy-but-effective-construction-lien-law/#:~:text=an%20NTO%2C76%C2%A0and%20final%20furnishing%20no,mine%20from%20which%20its%20raw)[floridabar.org](https://www.floridabar.org/the-florida-bar-journal/floridas-unwieldy-but-effective-construction-lien-law/#:~:text=improvements%20thereto%2C%20the%20final%20furnishing,80%C2%A0Determining%20exactly%20when).

**Practical Guidance:** Mark the date when you *substantially finish*
your work or delivery under the contract. Absent further required work,
that is your final furnishing date. **Do not rely on minor callbacks,
warranty repairs, or off-site paperwork** to push out the deadline –
Florida courts consistently hold those do not
count[floridabar.org](https://www.floridabar.org/the-florida-bar-journal/floridas-unwieldy-but-effective-construction-lien-law/#:~:text=improvements%20thereto%2C%20the%20final%20furnishing,80%C2%A0Determining%20exactly%20when).
If a project stalls, as in *Michnal*, and you perform
contract-management tasks to attempt to move it forward, document those
activities (e.g. letters, site visits) in case you need to prove they
were part of the agreed work. However, these situations are risky; the
surest course is to **record your Claim of Lien as soon as you are
unpaid and have ceased meaningful work.** Florida law even allows liens
to be recorded during the progress of the
work[cobbgonzalez.com](https://www.cobbgonzalez.com/floridas-construction-liens-laws-the-claim-of-lien/#:~:text=can%20effectively%20record%20a%20construction,lien%20on%20a%20property)
– you need not wait until final completion if payment problems arise.
Once your **90-day window** is running, err on the side of **recording
early** rather than later. A late filing cannot be saved, and owners or
lenders have no obligation to warn you that your time is about to
expire.

Also note that if the prime contract is **terminated** early, §713.08(5)
sets an *alternative* deadline: the lien must be recorded within 90 days
of **termination** *or* last furnishing, whichever is
**earlier**[cobbgonzalez.com](https://www.cobbgonzalez.com/floridas-construction-liens-laws-the-claim-of-lien/#:~:text=The%20claim%20of%20lien%20may,the%20lienor%2C%20whichever%20occurs%20first).
This prevents extending lien rights indefinitely on an abandoned job. In
practice, treat the termination date as you would a last work date. In
all cases, count calendar days (not business days) and remember that day
1 is the day after final furnishing. There is **no “good cause”
exception** for missing the 90-day deadline – one day late is fatal to
the lien.

## Service of Notices: Notice to Owner & Copy of Claim of Lien

For subcontractors, suppliers, and other **non-privity lienors** (those
who did not contract directly with the owner), **timely service of a
Notice to Owner (NTO)** is an essential compliance step. Florida
Statutes §713.06(2) requires that a Notice to Owner be served **within
45 days of commencing to furnish labor or materials** (or before
completion, if
sooner)[law.justia.com](https://law.justia.com/cases/florida/supreme-court/1995/84075-0.html#:~:text=DOES%20A%20SUBCONTRACTOR%20BEGIN%20TO,INSTALLATION%20ON%20THE%20JOB%20SITE)[floridabar.org](https://www.floridabar.org/the-florida-bar-journal/floridas-unwieldy-but-effective-construction-lien-law/#:~:text=specialize%20in%20preparing%20and%20serving,or%20services%20to%20the%20project).
Failure to serve the NTO on time means the lien **“shall be invalid”**
(if the owner hasn’t paid the general contractor in full, the lienor
loses the right to encumber the property for that work). Florida courts
strictly enforce this deadline as well – substantial compliance is not
enough on timing. *For instance, in a leading case,* **Konsler Steel Co.
v. Partin**, a supplier recorded its lien and *then* sent the NTO on day
90 (far past 45 days from first delivery). The Fourth DCA held the lien
invalid for missing the NTO deadline, invoking the strict compliance
rule, but the Florida Supreme Court ultimately reversed on other grounds
unique to that 1970s statutory scheme (involving the owner’s failure to
obtain a contractor’s
affidavit)[law.justia.com](https://law.justia.com/cases/florida/supreme-court/1978/50238-0.html#:~:text=This%20case%20involves%20the%20operation,Jurisdiction%20vests%20pursuant)[law.justia.com](https://law.justia.com/cases/florida/supreme-court/1978/50238-0.html#:~:text=After%20receipt%20of%20notice%2C%20respondent,Steel%20Company%2C%20supra%2C%20at%20685).
Today, however, due to statutory changes (including §713.37’s mandate of
strict construction), courts would not indulge a late Notice to Owner.
The Florida Bar has noted that while the content of an NTO only requires
“substantial compliance,” **the timing requirement demands strict
compliance** – the NTO **must** be received by the owner within 45 days
of first
furnishing[floridabar.org](https://www.floridabar.org/the-florida-bar-journal/floridas-unwieldy-but-effective-construction-lien-law/#:~:text=specialize%20in%20preparing%20and%20serving,or%20services%20to%20the%20project).
In short, if you’re a subcontractor or supplier, **do not miss the
45-day NTO window**, or your later Claim of Lien will be worthless.

Another *service* requirement comes after recording the lien: under
**§713.08(4)(c)**, the lienor should serve a copy of the Claim of Lien
on the owner (and others entitled to it) either before recording or
within **15 days after**
recording[floridabar.org](https://www.floridabar.org/the-florida-bar-journal/floridas-unwieldy-but-effective-construction-lien-law/#:~:text=%E2%80%A2%20Perfecting%20a%20Claim%20of,to%20show%20cause%20why%20the).
Acceptable methods are those in §713.18 (e.g. certified mail, etc.).
Notably, failing to deliver the lien copy *does not invalidate the lien*
in itself – the statute does not provide voiding the lien as a penalty
(and the lien is of public record once recorded
anyway)[floridabar.org](https://www.floridabar.org/the-florida-bar-journal/floridas-unwieldy-but-effective-construction-lien-law/#:~:text=101%20%C2%A0Fla,a%20lien%20against%20the%20property).
However, serving the claim of lien is **strongly
recommended**[floridabar.org](https://www.floridabar.org/the-florida-bar-journal/floridas-unwieldy-but-effective-construction-lien-law/#:~:text=101%20%C2%A0Fla,a%20lien%20against%20the%20property).
First, it is required by the statute, and second, a failure to serve
might deprive the owner of actual notice, potentially affecting an
attorney’s fee claim or goodwill with the court. In practice, most
conscientious lienors do serve the lien on the owner and document that
service. The bottom line is that while an un-served lien might still be
enforceable against the property, you should **always send the owner a
copy of the lien** (via certified mail or another confirmed delivery)
and do so **promptly**. It is an easy step to show your good-faith
compliance.

**Practical Guidance:** If you are a sub or supplier, implement a
**Notice-to-Owner system** on every project. Ideally, serve the NTO
*before or on your first day of work/delivery*. (Florida allows NTO
service anytime up to 45 days after start, but earlier is better.) Use a
trackable delivery (certified mail or an authorized delivery service)
and keep the receipt, as you may later need to prove service. Even if
you contract directly with the owner (so no NTO is required), remember
to **serve a copy of your Claim of Lien** after recording
it[floridabar.org](https://www.floridabar.org/the-florida-bar-journal/floridas-unwieldy-but-effective-construction-lien-law/#:~:text=%E2%80%A2%20Perfecting%20a%20Claim%20of,to%20show%20cause%20why%20the).
Mark the 15-day post-recording window in your calendar and send the lien
by certified mail to the owner’s last known address (and the lender, if
any). Preserve evidence of mailing. Though neglecting to send the lien
copy won’t automatically void your lien, it could undercut your position
if the owner moves to discharge the lien claiming lack of notice.
Moreover, an owner unaware of a lien might sell or refinance the
property, causing complications – prompt service avoids such surprises.

## Claim of Lien Content Requirements and Common Defects

A Claim of Lien in Florida must contain **specific information** as
listed in **§713.08(1)**. These include: (1) the lienor’s name and
address, (2) the name of the party who hired the lienor, (3) the labor,
services, or materials furnished and the contract price or value, (4) a
description of the property sufficient for identification, (5) the
owner’s name, (6) the first and last dates of furnishing, (7) the
amount due (unpaid) and any applicable finance charge, and (8) if not in
privity with the owner, the date and method of serving the Notice to
Owner (and if not in privity with the general contractor, the
date/method of serving a copy of the NTO on the
contractor)[cobbgonzalez.com](https://www.cobbgonzalez.com/floridas-construction-liens-laws-the-claim-of-lien/#:~:text=Florida%20Statute%20%C2%A7713,of%20information%20are%20as%20follows)[cobbgonzalez.com](https://www.cobbgonzalez.com/floridas-construction-liens-laws-the-claim-of-lien/#:~:text=,owner).
The claim of lien must be **signed and sworn to** by the lienor or the
lienor’s agent – typically this means it is notarized, or executed with
an equivalent jurat or
verification[floridabar.org](https://www.floridabar.org/the-florida-bar-journal/floridas-unwieldy-but-effective-construction-lien-law/#:~:text=%E2%80%A2%20Perfecting%20a%20Claim%20of,to%20show%20cause%20why%20the).
Florida provides a statutory form for the Claim of Lien in §713.08(3),
which, if used, will contain all necessary elements in the proper
format[cobbgonzalez.com](https://www.cobbgonzalez.com/floridas-construction-liens-laws-the-claim-of-lien/#:~:text=ethod%20of%20service%20of%20the,on%20the%20contractor%20or%20subcontractor).
Using the statutory form is highly advisable, as it ensures **nothing
vital is omitted**.

Despite the detailed requirements, Florida’s lien law also recognizes
that **minor errors or omissions in a lien claim should not necessarily
invalidate the lien**. §713.08(4)(a) explicitly provides that any
omission or error in the lien details **“shall not prevent the
enforcement of such lien as against one who has not been adversely
affected by such omission or
error.”**[case-law.vlex.com](https://case-law.vlex.com/vid/george-j-motz-const-887816411#:~:text=Section%20%284%29%28a%29%20of%20F,by%20such%20omission%20or%20error).
In other words, **substantial compliance with the content requirements
is sufficient**, so long as any mistake does not prejudice the owner or
another
lienee[case-law.vlex.com](https://case-law.vlex.com/vid/george-j-motz-const-887816411#:~:text=Section%20%284%29%28a%29%20of%20F,by%20such%20omission%20or%20error)[case-law.vlex.com](https://case-law.vlex.com/vid/george-j-motz-const-887816411#:~:text=clear%20that%20substantial%20compliance%20with,2d%20589).
Florida courts have applied this *prejudice test* in numerous cases. For
example, in **George J. Motz Construction Corp. v. Coral Pines, Inc.**,
a contractor’s recorded lien inadvertently named “George J. Motz” (an
individual) as the lienor instead of the correct corporate name “George
J. Motz Construction
Corp.”[case-law.vlex.com](https://case-law.vlex.com/vid/george-j-motz-const-887816411#:~:text=Plaintiff%2C%20George%20J,Motz%20Construction%20Corp)[case-law.vlex.com](https://case-law.vlex.com/vid/george-j-motz-const-887816411#:~:text=designated%20the%20lienor%27s%20namr%20as,Motz%20Construction%20Corp).
The owner seized on this discrepancy and obtained judgment on the
pleadings, arguing no valid lien was filed by the corporation. The
Fourth DCA reversed, holding that **listing the individual’s name
instead of the corporate name was a non-prejudicial clerical error**,
given that the attached contract and context made it clear the
construction corporation was the
claimant[case-law.vlex.com](https://case-law.vlex.com/vid/george-j-motz-const-887816411#:~:text=The%20sole%20question%20for%20this,1)[case-law.vlex.com](https://case-law.vlex.com/vid/george-j-motz-const-887816411#:~:text=Section%20%284%29%28a%29%20of%20F,by%20such%20omission%20or%20error).
Citing §713.08(4)(a), the court stressed that the lien law’s intent is
remedial and protective, and that *“strict compliance with each
technical nicety”* in the lien statement is not required to enforce
it[case-law.vlex.com](https://case-law.vlex.com/vid/george-j-motz-const-887816411#:~:text=Section%20%284%29%28a%29%20of%20F,by%20such%20omission%20or%20error)[case-law.vlex.com](https://case-law.vlex.com/vid/george-j-motz-const-887816411#:~:text=clear%20that%20substantial%20compliance%20with,2d%20589).
The owner in *Motz* was not misled about who the lienor was (the names
“George J. Motz” and the corporation were obviously related and the
address was the same), so the lien remained
enforceable[case-law.vlex.com](https://case-law.vlex.com/vid/george-j-motz-const-887816411#:~:text=clear%20that%20substantial%20compliance%20with,2d%20589).

Similarly, in **Royal Ambassador Condo. Ass’n v. East Coast Supply**, a
supplier filed a single claim of lien against an entire condominium
property for roofing materials, rather than apportioning the lien to
each unit. The association argued the lien was invalid under the
Condominium Act (which requires owner consent for a lien on the whole
property). The Fourth DCA found the lien was overbroad but **not void ab
initio** – it should be enforceable to the extent of each unit’s share
**absent prejudice to any unit
owner**[descrybe.ai](https://descrybe.ai/case-details/c1930203#:~:text=The%20case%20involves%20an%20appeal,appellate%20court%20affirmed%20this%20finding)[descrybe.ai](https://descrybe.ai/case-details/c1930203#:~:text=Application%3A%20The%20court%20concluded%20that,necessitating%20further%20examination%20at%20trial).
The case was remanded to determine if any owners were adversely affected
and to reform the judgment to comply with statutory requirements (e.g.,
adjusting the lien to each condo
unit)[descrybe.ai](https://descrybe.ai/case-details/c1930203#:~:text=consent%20from%20all%20owners%2C%20thereby,instructions%20for%20specific%20adjustments%20to)[descrybe.ai](https://descrybe.ai/case-details/c1930203#:~:text=in%20part%20but%20identified%20that,specific%20adjustments%20to%20the%20judgment).
The key point is the court’s *equitable* approach: **lien mistakes that
can be corrected without harming owners or third parties will not
invalidate the
lien**[descrybe.ai](https://descrybe.ai/case-details/c1930203#:~:text=Application%3A%20The%20court%20concluded%20that,necessitating%20further%20examination%20at%20trial).

Common content mistakes include: misidentifying the owner (or spelling
their name wrong), slight legal description errors, recording the lien
for the wrong amount, or misstating first/last work dates. Under
§713.08(4)(a), courts ask whether such an error actually prejudiced the
owner or any other person entitled to rely on the lien document. If an
error would *reasonably mislead* someone about the claimant or the
property, it’s likely prejudicial. For example, if a lien gives the
wrong legal description such that a purchaser or lender wouldn’t
discover the lien on a title search of the correct property, that error
is serious. But if the error is minor (say, a typo in the lot number
that is still obviously the owner’s parcel, or a transposed letter in a
name), and everyone knows which property and claimant is intended, the
lien can still be
enforced[descrybe.ai](https://descrybe.ai/case-details/c1930203#:~:text=Application%3A%20The%20court%20concluded%20that,necessitating%20further%20examination%20at%20trial).
The burden is often on the owner to show they were **“adversely
affected”** by the
mistake[case-law.vlex.com](https://case-law.vlex.com/vid/george-j-motz-const-887816411#:~:text=Section%20%284%29%28a%29%20of%20F,by%20such%20omission%20or%20error).
Absent that, Florida courts lean toward upholding the lien to fulfill
the statute’s purpose of protecting those who improve real
property[case-law.vlex.com](https://case-law.vlex.com/vid/george-j-motz-const-887816411#:~:text=clear%20that%20substantial%20compliance%20with,2d%20589).

That said, lienors should not become complacent about accuracy.
**Prevention is better than litigation** over whether an error is
harmless. A glaring mistake could invite a court to find the lien
unenforceable (and even if ultimately deemed harmless, it gives the
owner an opening to delay or increase your legal costs). One emerging
issue is **who prepares and signs the lien**. The Claim of Lien must be
signed under oath by the lienor or the lienor’s agent. Some contractors
hire third-party “notice services” or use online platforms to prepare
and even sign liens on their behalf. This can be perilous. In a recent
trial court order (**Lamm & Co. Partners v. Newline WP Services**, 2022,
Orange Cty.), a $253,584 lien was **discharged** because it was prepared
and signed by a non-employee, out-of-state “processing center” agent
(apparently a lien service) rather than by the lienor or its
attorney[hammerngavel.com](https://hammerngavel.com/blog/recent-court-order-discharges-25358400-lien-that-was-prepared-by-third-party-rather-than-employee-or-attorney#:~:text=1,%E2%80%9D)[hammerngavel.com](https://hammerngavel.com/blog/recent-court-order-discharges-25358400-lien-that-was-prepared-by-third-party-rather-than-employee-or-attorney#:~:text=4,the%20facts%20in%20the%20lien).
The court found the signer had *no personal knowledge* of the facts
(indeed, the lien stated the agent was only signing “for purposes of
presenting” the lien and disclaimed
knowledge)[hammerngavel.com](https://hammerngavel.com/blog/recent-court-order-discharges-25358400-lien-that-was-prepared-by-third-party-rather-than-employee-or-attorney#:~:text=belong%20to%20LevelSet).
This lack of a true oath and the act of an unauthorized third party
preparing legal claims were deemed the **unlicensed practice of law**,
violating Florida’s requirement that liens be prepared by the lienor or
a licensed Florida
attorney[hammerngavel.com](https://hammerngavel.com/blog/recent-court-order-discharges-25358400-lien-that-was-prepared-by-third-party-rather-than-employee-or-attorney#:~:text=2,appeared%20to%20belong%20to%20LevelSet)[hammerngavel.com](https://hammerngavel.com/blog/recent-court-order-discharges-25358400-lien-that-was-prepared-by-third-party-rather-than-employee-or-attorney#:~:text=The%20court%20also%20found%20that,the%20unlicensed%20practice%20of%20law).
The court held these irregularities – along with other content errors in
the lien – justified full discharge of the
lien[hammerngavel.com](https://hammerngavel.com/blog/recent-court-order-discharges-25358400-lien-that-was-prepared-by-third-party-rather-than-employee-or-attorney#:~:text=While%20the%20court%20found%20that,21%2C%20Florida%20Statutes).
This order serves as a caution: **rely on qualified professionals and
ensure the person swearing to the lien knows the project’s details**. An
improperly executed lien (wrong “prepared by” or a false/verbatim
notarization without a real oath) can be attacked successfully.

**Practical Guidance:** Double-check every detail on your Claim of Lien
**before** recording. Use the statutory form or a proven template that
tracks §713.08’s
requirements[cobbgonzalez.com](https://www.cobbgonzalez.com/floridas-construction-liens-laws-the-claim-of-lien/#:~:text=ethod%20of%20service%20of%20the,on%20the%20contractor%20or%20subcontractor).
Key points to get right: the **property description** (use the full
legal description from the deed or Notice of Commencement; if
unavailable, use a clear street address and parcel ID, but legal
description is best), the **exact name of the owner** (as per public
records; for a corporate owner, the correct legal name), and your own
name and address. Ensure you correctly state the **first and last
dates** you furnished labor/material – these should align with project
records (delivery tickets, daily logs). The **amount unpaid** should be
calculated carefully; do *not* include amounts not yet due under your
contract or items not provided. (For instance, if your contract included
profit/overhead, that’s fine to include as part of the contract value of
work performed, but do not list “anticipated profit” as a separate item
on a lien for work not completed – this was criticized in cases like
*Viyella Co. v. Gomes*, where a contractor’s inclusion of unearned
profit contributed to a finding of a fraudulent
lien[floridabar.org](https://www.floridabar.org/the-florida-bar-journal/floridas-unwieldy-but-effective-construction-lien-law/#:~:text=for%20the%20court%20to%20find,97).)
If you’re a sub or supplier, make sure you list the date you served the
Notice to Owner and how you served
it[cobbgonzalez.com](https://www.cobbgonzalez.com/floridas-construction-liens-laws-the-claim-of-lien/#:~:text=,due%20under%20the%20lienor%E2%80%99s%20contract)
– omission of that NTO service info *could* mislead someone reviewing
the lien (though courts have forgiven it when the owner obviously
received the NTO in another form).

Absolutely ensure the lien is **properly executed under oath**. Ideally,
an officer of your company or the person with direct knowledge of the
project (e.g., the project manager or credit manager) should sign in
front of a notary, swearing the contents are true. Florida also permits
an unnotarized oath using specific perjury language. For example, in
**J.S.L. Construction v. Levy**, the lienor’s president signed a
declaration on the lien stating: *“Under penalties of perjury, I declare
that I have read the foregoing ... and that the facts stated in it are
true.”*[casemine.com](https://www.casemine.com/judgement/us/5914692eadd7b049342d2e6d#:~:text=Fourth%2C%20the%20statement%20provided%20by,that%20the%20facts%20stated%20in)[casemine.com](https://www.casemine.com/judgement/us/5914692eadd7b049342d2e6d#:~:text=it%20are%20true,1103%20%28Fla).
The Third DCA held this was a valid “oath” per Fla. Stat. §92.525,
equivalent to a notarized
verification[casemine.com](https://www.casemine.com/judgement/us/5914692eadd7b049342d2e6d#:~:text=it%20are%20true,1103%20%28Fla).
Because the owner in *J.S.L.* had not sent a proper sworn Statement of
Account demand (discussed below), the court noted that the lien’s form
was sufficient and distinguished the situation from *Stresscon* (where
no oath was initially provided at
all)[casemine.com](https://www.casemine.com/judgement/us/5914692eadd7b049342d2e6d#:~:text=Other%20portions%20of%20Chapter%20713,%281999%29%20%28same)[casemine.com](https://www.casemine.com/judgement/us/5914692eadd7b049342d2e6d#:~:text=In%20Stresscon%20v,This%20distinguishes%20Stresscon).
So, you may either notarize your lien or include the statutory perjury
clause – but *do one or the other*. Never let a third-party service
“robo-sign” on your behalf without a real oath. If you use a lien
preparation service, **review the document carefully** before it’s
recorded and insist that *you* (or an authorized officer) sign the final
version under oath. Some services will prepare the draft, then send it
to you to sign and notarize – that arrangement is fine. What you want to
avoid is the scenario in *Lamm & Newline*, where the lien was
effectively generated and signed by someone with no connection to the
project[hammerngavel.com](https://hammerngavel.com/blog/recent-court-order-discharges-25358400-lien-that-was-prepared-by-third-party-rather-than-employee-or-attorney#:~:text=3,presenting%20this%20claim%20of%20lien)[hammerngavel.com](https://hammerngavel.com/blog/recent-court-order-discharges-25358400-lien-that-was-prepared-by-third-party-rather-than-employee-or-attorney#:~:text=The%20court%20also%20found%20that,the%20unlicensed%20practice%20of%20law).
Not only could that void your lien, but it might also expose the service
(and possibly you) to legal repercussions. Using a Florida-licensed
**attorney** to prepare and sign the lien on your behalf is permissible
– attorneys can sign as your agent and that is the authorized practice
of law. But if an attorney prepares it, typically they’ll still have you
sign the verification since you have personal knowledge.

In summary, **accuracy and authenticity** are paramount. Florida’s
courts will overlook **harmless, non-misleading mistakes** in a lien
(like a typo or transposed name) to uphold a valid
debt[case-law.vlex.com](https://case-law.vlex.com/vid/george-j-motz-const-887816411#:~:text=Section%20%284%29%28a%29%20of%20F,by%20such%20omission%20or%20error)[descrybe.ai](https://descrybe.ai/case-details/c1930203#:~:text=Application%3A%20The%20court%20concluded%20that,necessitating%20further%20examination%20at%20trial),
but they will not overlook *significant* errors or procedural missteps
that threaten the integrity of the lien process. By carefully complying
with §713.08’s content requirements and double-checking your lien for
errors, you can avoid giving the other side ammunition to challenge your
lien.

## Responding to Owner’s Demands: Sworn Statement of Account (§713.16)

Even after a lien is recorded, a lienor’s compliance obligations aren’t
over. Florida law provides owners with tools to **contest or narrow lien
claims**, and lienors must **strictly adhere** to certain duties in
response. One critical owner right is the **Request for Sworn Statement
of Account** under §713.16(2). An owner (or the owner’s agent or
contractor) may at any time demand that the lienor provide a written,
sworn statement of the lienor’s account – essentially a breakdown of the
labor/materials furnished, the amounts paid to date, and the amount due
(and to become due, if
known)[casemine.com](https://www.casemine.com/judgement/us/5914692eadd7b049342d2e6d#:~:text=Third%2C%20J,in%20substantially%20the%20following%20form)[casemine.com](https://www.casemine.com/judgement/us/5914692eadd7b049342d2e6d#:~:text=,property%20description).
The statute provides a specific form for this request, including a
bolded warning: *“Your failure to furnish the requested statement,
signed under oath, within 30 days…will result in the loss of your
lien.”*[casemine.com](https://www.casemine.com/judgement/us/5914692eadd7b049342d2e6d#:~:text=demand%20by%20an%20owner%20which,in%20substantially%20the%20following%20form)[casemine.com](https://www.casemine.com/judgement/us/5914692eadd7b049342d2e6d#:~:text=,property%20description).
If the owner’s demand is properly made in writing and delivered as the
statute prescribes, the lienor **must comply within 30 days** or forfeit
the lien rights by operation of
law[casemine.com](https://www.casemine.com/judgement/us/5914692eadd7b049342d2e6d#:~:text=%C2%A7%20713,of%20his%20or%20her%20lien).

Florida courts have been unforgiving on this point. In **Home Electric
of Dade County, Inc. v. Gonas**, the Florida Supreme Court held that a
contractor’s failure to timely furnish a statement of account when
demanded caused the lien to be lost – even though the owner’s demand
letter in that case did *not* mention the 30-day deadline as required by
the
statute[law.justia.com](https://law.justia.com/cases/florida/supreme-court/1991/76234-0.html#:~:text=This%20Court%27s%20decision%20in%20Home,Home)[law.justia.com](https://law.justia.com/cases/florida/supreme-court/1991/76234-0.html#:~:text=Electric%2C%20547%20So,its%20lien%20must%20be%20denied).
The Court emphasized that *“strict compliance”* with statutory
prerequisites was required for a lienor seeking affirmative
relief[law.justia.com](https://law.justia.com/cases/florida/supreme-court/1991/76234-0.html#:~:text=required%20time.%20Noting%20,its%20lien%20must%20be%20denied).
By not responding in time, the contractor sacrificed an otherwise valid
lien, and the lien was
denied[law.justia.com](https://law.justia.com/cases/florida/supreme-court/1991/76234-0.html#:~:text=Electric%2C%20547%20So,its%20lien%20must%20be%20denied)[law.justia.com](https://law.justia.com/cases/florida/supreme-court/1991/76234-0.html#:~:text=As%20this%20Court%20has%20stated,denial%20of%20the%20mechanic%27s%20lien).

Likewise, in **Stresscon v. Madiedo**, a subcontractor (*Stresscon*) did
send a timely *written* statement of account within 30 days of the
owner’s demand, but the statement was **not notarized** (not
sworn)[law.justia.com](https://law.justia.com/cases/florida/supreme-court/1991/76234-0.html#:~:text=work%20completed%2C%20Madiedo%20sent%20a,provided%20an%20affidavit%20swearing%20to)[law.justia.com](https://law.justia.com/cases/florida/supreme-court/1991/76234-0.html#:~:text=This%20Court%27s%20decision%20in%20Home,strict%20compliance%20is%20an%20indispensable).
When the owner moved for summary judgment to discharge the lien,
Stresscon belatedly attempted to cure the defect by providing an
affidavit verifying the earlier
statement[law.justia.com](https://law.justia.com/cases/florida/supreme-court/1991/76234-0.html#:~:text=Thereafter%2C%20Stresscon%20sent%20a%20timely,We%20approve%20the%20result%20below).
The Florida Supreme Court held that this late verification could not
save the lien. Because §713.16(2) explicitly required the statement to
be “under oath” and included no exception for substantial compliance,
the failure to supply a sworn statement within 30 days was fatal –
*prejudice to the owner was
irrelevant*[law.justia.com](https://law.justia.com/cases/florida/supreme-court/1991/76234-0.html#:~:text=The%20fact%20that%20no%20prejudice,who%20has%20not%20been%20adversely)[law.justia.com](https://law.justia.com/cases/florida/supreme-court/1991/76234-0.html#:~:text=following%20form,the%20validity%20of%20a%20lien).
The Court rejected arguments that no harm was done or that the omission
was cured later; in lien law, if the statute doesn’t allow a cure or
excuse, the courts will not create
one[law.justia.com](https://law.justia.com/cases/florida/supreme-court/1991/76234-0.html#:~:text=e,to%20contractors%2C%20notice%20of%20nonpayment)[law.justia.com](https://law.justia.com/cases/florida/supreme-court/1991/76234-0.html#:~:text=As%20this%20Court%20has%20stated,denial%20of%20the%20mechanic%27s%20lien).
The lien was discharged due to the unverified statement, despite it
being accurate and on
time[law.justia.com](https://law.justia.com/cases/florida/supreme-court/1991/76234-0.html#:~:text=work%20completed%2C%20Madiedo%20sent%20a,relying%20upon%20Home%20Electric%20of)[law.justia.com](https://law.justia.com/cases/florida/supreme-court/1991/76234-0.html#:~:text=As%20this%20Court%20has%20stated,denial%20of%20the%20mechanic%27s%20lien).

These cases make clear that an owner’s demand for a statement of account
is a **powerful weapon**: it can effectively shorten the life of a lien
if the lienor is not extremely diligent. However, lienors should also
note the procedural requirements on the owner. Section 713.16(2) says
the owner’s request “*must be in substantially the \[statutory\] form*”
including the warning about loss of lien
rights[casemine.com](https://www.casemine.com/judgement/us/5914692eadd7b049342d2e6d#:~:text=Third%2C%20J,in%20substantially%20the%20following%20form)[casemine.com](https://www.casemine.com/judgement/us/5914692eadd7b049342d2e6d#:~:text=,property%20description).
If an owner’s letter omits that statutory warning or is not *written* at
all, a lienor might argue it wasn’t a valid demand (and thus failure to
respond shouldn’t forfeit the lien). In **J.S.L. Construction Co. v.
Levy**, the owners argued the lien should be discharged for not
providing a sworn account, but the record showed *no proper written
demand* was ever served on the
lienor[casemine.comcasemine.com](https://www.casemine.com/judgement/us/5914692eadd7b049342d2e6d#:~:text=J,provide%20a%20statement%20under%20oath).
The Third DCA agreed that in the absence of a demonstrated written
request in the statutory form, the contractor’s lien could not be
defeated on that
ground[casemine.com](https://www.casemine.com/judgement/us/5914692eadd7b049342d2e6d#:~:text=J,provide%20a%20statement%20under%20oath).
*J.S.L.* further noted that, even if a demand had been made, the
lienor’s unsworn but **“penalty of perjury”** verified statement
likely satisfied the “under oath” requirement under current
law[casemine.com](https://www.casemine.com/judgement/us/5914692eadd7b049342d2e6d#:~:text=Fourth%2C%20the%20statement%20provided%20by,that%20the%20facts%20stated%20in)[casemine.com](https://www.casemine.com/judgement/us/5914692eadd7b049342d2e6d#:~:text=it%20are%20true,1103%20%28Fla)
(Florida law in 1994 was amended to accept unsworn declarations as
“oaths” – something not available at the time of *Stresscon* in 1991).
The court distinguished *Stresscon* on that basis: in *Stresscon*, the
lienor provided **no attestation whatsoever** until after the 30 days
expired, whereas *J.S.L.*’s president did certify the statement (just
not notarized, but acceptable by statute) and, importantly, the owners
never properly demanded it in the first
place[casemine.com](https://www.casemine.com/judgement/us/5914692eadd7b049342d2e6d#:~:text=In%20Stresscon%20v,This%20distinguishes%20Stresscon).

**Practical Guidance:** As a lienor, you must be prepared to **respond
immediately to any §713.16 demand**. Treat any correspondence from the
owner (or general contractor or attorney for same) asking for an
“accounting” or “itemization of your lien” as a potential 30-day
demand – compare it to the statutory form. If it substantially tracks
that language (especially if it includes the bold warning about losing
your lien), the clock is ticking. **Mark the deadline** (30 days from
receipt) and ensure you provide a **written statement under oath** to
the requesting party within that time. The statement should detail: the
nature of the labor/materials performed to date, the materials still to
be furnished (if any), the amount paid so far, the amount currently due,
and the amount to become due (if
known)[casemine.com](https://www.casemine.com/judgement/us/5914692eadd7b049342d2e6d#:~:text=,property%20description).
Essentially, it’s a snapshot of your contract status and lien claim. Be
truthful and as exact as possible – an intentionally false or “padded”
statement in response can itself be deemed a *fraudulent lien* act under
§713.31. Deliver the response in a provable way (e.g., certified mail or
email/fax with confirmation, as allowed). If you cannot gather the
information immediately, **do not ignore the request or assume you can
stall** – Florida law provides no extension. Even if the owner’s request
was missing the official warning, it’s risky to bank on that
technicality; best practice is to respond timely with a sworn statement
regardless, which moots any argument. By doing so, you preserve your
lien and display good faith. Non-response or a late response will hand
the owner a valid reason to petition the court to discharge your lien
(and likely win attorney’s fees against you for doing so).

If you believe the owner’s request letter was defective (say, missing
the warning or not properly addressed), you might have legal grounds to
contest a lien discharge. But that would entail litigation and
uncertainty – precisely what you want to avoid when securing payment.
Thus, **always err in favor of compliance**: respond in 30 days, in
writing, under oath. It’s a relatively simple document to produce
compared to the value of your lien rights.

## Fraudulent Liens: Willful Exaggeration and Its Consequences

Florida takes a hard line against **fraudulent liens**. Under
**§713.31(2)(a)**, a construction lien is deemed *fraudulent* if the
lienor has **willfully exaggerated** the amount, **willfully included
work or materials not performed or furnished**, or asserted lien rights
for work not authorized under the contract (or has compiled the claim
with willful and gross negligence). In essence, if a lienor
intentionally overstates the lien, the lien can be declared fraudulent
and unenforceable. The **penalties** for a fraudulent lien are severe:
the lien is extinguished, and the lienor can be made to pay the owner’s
damages, which **must** include reasonable attorney’s fees and court
costs incurred by the owner in defending or attacking the
lien[caselaw.findlaw.com](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/fl-district-court-of-appeal/1380588.html#:~:text=Paragraph%20713,to%20ignorance%20or%20good%20faith).
Furthermore, a lienor who **willfully files a fraudulent lien commits a
third-degree felony** under
§713.31(3)[flsenate.gov](https://www.flsenate.gov/Laws/Statutes/2012/713.31#:~:text=Any%20person%20who%20willfully%20files,082)
– although criminal prosecutions are rare, the statute’s presence
underscores the seriousness of filing an inflated lien.

It’s important to distinguish a *fraudulent lien* from an ordinary
**disputed lien**. Florida courts have developed guidance through case
law: a **good-faith dispute or minor error in the lien amount** will
*not* automatically render a lien
fraudulent[floridabar.org](https://www.floridabar.org/the-florida-bar-journal/floridas-unwieldy-but-effective-construction-lien-law/#:~:text=Exaggerated%20lien%20amounts%20may%20also,3d%20DCA%202008%29%2C%20reduced)[floridabar.org](https://www.floridabar.org/the-florida-bar-journal/floridas-unwieldy-but-effective-construction-lien-law/#:~:text=entitled%20to%20the%20amount%20claimed,90).
The key inquiry is the lienor’s **intent** and the magnitude of the
exaggeration. As the Third DCA explained in **Delta Painting, Inc. v.
Baumann**, *“the lienor's intent and good or bad faith in filing a lien
must be based on competent substantial
evidence”*[caselaw.findlaw.com](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/fl-district-court-of-appeal/1380588.html#:~:text=court%20said%3A)[caselaw.findlaw.com](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/fl-district-court-of-appeal/1380588.html#:~:text=unauthorized%20by%20the%20Baumanns,court%27s%20conclusions%20in%20this%20regard).
If the evidence shows the lienor knowingly and deliberately claimed
amounts not earned or not allowed, then the lien is fraudulent – and
once that bad faith is proven, *“a finding of a fraudulent lien by a
trial court is not a discretionary matter”* (the court must deem it
fraudulent)[floridabar.org](https://www.floridabar.org/the-florida-bar-journal/floridas-unwieldy-but-effective-construction-lien-law/#:~:text=Ten%20years%20earlier%2C%20the%20same,admitted%20that%20he%20hadn%E2%80%99t%20really)[floridabar.org](https://www.floridabar.org/the-florida-bar-journal/floridas-unwieldy-but-effective-construction-lien-law/#:~:text=Baumann%20%2C%20710%20So,%E2%80%9D92%C2%A0More%20recently%2C%20the%20Fifth%20District).
In *Delta Painting*, for instance, the contractor’s lien included
charges for additional work that the homeowners had not authorized, and
even claims for work that was never performed at
all[caselaw.findlaw.com](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/fl-district-court-of-appeal/1380588.html#:~:text=We%20begin%20our%20review%20by,We%20think).
The lienor apparently *knew* these items were not rightful but included
them anyway. The Third DCA held such evidence was *“wholly inconsistent
with any notion of a minor mistake or good faith dispute”* and affirmed
the trial court’s ruling that the lien was fraudulent as a matter of
fact[caselaw.findlaw.com](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/fl-district-court-of-appeal/1380588.html#:~:text=We%20begin%20our%20review%20by,We%20think)[caselaw.findlaw.com](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/fl-district-court-of-appeal/1380588.html#:~:text=included%20a%20claim%20for%20work,court%27s%20conclusions%20in%20this%20regard).

Contrast that with **Politano v. GPA Construction Group**, a 2008 Third
DCA case with different facts. There, a contractor’s lien was arguably
overstated because it included some non-lienable items (like a separate
line for overhead/profit, and possibly other costs not strictly part of
the
improvement)[caselaw.findlaw.com](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/fl-district-court-of-appeal/1380588.html#:~:text=Paragraph%20713,to%20ignorance%20or%20good%20faith)[caselaw.findlaw.com](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/fl-district-court-of-appeal/1380588.html#:~:text=is%20claimed%20or%20in%20which,to%20ignorance%20or%20good%20faith).
The owner moved to discharge the lien as fraudulent. The trial court
indeed *reduced* the lienable amount but *declined to find fraud*,
crediting the contractor’s testimony that he believed in good faith he
was entitled to those
amounts[floridabar.org](https://www.floridabar.org/the-florida-bar-journal/floridas-unwieldy-but-effective-construction-lien-law/#:~:text=entitled%20to%20the%20amount%20claimed,90)[caselaw.findlaw.com](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/fl-district-court-of-appeal/1380588.html#:~:text=The%20court%20made%20reductions%20in,the%20lien%20was%20not%20fraudulent).
The trial judge even noted the contractor’s **demeanor** and found no
willful exaggeration – essentially concluding the overstatement resulted
from mistake or dispute, not intent to
defraud[floridabar.org](https://www.floridabar.org/the-florida-bar-journal/floridas-unwieldy-but-effective-construction-lien-law/#:~:text=entitled%20to%20the%20amount%20claimed,90)[caselaw.findlaw.com](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/fl-district-court-of-appeal/1380588.html#:~:text=legitimate%20dispute%20over%20some%20items%2C,the%20lien%20was%20not%20fraudulent).
On appeal, the Third DCA **affirmed** that decision, holding that the
trial court did not abuse its discretion in refusing to label the lien
fraudulent[caselaw.findlaw.com](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/fl-district-court-of-appeal/1380588.html#:~:text=find%20that%20a%20lien%20was,and%20not%20a%20willful%20exaggeration)[caselaw.findlaw.com](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/fl-district-court-of-appeal/1380588.html#:~:text=This%20is%20an%20appeal%20in,and%20not%20a%20willful%20exaggeration).
The appellate court distinguished an earlier case, *Martin v. Jack Yanks
Constr. Co.* (another Third DCA case), where the contractor had done
“little to no work” yet filed a lien for the full contract amount –
clearly a case of **brazen exaggeration** without any valid
basis[caselaw.findlaw.com](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/fl-district-court-of-appeal/1380588.html#:~:text=The%20owners%27%20reliance%20on%20Martin,at%20122).
In *Martin*, the entire lien was far beyond anything that could have
been owed, and it was deemed fraudulent *per se*. By contrast, in
*Politano*, the parties had a valid long-term contract, substantial work
was done, and the dispute was over the percentage of completion and
certain
charges[caselaw.findlaw.com](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/fl-district-court-of-appeal/1380588.html#:~:text=Here%2C%20the%20parties%20not%20only,the%20owners%20terminated%20the%20contractor)[caselaw.findlaw.com](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/fl-district-court-of-appeal/1380588.html#:~:text=was%20compensable%20based%20on%20the,the%20owners%20terminated%20the%20contractor).
The **Politano** court made an important point: just because a court
*reduces* a lien’s amount during litigation does *not* automatically
mean the lien was
fraudulent[caselaw.findlaw.com](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/fl-district-court-of-appeal/1380588.html#:~:text=The%20owners%20argue%20that%20under,This%20court%20said).
A reduction might occur simply because some claimed items turned out not
to be lienable or were unproved – but if the lienor had a reasonable
foundation for them, the lien can still be in good
faith[caselaw.findlaw.com](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/fl-district-court-of-appeal/1380588.html#:~:text=The%20owners%20argue%20that%20under,This%20court%20said)[caselaw.findlaw.com](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/fl-district-court-of-appeal/1380588.html#:~:text=the%20lienor%27s%20intent%20and%20good,finding%2C%20we%20cannot%20disturb%20the).
Good faith can be a defense to a fraud allegation, but it must be
supported by evidence (the lienor’s testimony, documentation,
etc.)[floridabar.org](https://www.floridabar.org/the-florida-bar-journal/floridas-unwieldy-but-effective-construction-lien-law/#:~:text=A%20lien%20is%20deemed%20fraudulent,willfully%20exaggerate%20the%20lien%2C%E2%80%9D%20a)[floridabar.org](https://www.floridabar.org/the-florida-bar-journal/floridas-unwieldy-but-effective-construction-lien-law/#:~:text=entitled%20to%20the%20amount%20claimed,90).
The Fifth DCA has similarly noted that a “good-faith dispute or minor
mistake” does not **compel** a finding that a lien is non-fraudulent –
i.e., even a small error could be fraud if intentional, but conversely,
even a large error might not be fraud if there’s credible proof of good
faith[floridabar.org](https://www.floridabar.org/the-florida-bar-journal/floridas-unwieldy-but-effective-construction-lien-law/#:~:text=Ten%20years%20earlier%2C%20the%20same,admitted%20that%20he%20hadn%E2%80%99t%20really)[floridabar.org](https://www.floridabar.org/the-florida-bar-journal/floridas-unwieldy-but-effective-construction-lien-law/#:~:text=faith%20has%20been%20determined%20by,95%C2%A0Delta%20Painting%20also%20included).
Ultimately, the determination is factual: did the lienor willfully
overstate the lien?

Other cases illustrate the extremes: In **Viyella Co. v. Gomes**, a
contractor filed a $107,000 lien but later *admitted in court that he
hadn’t really performed much of the work
claimed*[floridabar.org](https://www.floridabar.org/the-florida-bar-journal/floridas-unwieldy-but-effective-construction-lien-law/#:~:text=has%20pointed%20out%20that%20such,97)[floridabar.org](https://www.floridabar.org/the-florida-bar-journal/floridas-unwieldy-but-effective-construction-lien-law/#:~:text=for%20the%20court%20to%20find,97).
In fact, another company (Delta Painting, presumably the same Delta as
in the case above) had performed no work at all for its
lien[floridabar.org](https://www.floridabar.org/the-florida-bar-journal/floridas-unwieldy-but-effective-construction-lien-law/#:~:text=has%20pointed%20out%20that%20such,97).
Such blatant misrepresentation led the court easily to conclude the
liens were fraudulent. Additionally, the *Viyella/Delta* scenario
involved the contractor including overhead and profit as separate lien
line-items on work not done – the Third DCA held that was **“not within
the purview of the lien law”** (essentially an improper padding of the
lien)[floridabar.org](https://www.floridabar.org/the-florida-bar-journal/floridas-unwieldy-but-effective-construction-lien-law/#:~:text=has%20pointed%20out%20that%20such,97)[floridabar.org](https://www.floridabar.org/the-florida-bar-journal/floridas-unwieldy-but-effective-construction-lien-law/#:~:text=for%20the%20court%20to%20find,97).
On the flip side, an example of leniency is the trial court’s ruling in
*Politano*, where inclusion of some nonlienable items was seen as an
error but not a willful fraud given the contractor’s credible good-faith
belief[floridabar.org](https://www.floridabar.org/the-florida-bar-journal/floridas-unwieldy-but-effective-construction-lien-law/#:~:text=entitled%20to%20the%20amount%20claimed,90)[floridabar.org](https://www.floridabar.org/the-florida-bar-journal/floridas-unwieldy-but-effective-construction-lien-law/#:~:text=court%20in%20%20Politano%20v,90).

**Consequences:** If a court finds a lien to be fraudulent,
§713.31(2)(b) provides that the lien is **voided** (not just reduced),
and the lienor can be ordered to pay the owner’s costs and attorney’s
fees incurred in the action of discharging the lien. Often, the owner
will file a counterclaim or separate action alleging fraudulent lien to
recover these fees and any other damages (for instance, if the
fraudulent lien prevented a property sale, carrying costs could be
claimed). Moreover, §713.31(3) allows the owner to refer the matter for
criminal prosecution as a third-degree
felony[flsenate.gov](https://www.flsenate.gov/Laws/Statutes/2012/713.31#:~:text=Any%20person%20who%20willfully%20files,082)
– while this is uncommon, it remains a sobering possibility for
egregious cases. And importantly, a lienor who loses a fraudulent lien
case will also lose any right to recover **their** attorney’s fees in
the foreclosure (since they are not the prevailing party – the owner
is). It is truly a lose-everything scenario for the lienor.

**Practical Guidance:** The best protection against a fraudulent lien
claim is *accuracy and honesty*. **Never deliberately inflate your
lien** as a negotiating tactic. Florida’s lien law does *not* allow
“gaming” the amount – it’s not a mere starting point for talks; it
must reflect your real, supportable claim. So, **calculate your lien
amount carefully**: it should equal the unpaid portion of your contract
work (including approved change orders or extras) that is lienable. If
you include work not yet done or materials not yet delivered, you are
crossing the line. If you include items that are not lienable (for
example, a claim for purely personal services, or certain consequential
damages), you risk a fraud finding – better to leave those out of the
lien and pursue them in a breach of contract lawsuit instead. **Document
the basis** for your lien figures. If you’re claiming $X for labor and
$Y for materials and $Z for change orders, have the invoices,
timesheets, or change order logs to back it up. Should the owner
challenge the lien as fraudulent, you want to demonstrate that you
genuinely believed you were owed the full amount.

Be mindful that **routine slight mistakes** (e.g., a math error that
overstates the total by a small percentage) typically won’t be deemed
“willful.” But significant overstatements – even if you think it’s
negotiating leverage – can jeopardize your entire claim. For example, do
not claim interest, attorney fees, or other add-ons in the lien amount;
those may be recoverable in a judgment *separate* from the lien, but
they are not part of the lien itself and could inflate the lien figure
improperly. Likewise, **do not include non-lienable work** (like purely
supervisory work not connected to improvement, or repair work done after
final furnishing, or materials that were ordered but not incorporated)
in your lien total. Florida courts look harshly on lienors who appear to
be overreaching.

If an owner asserts your lien is fraudulent, the courts will examine
your **intent**. Showing that you consulted with an attorney or that you
followed a standard approach in good faith can help rebut allegations of
willfulness. For instance, including 10% overhead and profit on a
cost-of-work lien might be standard under your contract – if so, clarify
that in your records or lien narrative to show you weren’t just making
up numbers. Conversely, avoid the temptation to “throw in” unrelated
costs (like damages for project delays or unapproved change requests)
into the lien – those are separate issues. Remember, a **fraudulent lien
= no lien at all** and a hefty fee award to the
owner[caselaw.findlaw.com](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/fl-district-court-of-appeal/1380588.html#:~:text=Paragraph%20713,to%20ignorance%20or%20good%20faith)[hammerngavel.com](https://hammerngavel.com/blog/recent-court-order-discharges-25358400-lien-that-was-prepared-by-third-party-rather-than-employee-or-attorney#:~:text=Finally%2C%20and%20perhaps%20less%20obviously%2C,point%20of%20being%20made%20whole).
It’s far better to claim a conservative, supportable lien amount and
preserve your rights to that, than to claim an inflated sum and lose
everything. If you truly are unsure about part of your claim’s validity
under the lien law, consult with counsel and err on the side of caution.
You can always pursue additional compensation through other legal
theories (breach of contract, quantum meruit) but your lien should be a
fortress of well-founded claims, not a grab-bag of every dollar you wish
to claim.

## Enforcement and Duration: One-Year Deadline, Contest Proceedings, and Attorney’s Fees

Recording a claim of lien is only step one; enforcing it is step two. A
lien gives you a right to force a sale of the property (foreclosure) to
get paid, but **Florida imposes strict time limits on enforcement**.
Under **§713.22(1)**, a lien is generally effective for **one year**
from the date of recording. If you do nothing in that year, the lien
automatically **expires by operation of law** (i.e. it becomes
unenforceable)[floridabar.org](https://www.floridabar.org/the-florida-bar-journal/floridas-unwieldy-but-effective-construction-lien-law/#:~:text=the%20NOC,105).
There is no need for the owner to file anything for that to happen – the
statute itself provides that an **unenforced lien “shall expire and
become null and void”** after one year unless a lawsuit to foreclose the
lien is filed in that
time[cobbgonzalez.com](https://www.cobbgonzalez.com/floridas-construction-liens-laws-the-claim-of-lien/#:~:text=WARNING%21).
(Sometimes, the lienor and owner will stipulate to extend the lien or
the lienor will voluntarily discharge and re-record a lien, but those
maneuvers have their own complexities and are not typical. The safest
course is to treat one year as a hard deadline.)

Moreover, owners have statutory mechanisms to **shorten** that one-year
period. Under **§713.21(4)**, an owner or interested party can file a
**Complaint (or Motion) to Show Cause** in court, essentially requiring
the lienor to appear before a judge (on an expedited basis) and show
cause why the lien should not be enforced or
vacated[floridabar.org](https://www.floridabar.org/the-florida-bar-journal/floridas-unwieldy-but-effective-construction-lien-law/#:~:text=the%20time%20of%20recording,to%2060%20days%20by%20recording).
The court will issue an order to the lienor setting a hearing (typically
at least 20 days out). If the lienor wants to keep the lien, they must
**file a foreclosure lawsuit (or already have one filed) and show the
court that it has been filed** within that 20-day
window[floridabar.org](https://www.floridabar.org/the-florida-bar-journal/floridas-unwieldy-but-effective-construction-lien-law/#:~:text=the%20time%20of%20recording,to%2060%20days%20by%20recording).
If the lienor fails to **commence an action** to enforce the lien (or
fails to show sufficient cause why the lien should remain) within 20
days, the court *shall* order the lien extinguished under
§713.21(4)[floridabar.org](https://www.floridabar.org/the-florida-bar-journal/floridas-unwieldy-but-effective-construction-lien-law/#:~:text=the%20time%20of%20recording,105).
This is known as the **“show cause” process**, and it is a quick way to
clear stale or suspect liens.

Alternatively, under **§713.22(2)**, an owner may record a **Notice of
Contest of Lien** in the public records. When such a notice is recorded
(and properly served on the lienor), it has the effect of **shortening
the lien’s life to 60 days** from the date of service of the
notice[floridabar.org](https://www.floridabar.org/the-florida-bar-journal/floridas-unwieldy-but-effective-construction-lien-law/#:~:text=the%20time%20of%20recording,105).
If the lienor does not file a foreclosure lawsuit within those 60 days,
the lien expires by statute (specifically, §713.22(2) says the lien
shall be extinguished at the end of the 60 days). The clerk of court
will mail a copy of the Notice of Contest to the lienor at the address
in the claim of lien, which starts the clock. This is an even *simpler*
method for owners to prompt action – no court hearing needed; it’s a
self-executing time reduction.

Florida courts uphold these truncation procedures strictly. As a lienor,
if you ignore a recorded Notice of Contest and miss the 60-day deadline,
your lien is gone, no excuses. Likewise, if you receive a show-cause
summons and do not file your foreclosure complaint (or counterclaim in
that proceeding) within the time allowed, you lose the lien. A recent
scenario illustrating enforcement pitfalls is the **Lamm & Newline**
case referenced earlier. There, after the contractor recorded its lien,
the owner transferred the lien to a security bond (per §713.24) and then
**filed a complaint under §713.21 to discharge the
lien**[hammerngavel.com](https://hammerngavel.com/blog/recent-court-order-discharges-25358400-lien-that-was-prepared-by-third-party-rather-than-employee-or-attorney#:~:text=In%20Company%20Partners%20LLC%20dba,21%2C%20Florida%20Statutes)[hammerngavel.com](https://hammerngavel.com/blog/recent-court-order-discharges-25358400-lien-that-was-prepared-by-third-party-rather-than-employee-or-attorney#:~:text=bond%20in%20the%20amount%20of,21%2C%20Florida%20Statutes).
The contractor filed an *answer* to that lawsuit but apparently did not
initiate a separate foreclosure action on the bond. The court found that
merely answering the owner’s discharge suit was not the same as
**affirmatively filing an action to enforce the lien/bond
claim**[hammerngavel.com](https://hammerngavel.com/blog/recent-court-order-discharges-25358400-lien-that-was-prepared-by-third-party-rather-than-employee-or-attorney#:~:text=While%20the%20court%20found%20that,21%2C%20Florida%20Statutes).
Because the contractor hadn’t filed its own foreclosure claim, the court
ruled the lien (bond) should be discharged for failure to comply with
§713.21’s mandate to commence an action within the prescribed
time[hammerngavel.com](https://hammerngavel.com/blog/recent-court-order-discharges-25358400-lien-that-was-prepared-by-third-party-rather-than-employee-or-attorney#:~:text=While%20the%20court%20found%20that,21%2C%20Florida%20Statutes).
This example shows that lienors must be proactive – don’t assume that
fighting off a discharge motion is enough; you need to be the party
pressing a foreclosure claim within the allowed period.

**Practical Guidance:** **Diary your deadlines.** From the moment you
record a lien, note the one-year anniversary date. That is your
*ultimate* deadline to file a lawsuit to foreclose the lien (in circuit
court, usually accompanied by a lis pendens on the property). In many
cases, you will try to negotiate payment before resorting to suit. But
**do not let negotiations lull you past the deadline**. The statute has
no provision for extension by agreement (except the owner could
voluntarily record something like an extension, which is rare). If the
year is nearing its end and you’re not fully paid, file suit before the
year runs, even if settlement talks are ongoing. You can always dismiss
the suit later if payment comes. Once you sue to foreclose and record a
**Lis Pendens**, the lien is preserved pending the outcome, and third
parties are deemed to have notice of your
claim[floridabar.org](https://www.floridabar.org/the-florida-bar-journal/floridas-unwieldy-but-effective-construction-lien-law/#:~:text=one%20year%20of%20the%20date,105).

Be vigilant for any **Notice of Contest of Lien** from the owner. If you
get one, treat it as a flashing red alarm – you now have only **60
days** (not a day more) to file your foreclosure
action[floridabar.org](https://www.floridabar.org/the-florida-bar-journal/floridas-unwieldy-but-effective-construction-lien-law/#:~:text=the%20time%20of%20recording,105).
The clerk’s mailing of the notice to your lien address is key; ensure
your lien listed a reliable address. If you move or if the address was
wrong, you might not get the notice, but it will still cut off your lien
(constructive notice via recording is enough). Therefore, if several
months have passed with no payment, it might be wise to file suit before
the owner even files a contest notice – beat them to the punch and you
won’t have to worry about a shortened deadline.

Similarly, if you are served with a **rule to show cause** or any court
paperwork aimed at discharging the lien, get your foreclosure claim
filed *immediately*. Often, the best course is to file your foreclosure
complaint and **answer the show-cause order by pointing to your filed
lawsuit** (attaching a copy and the filing info). Florida’s summary
procedure for show-cause (Florida Stat. §713.21(4)) is unforgiving on
delay – the court can discharge the lien at that show-cause hearing if
you haven’t complied with the
statute[floridabar.org](https://www.floridabar.org/the-florida-bar-journal/floridas-unwieldy-but-effective-construction-lien-law/#:~:text=the%20time%20of%20recording,105).
Therefore, treat any such motion or order as high priority and involve
your construction attorney at once.

When you do file a foreclosure action, remember to include all necessary
parties (the property owner, the contractor if you’re a sub, any surety
if the lien was bonded off, any other lienors or judgment creditors, and
any mortgage lenders if foreclosure of the property is involved). An
action **to enforce the lien on the property must be filed within the
time limit** or the lien dies, but note you could still sue the
breaching party in contract even after the lien is gone – you just lose
the property as security. Some lienors choose to sue in contract earlier
(especially if privity with the owner exists) and include a lien
foreclosure count in the same suit. That’s fine, as long as it’s done
timely.

**Attorney’s Fees:** Florida’s lien statute (§713.29) provides that the
**prevailing party** in any action to enforce a lien (or to challenge a
lien) is entitled to recover their reasonable attorney’s fees. This is a
double-edged sword. If you prevail in foreclosing your lien (even via a
settlement where the owner pays you, typically you can negotiate your
accrued attorney fees into the deal), the owner will usually be on the
hook for your
fees[hammerngavel.com](https://hammerngavel.com/blog/recent-court-order-discharges-25358400-lien-that-was-prepared-by-third-party-rather-than-employee-or-attorney#:~:text=Finally%2C%20and%20perhaps%20less%20obviously%2C,point%20of%20being%20made%20whole).
But if the owner succeeds in getting your lien discharged or reduced to
$0 (for instance, you lose at trial or the lien is declared fraudulent
or void), then *you* will be ordered to pay the owner’s attorney’s
fees[hammerngavel.com](https://hammerngavel.com/blog/recent-court-order-discharges-25358400-lien-that-was-prepared-by-third-party-rather-than-employee-or-attorney#:~:text=Finally%2C%20and%20perhaps%20less%20obviously%2C,point%20of%20being%20made%20whole)[hammerngavel.com](https://hammerngavel.com/blog/recent-court-order-discharges-25358400-lien-that-was-prepared-by-third-party-rather-than-employee-or-attorney#:~:text=quarter%20million%20dollar%20lien,point%20of%20being%20made%20whole).
The fees and costs in protracted lien litigation can be significant –
often tens of thousands of dollars – and can sometimes even exceed the
lien amount in dispute. For example, in *Lamm & Newline*, by discharging
the lien, the owner not only wiped out the $253k lien but also
positioned itself to recover fees from the contractor, putting the
contractor in a deep financial
hole[hammerngavel.com](https://hammerngavel.com/blog/recent-court-order-discharges-25358400-lien-that-was-prepared-by-third-party-rather-than-employee-or-attorney#:~:text=addressed%20fully).

The prospect of fee-shifting means that **compliance and caution are
financially crucial**. A small mistake that voids your lien could result
not just in lost lien rights but owing the other side’s legal bill. By
the same token, when you know your lien is valid and you’ve complied
with all requirements, the fee provision can pressure the owner to
settle (because if they fight and lose, they pay your fees). Always
evaluate the strength of your lien objectively. If there’s a legitimate
question about your lien’s validity (maybe a borderline deadline issue
or a questionable notice), weigh the risk of proceeding and potentially
owing fees. It may sometimes be better to negotiate a compromise than to
litigate a shaky lien to the bitter end.

**Practical Guidance:** Mark all relevant dates (45 days for NTO, 90
days to record lien, 1 year to file suit, etc.) in a project timeline.
If an owner triggers a contest or show-cause, **accelerate your action**
accordingly. Once in court, prosecute your foreclosure diligently – a
lis pendens should be filed to preserve
priority[floridabar.org](https://www.floridabar.org/the-florida-bar-journal/floridas-unwieldy-but-effective-construction-lien-law/#:~:text=lien%20should%20not%20be%20enforced,105),
and you must generally bring it to judgment within a reasonable time (if
you stall excessively, an owner could move to dismiss for lack of
prosecution).

Because of the fee exposure, it’s wise to **seek a negotiated
resolution** if possible. Mediation is common in lien cases. But never
let settlement talks make you miss a statutory cut-off – you can file
suit to preserve the lien and still continue negotiating (filing doesn’t
preclude amicable resolution; it simply protects your rights).

Lastly, remember that an owner can discharge your lien by **transferring
it to a bond or cash deposit** (§713.24). In that event, your lien
attaches to the security, not the real estate. You would then foreclose
against the bond (the surety or cash fund) rather than the property, but
the same deadlines and principles
apply[hammerngavel.com](https://hammerngavel.com/blog/recent-court-order-discharges-25358400-lien-that-was-prepared-by-third-party-rather-than-employee-or-attorney#:~:text=In%20Company%20Partners%20LLC%20dba,21%2C%20Florida%20Statutes).
If the lien is transferred to bond, be sure to include the surety and
the principal on the bond in your lawsuit. The advantage for owners in
doing this is it frees the property from the lien, but for you, it just
means the source of recovery changes. Your compliance obligations
(timing, accuracy, etc.) remain just as strict when proceeding on a
transferred lien.

## Conclusion and Lienor Best Practices

Florida’s construction lien cases reveal a consistent theme: **courts
rigorously enforce the letter of the law** to balance the equitable
rights between owners and those improving the property. The lien law is
powerful – it can secure payment by encumbering the owner’s land – but
with that power comes exacting statutory prerequisites. From the
foregoing survey, the **overall judicial guidance** can be summarized as
follows:

  - **Deadlines are strict.** Missing the 45-day Notice to Owner or the
    90-day recording period or the 1-year enforcement period is usually
    fatal to the
    lien[floridabar.org](https://www.floridabar.org/the-florida-bar-journal/floridas-unwieldy-but-effective-construction-lien-law/#:~:text=specialize%20in%20preparing%20and%20serving,or%20services%20to%20the%20project)[casemine.com](https://www.casemine.com/judgement/us/5914692eadd7b049342d2e6d#:~:text=Second%2C%20the%20lien%20was%20timely,the%20project%20until%20this%20electrical).
    Courts do not excuse late action absent a specific statutory basis.

  - **“Final furnishing” is interpreted realistically.** It’s the last
    substantial item of work contributing to the project’s completion –
    not trivial aftermath
    tasks[floridabar.org](https://www.floridabar.org/the-florida-bar-journal/floridas-unwieldy-but-effective-construction-lien-law/#:~:text=improvements%20thereto%2C%20the%20final%20furnishing,80%C2%A0Determining%20exactly%20when).
    While some courts have stretched the concept when equity demanded
    (e.g. *Michnal*), counting on that is risky.

  - **Content errors can be forgiven if harmless.** Florida’s prejudice
    rule (§713.08(4)(a)) saves liens from technical mistakes that don’t
    mislead[case-law.vlex.com](https://case-law.vlex.com/vid/george-j-motz-const-887816411#:~:text=Section%20%284%29%28a%29%20of%20F,by%20such%20omission%20or%20error)[descrybe.ai](https://descrybe.ai/case-details/c1930203#:~:text=Application%3A%20The%20court%20concluded%20that,necessitating%20further%20examination%20at%20trial).
    But the burden is on the lienor to show the error was minor; blatant
    mistakes can still void a lien. It’s better to have no errors than
    to rely on judicial mercy.

  - **Notices and owner requests must be heeded.** The *Home Electric*
    and *Stresscon* line of cases make clear that when the statute says
    “do X in 30 days or lose the lien,” it means
    it[law.justia.com](https://law.justia.com/cases/florida/supreme-court/1991/76234-0.html#:~:text=Electric%2C%20547%20So,its%20lien%20must%20be%20denied)[law.justia.com](https://law.justia.com/cases/florida/supreme-court/1991/76234-0.html#:~:text=As%20this%20Court%20has%20stated,denial%20of%20the%20mechanic%27s%20lien).
    Lienors must respond to owner’s information demands exactly as
    required, and timely service of NTO is indispensable for subs.

  - **Fraudulent liens will backfire.** Courts distinguish between an
    *honest dispute* and a *willful inflation*. They will void liens
    (and even refer for prosecution) if a lienor files a knowingly
    exaggerated
    claim[caselaw.findlaw.com](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/fl-district-court-of-appeal/1380588.html#:~:text=The%20owners%27%20reliance%20on%20Martin,at%20122)[floridabar.org](https://www.floridabar.org/the-florida-bar-journal/floridas-unwieldy-but-effective-construction-lien-law/#:~:text=has%20pointed%20out%20that%20such,97).
    Good faith and accuracy protect your lien; sharp practice destroys
    it.

  - **Prevailing party fees fuel the incentive to comply.** Because an
    owner can recover fees for defeating a non-compliant or fraudulent
    lien[hammerngavel.com](https://hammerngavel.com/blog/recent-court-order-discharges-25358400-lien-that-was-prepared-by-third-party-rather-than-employee-or-attorney#:~:text=Finally%2C%20and%20perhaps%20less%20obviously%2C,point%20of%20being%20made%20whole),
    and a lienor can recover fees if the lien is upheld, both sides have
    skin in the game. This tilts in favor of those who *follow the
    statute closely*, as they are more likely to prevail.

For **contemporary construction practitioners (contractors, subs,
suppliers)**, the following actionable recommendations will help ensure
lien compliance:

  - **Know the Lien Law Basics:** Educate your project management and
    credit teams on Chapter 713’s requirements. Simple checklists (serve
    NTO by X date, record lien by Y date, etc.) can prevent fatal
    oversights.

  - **Use Notices of Commencement:** At project start, obtain the
    recorded Notice of Commencement (NOC) from the owner if available.
    It contains owner and lender info and project legal description –
    critical for your NTO and lien. Also note the NOC expiration (one
    year from recording, typically) because if it expires, your lien
    priority might be based on actual recording
    date[floridabar.org](https://www.floridabar.org/the-florida-bar-journal/floridas-unwieldy-but-effective-construction-lien-law/#:~:text=A%20copy%20must%20be%20served,effect%20once%20an%20action%20to).

  - **Serve the Notice to Owner (if required) promptly:** Don’t wait –
    send it at the beginning of your work or delivery. Keep proof of
    delivery (green card, etc.). Sub-subcontractors: ensure you also
    send any required notice to the contractor. Without an NTO, you
    generally cannot
    lien[law.justia.com](https://law.justia.com/cases/florida/supreme-court/1995/84075-0.html#:~:text=DOES%20A%20SUBCONTRACTOR%20BEGIN%20TO,INSTALLATION%20ON%20THE%20JOB%20SITE)[law.justia.com](https://law.justia.com/cases/florida/supreme-court/1978/50238-0.html#:~:text=to%20furnish%20timely%20notice%20to,We%20reverse).

  - **Diary the 90-Day Lien Window:** Maintain job logs to pinpoint your
    last furnishing date. If work stretches, update that date. But never
    assume you can extend it by doing trivial tasks – once substantial
    work ceases, the clock likely
    runs[floridabar.org](https://www.floridabar.org/the-florida-bar-journal/floridas-unwieldy-but-effective-construction-lien-law/#:~:text=an%20NTO%2C76%C2%A0and%20final%20furnishing%20no,mine%20from%20which%20its%20raw).
    Plan to record the lien well before day 90 to avoid any ambiguity or
    miscounting.

  - **Prepare Accurate Lien Documents:** Use the statutory form and fill
    in all blanks
    correctly[cobbgonzalez.com](https://www.cobbgonzalez.com/floridas-construction-liens-laws-the-claim-of-lien/#:~:text=ethod%20of%20service%20of%20the,on%20the%20contractor%20or%20subcontractor).
    Double-check names (spell them exactly as in official records),
    addresses, and legal descriptions. List the first and last work
    dates accurately (these often get scrutinized). Compute the amount
    due from contract accounting records – ensure it excludes paid
    amounts and unperformed work. If in doubt whether an item is
    lienable, consult counsel or err on excluding it to avoid later
    problems.

  - **Execute the Lien Properly:** Have an authorized representative
    sign the lien and **swear to it**. Preferably sign in front of a
    notary. Alternatively, include the precise “penalty of perjury”
    statement as allowed by Fla. Stat.
    92.525[casemine.com](https://www.casemine.com/judgement/us/5914692eadd7b049342d2e6d#:~:text=Fourth%2C%20the%20statement%20provided%20by,that%20the%20facts%20stated%20in).
    Do *not* use a proxy signer with no knowledge. Also, the lien should
    show the name and address of the person who prepared it (which can
    be the lienor or an attorney – avoid listing a non-lawyer service as
    “prepared by” as that invites scrutiny).

  - **Serve the Lien on the Owner:** Within 15 days of recording, send a
    copy of the claim of lien to the owner (and copy to the lender, if
    any, to put them on notice
    too)[floridabar.org](https://www.floridabar.org/the-florida-bar-journal/floridas-unwieldy-but-effective-construction-lien-law/#:~:text=%E2%80%A2%20Perfecting%20a%20Claim%20of,to%20show%20cause%20why%20the).
    Use certified mail and save the receipt. While not voiding the lien,
    this is required by statute and is just good practice to maintain
    transparency.

  - **Anticipate the Owner’s Next Move:** Often, after you lien, an
    owner may respond by either settling, contesting the lien’s
    validity, or transferring it to a bond. Be prepared for any. If you
    receive a **Request for Sworn Statement of Account**, mark the due
    date (30 days) and respond under oath in
    time[casemine.com](https://www.casemine.com/judgement/us/5914692eadd7b049342d2e6d#:~:text=demand%20by%20an%20owner%20which,in%20substantially%20the%20following%20form)[casemine.com](https://www.casemine.com/judgement/us/5914692eadd7b049342d2e6d#:~:text=%C2%A7%20713,of%20his%20or%20her%20lien).
    If the owner files a **Notice of Contest**, mark that new 60-day
    deadline and file suit in
    time[floridabar.org](https://www.floridabar.org/the-florida-bar-journal/floridas-unwieldy-but-effective-construction-lien-law/#:~:text=the%20time%20of%20recording,105).
    If the owner files a **Show Cause** motion/order, get your
    foreclosure suit on file *before* the hearing date (within the 20
    days
    given)[floridabar.org](https://www.floridabar.org/the-florida-bar-journal/floridas-unwieldy-but-effective-construction-lien-law/#:~:text=the%20time%20of%20recording,to%2060%20days%20by%20recording).
    In short, remain vigilant and ready to act quickly post-lien.

  - **Consider Less Drastic Security**: In some cases, especially for
    subcontractors, a **Lien Release Bond** (where you as lienor bond
    your own lien) might be an option if there’s a dispute about
    compliance. This is rare and usually not ideal, but it’s a way to
    keep a lien in play if, say, you missed an NTO – you could
    potentially bond around privity issues. However, this is complex and
    requires legal advice; prevention of mistakes is far better than
    trying to salvage them later.

  - **Pursue Foreclosure (or Suit on Bond) Timely:** Do not let the
    one-year period
    lapse[floridabar.org](https://www.floridabar.org/the-florida-bar-journal/floridas-unwieldy-but-effective-construction-lien-law/#:~:text=the%20NOC,105).
    Many practitioners aim to file the foreclosure within, say, 11
    months of lien recording to avoid any argument about time. Also, if
    the property is changing hands or a refinance is looming, earlier
    foreclosure (with lis pendens) might be necessary to protect your
    priority.

  - **Be Ethical and Reasonable:** This cannot be overstated – **do not
    inflate your lien**. Claim only what you honestly believe is due and
    can substantiate. Florida law doesn’t punish a lienor for a
    **“good-faith dispute as to the amount
    due”**[floridabar.org](https://www.floridabar.org/the-florida-bar-journal/floridas-unwieldy-but-effective-construction-lien-law/#:~:text=A%20lien%20is%20deemed%20fraudulent,3d%20DCA%202008%29%2C%20reduced),
    but it comes down to evidence. If you’re ever internally unsure
    “should I really lien for this much?”, that doubt is a sign to
    double-check your numbers or consult a construction attorney. It’s
    better to lien for a bit less (and maintain credibility) than to
    overreach and risk the entire lien. You can always amend a lien
    downward or accept a bit less in settlement, but if your lien is
    voided as fraudulent, you lose all leverage.

  - **Leverage Attorney Guidance:** Florida’s lien law has many nuances
    (e.g., special rules for liening landlord-tenant improvements, or
    for design professionals, etc., which are beyond our scope). When in
    doubt, involve a Florida construction lawyer early. The cost of a
    brief consultation or document review is minor compared to the value
    of preserving a six- or seven-figure lien claim (or avoiding a
    fraudulent lien trap). An attorney can also assist in foreclosing
    the lien and navigating any owner challenges.

By following these guidelines, lienors across all roles – general
contractors in direct contract with owners, subcontractors, sub-subs,
and material suppliers – can **maximize their chances of filing valid,
enforceable liens** and securing payment for their work. Florida’s
current mechanic’s lien jurisprudence reflects a balancing act: it
protects diligent lienors’ rights (no owner windfall from technical
slip-ups that caused no
harm)[case-law.vlex.com](https://case-law.vlex.com/vid/george-j-motz-const-887816411#:~:text=Section%20%284%29%28a%29%20of%20F,by%20such%20omission%20or%20error)[descrybe.ai](https://descrybe.ai/case-details/c1930203#:~:text=Application%3A%20The%20court%20concluded%20that,necessitating%20further%20examination%20at%20trial),
but it also swiftly penalizes non-compliance or abuse of the lien
process (late filings, unsworn claims, or willful
exaggerations)[law.justia.com](https://law.justia.com/cases/florida/supreme-court/1991/76234-0.html#:~:text=Electric%2C%20547%20So,its%20lien%20must%20be%20denied)[floridabar.org](https://www.floridabar.org/the-florida-bar-journal/floridas-unwieldy-but-effective-construction-lien-law/#:~:text=Ten%20years%20earlier%2C%20the%20same,admitted%20that%20he%20hadn%E2%80%99t%20really).
The overarching trend is **“pay attention to the statute and you’ll be
protected; cut corners and you do so at your peril.”** Keeping that in
mind, construction practitioners should treat the mechanics’ lien
process as a formal, detail-oriented task – as important as the
construction work itself – to ensure they *get paid in full* for
improving Florida properties, with their lien rights intact and upheld.
