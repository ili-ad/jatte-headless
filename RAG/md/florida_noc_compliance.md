# Florida Notice of Commencement Compliance – Case Law Analysis

## Introduction and Legal Background

Under Florida’s Construction Lien Law, a **Notice of Commencement
(NOC)** is a statutory document that a property owner (or owner’s agent)
must record in the county public records **before starting
construction** on improvements exceeding a small cost threshold (now
**\>$5,000** for most
projects)[floridabar.org](https://www.floridabar.org/the-florida-bar-journal/statutes-affecting-florida-construction/#:~:text=,not%20apply%20to%20maintenance%20work).
The NOC contains key information (property description, owner’s name,
contractor, lender, surety, etc.) and must be **signed by the owner**
(no one else may sign in their
stead)[caselaw.findlaw.com](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/court/fl-district-court-of-appeal/2071581.html#:~:text=The%20statute%20allows%20the%20owner,5th%20DCA%201988).
A copy must be posted at the job site prior to the first
inspection[taylordaylaw.com](https://www.taylordaylaw.com/2024/10/notices-of-commencement-for-florida-construction-jobs/#:~:text=Except%20for%20certain%20exempt%20improvements%2C,to%20provide%20a%20lienor%20with).
Certain minor work (small-value or specified repairs) and public
projects are exempt from this
requirement[flsenate.gov](https://www.flsenate.gov/Session/Bill/2023/797/Analyses/h0797a.CJS.PDF#:~:text=%E2%80%9CClerk%E2%80%99s%20office%E2%80%9D%20means%20the%20office,135%281%29%28d)[floridabar.org](https://www.floridabar.org/the-florida-bar-journal/statutes-affecting-florida-construction/#:~:text=,not%20apply%20to%20maintenance%20work).

The NOC serves two main purposes in Florida’s lien framework. **First**,
it protects owners by establishing a clear **“commencement” date** for
the project – effectively a trigger for lien priority and
limitations[caselaw.findlaw.com](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/court/fl-district-court-of-appeal/2071581.html#:~:text=Integral%20to%20our%20analysis%20here,The).
By statute, properly perfected construction liens can **relate back** in
priority to the date the NOC was
recorded[taylordaylaw.com](https://www.taylordaylaw.com/2024/10/notices-of-commencement-for-florida-construction-jobs/#:~:text=Nevertheless%2C%20the%20standard%20here%20is,notice%20of%20commencement%20been%20filed).
(If no NOC is recorded, a lien’s priority *attaches only on the date of
its recording*, meaning intervening liens or mortgages may take
priority[taylordaylaw.com](https://www.taylordaylaw.com/2024/10/notices-of-commencement-for-florida-construction-jobs/#:~:text=Nevertheless%2C%20the%20standard%20here%20is,notice%20of%20commencement%20been%20filed).)
**Second**, the NOC provides potential lienors (e.g. subcontractors,
suppliers) with all information needed to serve their required **Notice
to Owner (NTO)**, which is a prerequisite to enforcing a
lien[caselaw.findlaw.com](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/court/fl-district-court-of-appeal/2071581.html#:~:text=owner%20by%20providing%20a%20trigger,is%20to%20receive%20copies%20of).
In essence, the NOC is a **“roadmap”** for lienors, listing the
project’s critical details (owner, general contractor, lender, etc.)
and an address for service of
notices[caselaw.findlaw.com](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/court/fl-district-court-of-appeal/2071581.html#:~:text=owner%20by%20providing%20a%20trigger,is%20to%20receive%20copies%20of).

Florida courts historically have required **strict compliance** with
lien law statutes, given that construction liens are creatures of
statute[caselaw.findlaw.com](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/court/fl-district-court-of-appeal/2071581.html#:~:text=laws%20were%20enacted%20for%20the,substantially%20complied%20with%20the%20statutory).
In the words of the Florida Supreme Court, the lien laws exist “to
protect those whose materials, labor and skills improve the land of
others” but courts will not be sympathetic to a party seeking relief
unless they **“strictly comply”** with the statutory
requirements[caselaw.findlaw.com](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/court/fl-district-court-of-appeal/2071581.html#:~:text=laws%20were%20enacted%20for%20the,substantially%20complied%20with%20the%20statutory).
However, case law demonstrates that **substantial compliance** can
suffice, particularly where an owner’s error in NOC compliance would
otherwise unfairly prejudice lienors who have done their
part[taylordaylaw.com](https://www.taylordaylaw.com/2024/10/notices-of-commencement-for-florida-construction-jobs/#:~:text=,in%20the%20Notice%20of%20Commencement)[caselaw.findlaw.com](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/court/fl-district-court-of-appeal/2071581.html#:~:text=regular%20and%20complete%20in%20all,at%20470).
The burden of filling out and recording the NOC properly falls squarely
on the owner (or an owner-authorized
agent)[caselaw.findlaw.com](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/court/fl-district-court-of-appeal/2071581.html#:~:text=stead,only%20confirmed%20the%20contrary%E2%80%94the%20lienor).
Courts have consistently held that a lienor is **not responsible for
policing errors or omissions** in an owner’s
NOC[caselaw.findlaw.com](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/court/fl-district-court-of-appeal/2071581.html#:~:text=stead,to%20shift%20this%20risk%20to)[caselaw.findlaw.com](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/court/fl-district-court-of-appeal/2071581.html#:~:text=is%20on%20the%20owner%2C%20whereas,at%20470).
In disputes over NOC defects, Florida courts balance the statutory
technicalities against the lien law’s protective purpose, often
relieving diligent lienors of harsh consequences where the *owner’s*
noncompliance with §713.13 was central.

Below is a survey of Florida cases – from trial level up through
District Courts of Appeal (and notable Supreme Court guidance) – where
compliance with the NOC requirement was a decisive issue. For each case,
the parties, NOC-related issues, outcomes, and legal reasoning are
summarized. The cases cover both **residential** and **commercial**
projects, illustrating common mistakes and their impact on lien rights
and contracts. A summary table of the key cases is also provided for
quick reference.

## Key Case Law on NOC Compliance in Florida

### Execution and Accuracy of the NOC (Owner Signature & Information)

**Edwin Taylor Corp. v. Mortgage Electronic Registration Sys., Inc.**,
311 So.3d 139 (Fla. 2d DCA 2020) – *Commercial project (priority dispute
between subcontractor and lender).*

  - **Parties:** A subcontractor (Edwin Taylor Corp.) vs. a lender
    (BB\&T, whose nominee was MERS) and property owner. The owner had
    hired a general contractor (GC) for
    construction[caselaw.findlaw.com](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/court/fl-district-court-of-appeal/2071581.html#:~:text=On%20January%207%2C%202014%2C%20the,BB%26T%27s%20notice%20of)[caselaw.findlaw.com](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/court/fl-district-court-of-appeal/2071581.html#:~:text=Edwin%20Taylor%2C%20who%20subcontracted%20with,all%20other%20claims%E2%80%94including%20BB%26T%27s%20claim).

  - **NOC Issue:** The GC recorded a NOC at project start which was
    **complete and accurate** *except* that it was **signed by the GC
    (not the
    owner)**[caselaw.findlaw.com](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/court/fl-district-court-of-appeal/2071581.html#:~:text=On%20January%207%2C%202014%2C%20the,property%20and%20recorded%20its%20own)[caselaw.findlaw.com](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/court/fl-district-court-of-appeal/2071581.html#:~:text=BB%26T%20moved%20for%20summary%20judgment,of%20the%20summary%20judgment%20motion).
    The owner knew of and did not object to this NOC, nor did the owner
    file any “Notice of Termination” to void
    it[caselaw.findlaw.com](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/court/fl-district-court-of-appeal/2071581.html#:~:text=notice%20of%20commencement%20was%20complete,1).
    The next day, the owner obtained a construction loan from BB\&T,
    which recorded its **mortgage** and a second NOC (signed by the
    owner) listing BB\&T as
    lender[caselaw.findlaw.com](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/court/fl-district-court-of-appeal/2071581.html#:~:text=aware%20of%20the%20general%20contractor%27s,1).

  - **Central Issue:** Whether the first NOC’s lack of the owner’s
    signature made it invalid under §713.13(1)(g), thereby preventing
    the subcontractor’s lien from relating back to that NOC’s recording
    date[caselaw.findlaw.com](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/court/fl-district-court-of-appeal/2071581.html#:~:text=BB%26T%20moved%20for%20summary%20judgment,records%20of%20Hillsborough%20County%2C%20Florida)[caselaw.findlaw.com](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/court/fl-district-court-of-appeal/2071581.html#:~:text=The%20trial%20court%20found%20merit,This%20was%20error).
    If the NOC was invalid, the subcontractor’s lien (recorded later)
    would attach after the mortgage and be junior; if the NOC was
    effective, the lien would relate back and take priority over the
    intervening
    mortgage[caselaw.findlaw.com](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/court/fl-district-court-of-appeal/2071581.html#:~:text=BB%26T%20moved%20for%20summary%20judgment,records%20of%20Hillsborough%20County%2C%20Florida)[caselaw.findlaw.com](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/court/fl-district-court-of-appeal/2071581.html#:~:text=The%20trial%20court%20found%20merit,This%20was%20error).

  - **Outcome:** The Second DCA **reversed** summary judgment for the
    lender and held that the NOC, though signed by the GC, was **not a
    nullity per se** in this
    context[caselaw.findlaw.com](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/court/fl-district-court-of-appeal/2071581.html#:~:text=Edwin%20Taylor%2C%20on%20the%20other,signed%20by%20all%20owners%20of)[caselaw.findlaw.com](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/court/fl-district-court-of-appeal/2071581.html#:~:text=Accordingly%2C%20we%20hold%20that%20a,lender%20on%20this%20basis%20was).
    A subcontractor who **strictly complied** with all lien
    prerequisites (serving notice to owner, timely recording the lien,
    etc.) could rely on a defective NOC that otherwise met all statutory
    requirements[caselaw.findlaw.com](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/court/fl-district-court-of-appeal/2071581.html#:~:text=We%20find%20Sasso%20instructive,an%20updated%20title%20search%20through)[caselaw.findlaw.com](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/court/fl-district-court-of-appeal/2071581.html#:~:text=Accordingly%2C%20we%20hold%20that%20a,lender%20on%20this%20basis%20was).
    The court ruled the subcontractor’s lien related back to the first
    NOC’s recording date despite the missing owner’s
    signature[caselaw.findlaw.com](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/court/fl-district-court-of-appeal/2071581.html#:~:text=We%20find%20Sasso%20instructive,an%20updated%20title%20search%20through)[caselaw.findlaw.com](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/court/fl-district-court-of-appeal/2071581.html#:~:text=Accordingly%2C%20we%20hold%20that%20a,lender%20on%20this%20basis%20was).

  - **Rationale:** The court emphasized that §713.13(1)(g) places the
    duty on the owner to sign the NOC, and the **risk of any error in a
    NOC falls on the owner (or its agent)** – not on
    lienors[caselaw.findlaw.com](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/court/fl-district-court-of-appeal/2071581.html#:~:text=stead,to%20shift%20this%20risk%20to).
    Here, the NOC substantially fulfilled its purpose: it was recorded
    before work began and contained all required information (owner
    identity, description, contractor,
    etc.)[caselaw.findlaw.com](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/court/fl-district-court-of-appeal/2071581.html#:~:text=We%20find%20Sasso%20instructive,an%20updated%20title%20search%20through).
    The subcontractor had no way to compel the owner’s signature and was
    entitled to assume the recorded NOC was
    valid[caselaw.findlaw.com](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/court/fl-district-court-of-appeal/2071581.html#:~:text=is%20on%20the%20owner%2C%20whereas,at%20470).
    Citing a similar 4th DCA case, the court noted it would be *“a
    strange construction”* of the law to make a lienor lose its
    protection “because an owner, over whom the lienor had no control,
    failed to complete some detail in recording the
    notice”[caselaw.findlaw.com](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/court/fl-district-court-of-appeal/2071581.html#:~:text=regular%20and%20complete%20in%20all,at%20470).
    Furthermore, the lender was in a better position to protect itself
    (it could have done an updated title search or required the owner to
    terminate the earlier NOC before
    closing)[caselaw.findlaw.com](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/court/fl-district-court-of-appeal/2071581.html#:~:text=In%20reaching%20its%20conclusion%2C%20the,the%20notice%20of%20commencement%20where)[caselaw.findlaw.com](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/court/fl-district-court-of-appeal/2071581.html#:~:text=the%20lienor%20to%20properly%20mail,4).
    Allowing the lender to exploit the owner’s technical omission would
    undermine the lien law’s
    purpose[caselaw.findlaw.com](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/court/fl-district-court-of-appeal/2071581.html#:~:text=the%20notice%20is%20true%20and,at%20470)[caselaw.findlaw.com](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/court/fl-district-court-of-appeal/2071581.html#:~:text=the%20subcontractor%20had%20the%20right,71).
    Thus, equity and the statutory scheme favored the lienor. The case
    set a precedent that **an owner-authorized agent’s signature can
    substantially comply** with the NOC requirement such that lien
    rights are
    preserved[caselaw.findlaw.com](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/court/fl-district-court-of-appeal/2071581.html#:~:text=Accordingly%2C%20we%20hold%20that%20a,lender%20on%20this%20basis%20was).

  - **Common Mistakes:** Failing to have the **owner personally sign**
    the NOC is a mistake; however, if an owner permits someone else
    (like a contractor) to sign on their behalf, the NOC may still be
    effective **so long as no interested party is adversely
    affected**[caselaw.findlaw.com](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/court/fl-district-court-of-appeal/2071581.html#:~:text=Accordingly%2C%20we%20hold%20that%20a,lender%20on%20this%20basis%20was).
    The owner’s inaction here (not correcting or terminating the NOC)
    was critical – it implied authorization. The lender’s mistake was
    not recognizing the first NOC’s existence/priority; prudent lenders
    should check for any prior NOCs and insist on proper termination or
    releases to avoid subordinate
    liens[caselaw.findlaw.com](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/court/fl-district-court-of-appeal/2071581.html#:~:text=the%20lienor%20to%20properly%20mail,4)[caselaw.findlaw.com](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/fl-district-court-of-appeal/1586479.html#:~:text=MHB%20was%20in%20the%20best,costs%20in%20the%20underlying%20action).

  - **Impact:** This decision preserves lien priority for subcontractors
    even when the NOC is technically imperfect, preventing owners or
    third parties from using minor NOC defects as a **“sword” against
    lienors who did everything else
    right[caselaw.findlaw.com](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/court/fl-district-court-of-appeal/2071581.html#:~:text=nullity%2C%20per%20se%2C%20in%20a,and%20remand%20for%20further%20proceedings)**.
    It clarifies that the **lien’s relation-back under §713.07(2) will
    apply** if the NOC substantially complies, thus upholding contract
    rights and payment expectations of subs and suppliers. Owners and
    lenders, on the other hand, are reminded that noncompliance with NOC
    formalities can jeopardize their interests (e.g. a lender’s mortgage
    priority)[caselaw.findlaw.com](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/court/fl-district-court-of-appeal/2071581.html#:~:text=In%20reaching%20its%20conclusion%2C%20the,the%20notice%20of%20commencement%20where)[caselaw.findlaw.com](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/court/fl-district-court-of-appeal/2071581.html#:~:text=the%20lienor%20to%20properly%20mail,4).

**Sasso Air Conditioning, Inc. v. United Cos. Lending Corp.**, 742 So.2d
468 (Fla. 4th DCA 1999) – *Residential construction (subcontractor vs.
bank).*

  - **Parties:** A subcontractor (Sasso Air Conditioning) placed a lien
    for unpaid work, and a bank (mortgagee) contested that lien’s
    priority. The property was co-owned (a husband and wife), and the
    bank held a mortgage recorded after construction began.

  - **NOC Issue:** The NOC for the project was recorded by the general
    contractor **before the bank’s mortgage**, but it had technical
    defects – it was **not signed by all owners** (only one spouse
    signed) and the subcontractor’s name was not listed on the
    NOC[caselaw.findlaw.com](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/court/fl-district-court-of-appeal/2071581.html#:~:text=sought%20to%20foreclose%20its%20mortgage,%E2%80%9Cno%20practical%20way%20to%20protect)
    (note: subcontractors are generally not listed on an NOC form, so
    the bank may have been grasping at any irregularity). The bank
    argued these defects made the NOC invalid, so the subcontractor’s
    lien should not relate back to the NOC
    date[caselaw.findlaw.com](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/court/fl-district-court-of-appeal/2071581.html#:~:text=sought%20to%20foreclose%20its%20mortgage,%E2%80%9Cno%20practical%20way%20to%20protect).

  - **Outcome:** The Fourth DCA ruled **in favor of the subcontractor**,
    holding that the NOC remained effective for lien-priority purposes
    despite the missing owner
    signature(s)[caselaw.findlaw.com](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/court/fl-district-court-of-appeal/2071581.html#:~:text=general%20contractor%20that%20was%20recorded,law%20does%20not%20require%20every)[caselaw.findlaw.com](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/court/fl-district-court-of-appeal/2071581.html#:~:text=the%20notice%20is%20true%20and,at%20470).
    The subcontractor’s lien was allowed to relate back ahead of the
    bank’s mortgage. Summary judgment for the bank was reversed and
    directed in favor of the
    subcontractor[caselaw.findlaw.com](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/court/fl-district-court-of-appeal/2071581.html#:~:text=In%20reaching%20its%20conclusion%2C%20the,the%20notice%20of%20commencement%20where).

  - **Rationale:** The court reasoned that the duty to ensure a proper
    NOC (including obtaining all owners’ signatures) lies with the
    owner, not the
    lienor[caselaw.findlaw.com](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/court/fl-district-court-of-appeal/2071581.html#:~:text=to%20the%20void%20notice%20of,had%20no%20control%2C%20failed%20to).
    A subcontractor has **“no practical way to protect itself”** or
    verify ownership details – *“the law does not require every
    contractor to conduct a title search to verify that the information
    contained in the notice is true and
    correct”*[caselaw.findlaw.com](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/court/fl-district-court-of-appeal/2071581.html#:~:text=to%20the%20void%20notice%20of,had%20no%20control%2C%20failed%20to).
    As long as the NOC appears regular and the lienor relied on it in
    good faith, the lienor should not be punished for an owner’s
    omission[caselaw.findlaw.com](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/court/fl-district-court-of-appeal/2071581.html#:~:text=is%20on%20the%20owner%2C%20whereas,at%20470).
    Importantly, the court noted it would be inequitable to “permit the
    lienor to lose its protection and the value of its labor” due to an
    owner’s failure to complete some detail on the
    NOC[caselaw.findlaw.com](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/court/fl-district-court-of-appeal/2071581.html#:~:text=regular%20and%20complete%20in%20all,at%20470).
    The 4th DCA also pointed out that the **bank was in the best
    position to protect itself** – a proper title search would have
    revealed the recorded NOC before the bank made the
    loan[caselaw.findlaw.com](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/court/fl-district-court-of-appeal/2071581.html#:~:text=In%20reaching%20its%20conclusion%2C%20the,the%20notice%20of%20commencement%20where).
    The bank should not “benefit from a defect” that it easily could
    have discovered, especially when it was **“the party who was most
    able to protect itself from
    loss”**[caselaw.findlaw.com](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/court/fl-district-court-of-appeal/2071581.html#:~:text=In%20reaching%20its%20conclusion%2C%20the,the%20notice%20of%20commencement%20where).
    In sum, the NOC was in **substantial compliance**, and the
    subcontractor met all other lien requirements, so the lien’s
    priority was
    preserved[caselaw.findlaw.com](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/court/fl-district-court-of-appeal/2071581.html#:~:text=Accordingly%2C%20the%20court%20reversed%20the,71).

  - **Common Mistakes:** Not having **all co-owners sign** the NOC is a
    frequent mistake in residential projects (e.g., one spouse signs for
    both). *Sasso* confirms that such an oversight will not invalidate
    lien rights of those who rely on the NOC – but it could have been
    avoided by ensuring every titled owner executed the document.
    Another mistake highlighted is the **bank’s failure to catch title
    discrepancies** – lenders should always verify that an NOC is
    properly executed by the true owner(s) before funding, as they may
    otherwise lose
    priority[caselaw.findlaw.com](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/court/fl-district-court-of-appeal/2071581.html#:~:text=In%20reaching%20its%20conclusion%2C%20the,the%20notice%20of%20commencement%20where).

  - **Impact on Liens:** This case established that minor NOC execution
    errors (like a missing signature) do **not automatically void an
    NOC** as against lienors. It set a precedent (later followed by
    *Edwin Taylor Corp.* above) that **substantial compliance is
    sufficient** for
    NOCs[taylordaylaw.com](https://www.taylordaylaw.com/2024/10/notices-of-commencement-for-florida-construction-jobs/#:~:text=,in%20the%20Notice%20of%20Commencement)[caselaw.findlaw.com](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/court/fl-district-court-of-appeal/2071581.html#:~:text=the%20notice%20is%20true%20and,at%20470).
    Liens can still be enforced and given full priority if the NOC’s
    purpose – providing notice and project information – was met. Owners
    cannot use their own slip-ups to dodge liability, and banks cannot
    seize on technicalities to leapfrog a lienor who improved the
    property[caselaw.findlaw.com](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/court/fl-district-court-of-appeal/2071581.html#:~:text=regular%20and%20complete%20in%20all,at%20470).

**Design Aluminum, Inc. v. DeSanti**, 521 So.2d 285 (Fla. 2d DCA 1988) –
*Residential renovation (material supplier vs. owner).*

  - **Parties:** A material supplier (Design Aluminum) sought to
    foreclose a lien for materials supplied to a home improvement
    project; the homeowner (DeSanti) defended against the lien.

  - **NOC Issue:** The case centered on an **inaccuracy in the NOC’s
    information**. The owner had recorded an NOC, but some detail
    (likely the owner’s name or address) was erroneous. The supplier’s
    required Notice to Owner was sent based on the faulty NOC
    information, raising the question of who bears responsibility for
    the error.

  - **Outcome & Precedent:** The Second DCA held that the **owner bears
    the burden of ensuring the NOC is accurate**, and the **“risk of any
    errors in the Notice of Commencement \[falls\] squarely on the
    shoulders of the
    owners.”**[caselaw.findlaw.com](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/court/fl-district-court-of-appeal/2071581.html#:~:text=stead,to%20shift%20this%20risk%20to).
    It explicitly found no legal basis to shift that burden to
    lienors[caselaw.findlaw.com](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/court/fl-district-court-of-appeal/2071581.html#:~:text=,the%20lack%20of%20authority%20for).
    Accordingly, a lienor’s rights will not be defeated due to
    misinformation in an NOC that the lienor relied upon. The lien in
    *Design Aluminum* was upheld, with the court noting that the lienor
    had **no duty to independently verify** the NOC’s
    contents[caselaw.findlaw.com](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/court/fl-district-court-of-appeal/2071581.html#:~:text=,the%20lack%20of%20authority%20for).
    This decision is frequently cited to affirm that **lienors are
    entitled to rely on a recorded NOC** and that owners must shoulder
    the consequences of mistakes in that
    document[caselaw.findlaw.com](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/court/fl-district-court-of-appeal/2071581.html#:~:text=stead,to%20shift%20this%20risk%20to)[caselaw.findlaw.com](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/court/fl-district-court-of-appeal/2071581.html#:~:text=,the%20lack%20of%20authority%20for).

  - **Common Mistakes:** Providing **incorrect information** on the NOC
    (e.g., wrong legal description, owner name, or address for notices)
    is a critical error by owners. *Design Aluminum* illustrates that
    while such mistakes may not invalidate liens, they can lead to
    **disputes and delays**. Owners should double-check all NOC details
    (and amend the NOC if an error is discovered) to avoid estoppel
    arguments or confusion. Lienors, for their part, should document
    that they followed the NOC as recorded – any error-induced missteps
    (like sending notice to a wrong address given in the NOC) can then
    be excused by the
    court[caselaw.findlaw.com](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/court/fl-district-court-of-appeal/2071581.html#:~:text=stead,to%20shift%20this%20risk%20to)[caselaw.findlaw.com](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/court/fl-district-court-of-appeal/2071581.html#:~:text=3,which%20the%20owner%20took%20title).

  - **Impact:** This case solidified a pro-lienor interpretation of NOC
    duties. It serves as a warning to owners that an NOC is not a mere
    formality – inaccuracies can come back to haunt the owner, as courts
    will not allow an owner to use its own error as a shield against
    paying for improvements. Practically, *Design Aluminum* encourages
    owners (or their agents) to exercise care in preparing NOCs, and
    reassures lienors that **good-faith reliance on NOC information**
    will protect their lien
    claims[caselaw.findlaw.com](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/court/fl-district-court-of-appeal/2071581.html#:~:text=,the%20lack%20of%20authority%20for).

**Napolitano v. Security First Fed. Sav. & Loan Ass’n**, 533 So.2d 948
(Fla. 5th DCA 1988) – *Residential construction loan (owner vs.
lender).*

  - **Parties:** A property owner (Napolitano) versus a construction
    lender (Security First F.S.L.A.). The owner had a construction loan
    to build a home, and the lender was responsible for certain aspects
    of the project’s administration.

  - **NOC Issue:** The lender, acting as the owner’s **authorized
    agent**, undertook to record the NOC but allegedly did so
    **improperly or untimely**. (The branch manager admitted their
    practice was to record the NOC to protect the mortgage’s
    priority[descrybe.ai](https://descrybe.ai/case-details/c7637516#:~:text=Security%20First%27s%20branch%20manager%20stated,their%20mortgage%20interest%2C%20and).)
    A dispute arose about a **mistake in the NOC or a delay in
    recording** it, which affected lien priority or the owner’s
    exposure.

  - **Outcome:** The Fifth DCA held that when a **lender** voluntarily
    takes on the task of recording the NOC on the owner’s behalf, the
    **lender assumes the owner’s statutory responsibility**, and thus
    **any errors or delays by the lender are attributable to the
    lender** (and not to the detriment of the owner or
    lienors)[caselaw.findlaw.com](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/court/fl-district-court-of-appeal/2071581.html#:~:text=in%20the%20Notice%20of%20Commencement,only%20confirmed%20the%20contrary%E2%80%94the%20lienor).
    In fact, the court noted that Florida law is clear that a lender who
    fails to record the NOC properly or timely **“will be liable to the
    owner”** for any resulting
    losses[jimersonfirm.com](https://www.jimersonfirm.com/blog/2021/02/top-five-construction-lien-law/#:~:text=The%20Top%20Five%20Things%20Lenders,to%20the%20owner%20for).
    Napolitano reinforced that the agent (here, the lender) must
    strictly perform the owner’s duty – a NOC must be recorded **before
    construction begins** (no matter what internal loan contingencies
    remain) to preserve the intended
    priority[courtlistener.com](https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/7637516/napolitano-v-security-first-federal-savings-loan-assn/#:~:text=,the%20approval%20or%20recording).

  - **Implications:** The case essentially shifts the **risk of NOC
    noncompliance onto lenders** when they undertake that
    role[caselaw.findlaw.com](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/court/fl-district-court-of-appeal/2071581.html#:~:text=in%20the%20Notice%20of%20Commencement,only%20confirmed%20the%20contrary%E2%80%94the%20lienor).
    A common mistake highlighted is **lender delay** – if a lender waits
    to record the NOC (for example, until after a mortgage is recorded
    or other conditions are met), it may inadvertently allow
    construction to start without an effective NOC, jeopardizing the
    mortgage’s priority. *Napolitano* warns that a lender in such a
    scenario could be held financially responsible for liens that gain
    priority or for the owner paying twice.

  - **Practice Pointer:** Construction lenders in Florida typically
    ensure an NOC is recorded at loan closing (often even requiring an
    owner-signed NOC as a funding condition). This case underscores why:
    failing to do so can make the lender **directly liable** and its
    collateral security (the mortgage) vulnerable. Owners should clarify
    with their lenders who will record the NOC, and ensure it’s done
    correctly. If a lender is handling it, the owner should follow up
    promptly—any mistake is the lender’s to bear, by
    law[caselaw.findlaw.com](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/court/fl-district-court-of-appeal/2071581.html#:~:text=in%20the%20Notice%20of%20Commencement,only%20confirmed%20the%20contrary%E2%80%94the%20lienor).

### Failure to Record or Post a NOC & Effect on Lien Notices

**Roof Structures, Inc. v. Piccou**, 544 So.2d 1138 (Fla. 4th DCA 1989)
– *Residential project (subcontractor vs. homeowner).*

  - **Scenario:** A homeowner (Piccou) **failed to record any NOC**
    before a subcontractor began work on their property. Lacking an NOC
    in the public records or posted on-site, the subcontractor relied on
    **building permit records** (or other sources) to identify the owner
    for sending its Notice to Owner. Unfortunately, those sources led
    the subcontractor to address the Notice to Owner to the **wrong
    person (or wrong address)**, meaning the actual owner did not
    receive the statutory notice. The owner later argued the
    subcontractor’s lien was invalid due to this failure to serve the
    proper owner.

  - **Outcome:** The Fourth DCA ruled in favor of the subcontractor,
    finding the lien **valid despite the notice mistake**, because the
    owner’s own omission (failing to record a NOC) was the root cause of
    the
    problem[acis-api.flcourts.gov](https://acis-api.flcourts.gov/courts/8f454fb9-4c7f-43df-856b-ab373e71c27f/cms/case/86ec4c23-2d4f-483b-a90f-e8d1c0dd327b/docketentrydocuments/2eebded0-ac17-44f6-b7b0-e4970dae5111#:~:text=Appraiser%E2%80%99s%20office%2C%20identified%20himself%20as,title%20search%20to%20file%2036).
    The court held that when an owner fails to file a NOC as required,
    and that **“material omission”** causes a lienor to detrimentally
    rely on other erroneous information (e.g., county records showing a
    prior owner), the owner is **estopped from exploiting the lienor’s
    resulting
    mistake**[acis-api.flcourts.gov](https://acis-api.flcourts.gov/courts/8f454fb9-4c7f-43df-856b-ab373e71c27f/cms/case/86ec4c23-2d4f-483b-a90f-e8d1c0dd327b/docketentrydocuments/2eebded0-ac17-44f6-b7b0-e4970dae5111#:~:text=Appraiser%E2%80%99s%20office%2C%20identified%20himself%20as,title%20search%20to%20file%2036).
    In *Roof Structures*, the absence of an NOC excused the
    subcontractor’s naming of the wrong owner in its Notice to Owner,
    and the lien was
    enforceable[acis-api.flcourts.gov](https://acis-api.flcourts.gov/courts/8f454fb9-4c7f-43df-856b-ab373e71c27f/cms/case/86ec4c23-2d4f-483b-a90f-e8d1c0dd327b/docketentrydocuments/2eebded0-ac17-44f6-b7b0-e4970dae5111#:~:text=Appraiser%E2%80%99s%20office%2C%20identified%20himself%20as,title%20search%20to%20file%2036).

  - **Legal Principle:** This case established that an owner who ignores
    the NOC requirement cannot then use the lack of notice (caused by
    their omission) as a defense. It reinforces an equitable principle:
    **“Good faith substantial compliance”** by a lienor will be
    sufficient when the owner’s failure in their NOC duty misleads the
    lienor[acis-api.flcourts.gov](https://acis-api.flcourts.gov/courts/8f454fb9-4c7f-43df-856b-ab373e71c27f/cms/case/86ec4c23-2d4f-483b-a90f-e8d1c0dd327b/docketentrydocuments/2eebded0-ac17-44f6-b7b0-e4970dae5111#:~:text=Appraiser%E2%80%99s%20office%2C%20identified%20himself%20as,title%20search%20to%20file%2036)[caselaw.findlaw.com](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/court/fl-district-court-of-appeal/2071581.html#:~:text=3,which%20the%20owner%20took%20title).
    The court recognized that lienors should not be penalized for
    information gaps that an NOC would have
    filled[acis-api.flcourts.gov](https://acis-api.flcourts.gov/courts/8f454fb9-4c7f-43df-856b-ab373e71c27f/cms/case/86ec4c23-2d4f-483b-a90f-e8d1c0dd327b/docketentrydocuments/2eebded0-ac17-44f6-b7b0-e4970dae5111#:~:text=Appraiser%E2%80%99s%20office%2C%20identified%20himself%20as,title%20search%20to%20file%2036).

  - **Key Mistake:** Simply **not recording a NOC** at all is a major
    owner error. Florida law mandates an NOC for most private projects,
    and *Roof Structures* shows why: without an NOC,
    subcontractors/suppliers must hunt for project information and may
    get it wrong. Here the owner’s noncompliance nearly cost them the
    protection of receiving a Notice to Owner (which is intended to give
    owners a heads-up to withhold payment if necessary). Owners who fail
    to record a NOC forfeit certain defenses – essentially, they lose
    the right to complain about defects in preliminary notices that
    result from their own
    omission[acis-api.flcourts.gov](https://acis-api.flcourts.gov/courts/8f454fb9-4c7f-43df-856b-ab373e71c27f/cms/case/86ec4c23-2d4f-483b-a90f-e8d1c0dd327b/docketentrydocuments/2eebded0-ac17-44f6-b7b0-e4970dae5111#:~:text=Appraiser%E2%80%99s%20office%2C%20identified%20himself%20as,title%20search%20to%20file%2036)[caselaw.findlaw.com](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/court/fl-district-court-of-appeal/2071581.html#:~:text=3,which%20the%20owner%20took%20title).

  - **Impact:** *Roof Structures* protects lienors’ rights in the face
    of owner noncompliance, ensuring that a **mechanic’s lien remains
    valid** even if the lienor’s Notice to Owner was imperfect due to
    the missing NOC info. It incentivizes owners to always record and
    post the NOC (since failing to do so can undermine their ability to
    contest liens for lack of notice). From the lienor’s perspective,
    this case (and others like it) provide an **“equitable safety net”**
    – if you make a reasonable, good-faith effort to comply and any
    mistake is traceable to the owner’s failure to follow the statute,
    the courts may still enforce your
    lien[acis-api.flcourts.gov](https://acis-api.flcourts.gov/courts/8f454fb9-4c7f-43df-856b-ab373e71c27f/cms/case/86ec4c23-2d4f-483b-a90f-e8d1c0dd327b/docketentrydocuments/2eebded0-ac17-44f6-b7b0-e4970dae5111#:~:text=Appraiser%E2%80%99s%20office%2C%20identified%20himself%20as,title%20search%20to%20file%2036)[caselaw.findlaw.com](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/court/fl-district-court-of-appeal/2071581.html#:~:text=3,which%20the%20owner%20took%20title).

**S & S Air Conditioning Co. v. Cantor**, 343 So.2d 923 (Fla. 3d DCA
1977) – *Residential (contractor vs. homeowner).*

  - **Scenario:** This early case dealt with an owner who **never
    recorded a NOC**, and a contractor who needed to serve a Notice to
    Owner. In absence of an NOC (which would normally list a specific
    address or designated agent for the owner), the contractor sent the
    notice to the **owner’s address as shown on the deed/public
    records**. The owner later claimed they never actually received the
    notice (perhaps the address was outdated or the owner was an
    out-of-state entity).

  - **Holding:** The Third DCA held that if **no NOC is recorded**, an
    owner is **estopped from denying the effectiveness of a Notice to
    Owner that was sent to the address in the public deed
    records**[caselaw.findlaw.com](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/court/fl-district-court-of-appeal/2071581.html#:~:text=relied%20on%20the%20information%20contained,which%20the%20owner%20took%20title).
    In other words, the contractor’s notice was deemed sufficient, even
    if the owner didn’t see it, because the owner could have prevented
    the issue by recording a NOC with a correct service
    address[caselaw.findlaw.com](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/court/fl-district-court-of-appeal/2071581.html#:~:text=relied%20on%20the%20information%20contained,which%20the%20owner%20took%20title).
    This aligns with the principle in *Roof Structures*: an owner who
    neglects the NOC cannot complain about technical notice deficiencies
    that occur as a result.

  - **Takeaway:** *S & S Air Conditioning* underscores that owners must
    **post and record a NOC to specify how they wish to receive
    notices**. If they don’t, lienors are entitled to rely on *any
    reasonable source of ownership information* (like property appraiser
    or deed records). Any bad address or missed communication is at the
    owner’s peril, not the
    lienor’s[caselaw.findlaw.com](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/court/fl-district-court-of-appeal/2071581.html#:~:text=relied%20on%20the%20information%20contained,which%20the%20owner%20took%20title).
    This case also illustrates the courts’ broader willingness to
    **forgive lienors’ procedural missteps when the owner’s
    noncompliance set the stage for the
    error**[caselaw.findlaw.com](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/court/fl-district-court-of-appeal/2071581.html#:~:text=relied%20on%20the%20information%20contained,which%20the%20owner%20took%20title).
    It was an important early decision carving out an exception to
    strict compliance – effectively foreshadowing the more formalized
    substantial compliance provisions in later statutes (e.g.,
    §713.06(2)(f) allowing some errors in NTO if no
    prejudice)[caselaw.findlaw.com](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/court/fl-district-court-of-appeal/2071581.html#:~:text=%28Fla,%282013).

**Symons Corp. v. Tartan-Lavers Delray Beach, Inc.**, 456 So.2d 1254
(Fla. 4th DCA 1984) – *Commercial development (subcontractor vs.
developer).*

  - **Scenario:** In this case, a subcontractor sent its Notice to Owner
    to the address/person listed on the recorded NOC. However, there was
    a dispute over whether the **owner actually received the notice**
    (perhaps the address was a jobsite or an office where the owner’s
    agent failed to forward it). The owner argued non-receipt as a
    defense against the lien.

  - **Holding:** The Fourth DCA ruled that when a lienor **detrimentally
    relies on the information in a properly recorded NOC to send the
    Notice to Owner, the owner is estopped from denying receipt of that
    notice[caselaw.findlaw.com](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/court/fl-district-court-of-appeal/2071581.html#:~:text=3,which%20the%20owner%20took%20title).**
    In Symons, the NOC had designated a certain person or address for
    service, and the subcontractor complied with that. The court would
    not allow the owner to avoid the lien by claiming the notice didn’t
    reach them; by listing that contact in the NOC, the owner
    effectively bore the risk of communication going
    awry[caselaw.findlaw.com](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/court/fl-district-court-of-appeal/2071581.html#:~:text=3,which%20the%20owner%20took%20title).

  - **Key Point:** *Symons* reinforces that the **NOC is binding on the
    owner with respect to notice**. If the owner (or its authorized
    agent) is named in the NOC to receive notices, the owner can’t later
    shrug off a lien by saying “I never saw the preliminary notice.”
    This case complements *S & S Air Conditioning* – together, they tell
    owners: **provide correct notice details in your NOC, or you waive
    defenses related to notice
    delivery**[caselaw.findlaw.com](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/court/fl-district-court-of-appeal/2071581.html#:~:text=3,which%20the%20owner%20took%20title).
    For lienors, the message is to **strictly follow the NOC’s
    guidance** on who/where to send the Notice to Owner; doing so will
    protect you, even if the owner tries to claim a lack of actual
    notice.

  - **Practice Tip:** Always keep proof of mailing or delivery of the
    Notice to Owner to the exact name/address in the NOC (or deed
    records if no NOC). In any later lien foreclosure, cases like
    *Symons* mean that proof will establish a presumption of notice. The
    owner cannot defeat your lien by playing “gotcha” with notice
    receipt if you adhered to the NOC’s
    instructions[caselaw.findlaw.com](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/court/fl-district-court-of-appeal/2071581.html#:~:text=3,which%20the%20owner%20took%20title).

### NOC Compliance in Landlord-Tenant Situations

**MHB Construction Services, LLC v. RM-NA HB Waterway Shoppes, LLC**, 74
So.3d 587 (Fla. 4th DCA 2011) – *Commercial leasehold improvement
(contractor vs. landlord & tenant).*

  - **Parties/Context:** A contractor (MHB) was hired by a **tenant** to
    build out a leased space in a shopping center. The landlord (RM-NA
    Waterway Shoppes) **did not directly contract** for the
    improvements, but to facilitate the tenant’s permit, the landlord
    **executed and recorded a Notice of Commencement** for the project
    (as is common in commercial
    leases)[kpsds.com](https://kpsds.com/construction-law/does-landlords-filing-of-notice-of-commencement-give-contractor-hired-by-tenant-the-right-to-lien-landlords-property#:~:text=In%20MHB%20Construction%20Services%2C%20LLC,Broward%20County%20in%20accordance%20with)[kpsds.com](https://kpsds.com/construction-law/does-landlords-filing-of-notice-of-commencement-give-contractor-hired-by-tenant-the-right-to-lien-landlords-property#:~:text=a%20tenant%20at%20a%20shopping,The%20trial).
    Importantly, two years prior, the landlord had also recorded a
    **“Notice of Lien Prohibition”** in accordance with §713.10,
    declaring that a tenant’s improvements would not attach as liens on
    the landlord’s fee
    interest[kpsds.com](https://kpsds.com/construction-law/does-landlords-filing-of-notice-of-commencement-give-contractor-hired-by-tenant-the-right-to-lien-landlords-property#:~:text=landlord%E2%80%99s%20fee%20simple%20interest%20in,landlord%2C%20and%20the%20contractor%20appealed).
    The tenant later failed to pay the contractor, so the contractor
    filed a lien and sued both tenant and landlord to foreclose.

  - **NOC Issue:** The contractor argued that by recording the NOC and
    identifying itself as the owner (or as having a controlling stake in
    the construction), the landlord had essentially held itself out as
    an **“owner” for lien purposes**, thus exposing the landlord’s
    property interest to the
    lien[kpsds.com](https://kpsds.com/construction-law/does-landlords-filing-of-notice-of-commencement-give-contractor-hired-by-tenant-the-right-to-lien-landlords-property#:~:text=On%20appeal%2C%20the%20contractor%20argued,place%2C%20the%20Fourth%20DCA%20held).
    In other words, MHB contended the landlord’s act of filing an NOC
    **estopped** the landlord from claiming the protection of the
    §713.10 lien
    prohibition[kpsds.com](https://kpsds.com/construction-law/does-landlords-filing-of-notice-of-commencement-give-contractor-hired-by-tenant-the-right-to-lien-landlords-property#:~:text=The%20Fourth%20District%20Court%20of,rights%20under%20the%20lien%20law)[kpsds.com](https://kpsds.com/construction-law/does-landlords-filing-of-notice-of-commencement-give-contractor-hired-by-tenant-the-right-to-lien-landlords-property#:~:text=On%20appeal%2C%20the%20contractor%20argued,place%2C%20the%20Fourth%20DCA%20held).

  - **Outcome:** The Fourth DCA rejected the contractor’s argument and
    sided with the landlord. It held that **nothing in §713.13 (NOC
    statute) suggests that a landlord’s execution of an NOC magically
    gives a contractor the right to lien the landlord’s fee interest if
    the landlord wasn’t party to the construction
    contract**[kpsds.com](https://kpsds.com/construction-law/does-landlords-filing-of-notice-of-commencement-give-contractor-hired-by-tenant-the-right-to-lien-landlords-property#:~:text=On%20appeal%2C%20the%20contractor%20argued,a%20tenant%20from%20attaching%20to).
    In this case, the landlord had fully complied with §713.10: the
    lease and the recorded Notice of Lien Prohibition *expressly
    forbade* liens attaching to the landlord’s interest for tenant
    improvements[kpsds.com](https://kpsds.com/construction-law/does-landlords-filing-of-notice-of-commencement-give-contractor-hired-by-tenant-the-right-to-lien-landlords-property#:~:text=construction%2C%E2%80%9D%20exposing%20it%20to%20the,10).
    The court found that those protections were not undone by the
    landlord signing the NOC as a courtesy or legal
    formality[kpsds.com](https://kpsds.com/construction-law/does-landlords-filing-of-notice-of-commencement-give-contractor-hired-by-tenant-the-right-to-lien-landlords-property#:~:text=construction%2C%E2%80%9D%20exposing%20it%20to%20the,of%20a%20landlord%20under%20section).
    Consequently, MHB’s lien could only attach to the tenant’s leasehold
    interest (if at all), and the landlord was properly dismissed from
    the foreclosure
    suit[caselaw.findlaw.com](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/fl-district-court-of-appeal/1586479.html#:~:text=could%20lien%20Landlord%27s%20fee%20simple,awarding%20attorney%27s%20fees%20and%20costs)[caselaw.findlaw.com](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/fl-district-court-of-appeal/1586479.html#:~:text=MHB%20argues%20that%20by%20executing,%E2%80%9D%20We%20disagree).
    The landlord was also awarded attorneys’ fees as the prevailing
    party[caselaw.findlaw.com](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/fl-district-court-of-appeal/1586479.html#:~:text=In%20its%20consolidated%20appeals%2C%20MHB,awarding%20attorney%27s%20fees%20and%20costs).

  - **Rationale:** The court’s analysis highlighted the **purpose of a
    Notice of Commencement** (quoting *Sasso* and *Symons* on how NOC
    info facilitates Notices to
    Owner)[caselaw.findlaw.com](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/fl-district-court-of-appeal/1586479.html#:~:text=We%20begin%20our%20analysis%20by,13%281%29%28a%29%2C%20Florida%20Statutes)[caselaw.findlaw.com](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/fl-district-court-of-appeal/1586479.html#:~:text=Though%20the%20Notice%20of%20Commencement,mail%20the%20Notice%20to%20Owner).
    It distinguished that purpose from the distinct protections of
    §713.10 for non-contracting landlords. The **key factors** were:
    (1) The **lease did not require** the improvements (so the landlord
    had not mandated the construction as a condition of the
    lease)[caselaw.findlaw.com](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/fl-district-court-of-appeal/1586479.html#:~:text=The%20lease%20did%20not%20require,Landlord%27s%20%2410%2C000%20contribution%20towards%20MHB%27s);
    (2) The lease in fact required the tenant to get **written consent**
    from the landlord for any improvements (reinforcing that the tenant
    was the sole contracting
    party)[caselaw.findlaw.com](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/fl-district-court-of-appeal/1586479.html#:~:text=The%20lease%20did%20not%20require,Landlord%27s%20%2410%2C000%20contribution%20towards%20MHB%27s);
    and (3) The landlord’s financial contribution to the project was
    minimal (\<$10k, under 10% of the cost, and contingent on final
    releases)[caselaw.findlaw.com](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/fl-district-court-of-appeal/1586479.html#:~:text=making%20improvements%29,require%20the%20improvements%20be%20made),
    indicating the landlord was not essentially an “owner” of the
    construction. Given those facts, plus the recorded lien prohibition,
    the court ruled that **the landlord’s interest was protected**
    despite the landlord having recorded the
    NOC[caselaw.findlaw.com](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/fl-district-court-of-appeal/1586479.html#:~:text=MHB%20was%20in%20the%20best,costs%20in%20the%20underlying%20action)[caselaw.findlaw.com](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/fl-district-court-of-appeal/1586479.html#:~:text=1,a%20final%20release%20from%20MHB).
    The NOC in this context was simply fulfilling a procedural role
    (likely to satisfy building permit requirements), not signaling that
    the landlord had assumed liability for the tenant’s contractor.
    Indeed, the court noted the NOC **properly listed the tenant as the
    “owner”** (with a leasehold interest) per
    §713.13(1)(a)3[caselaw.findlaw.com](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/fl-district-court-of-appeal/1586479.html#:~:text=2,interest%20was%20a%20leasehold%20interest).
    Thus, there was **no misrepresentation**: any lienor reading the NOC
    would know the improvement was for a leasehold, putting them on
    notice of the need to protect themselves (which the contractor could
    have by contract with the landlord or payment bond,
    etc.)[caselaw.findlaw.com](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/fl-district-court-of-appeal/1586479.html#:~:text=MHB%20was%20in%20the%20best,costs%20in%20the%20underlying%20action)[caselaw.findlaw.com](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/fl-district-court-of-appeal/1586479.html#:~:text=2,interest%20was%20a%20leasehold%20interest).

  - **Common Mistakes:** The contractor’s **misconception** here was
    assuming that a landlord-recorded NOC = landlord consent to be
    liable for liens. Florida law allows landlords to protect themselves
    via lease clauses and a recorded Notice of Lien Prohibition, as done
    here[kpsds.com](https://kpsds.com/construction-law/does-landlords-filing-of-notice-of-commencement-give-contractor-hired-by-tenant-the-right-to-lien-landlords-property#:~:text=construction%2C%E2%80%9D%20exposing%20it%20to%20the,10).
    A mistake some landlords make is **failing to record a lien
    prohibition or to properly draft the lease** – had the landlord not
    done so, a different outcome might occur (liens could reach the
    landlord’s interest if the lease *required* improvements or if the
    landlord funded a substantial
    portion)[caselaw.findlaw.com](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/fl-district-court-of-appeal/1586479.html#:~:text=level%20of%20protection%20afforded%20to,landlords%20under%20Chapter%20713)[caselaw.findlaw.com](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/fl-district-court-of-appeal/1586479.html#:~:text=making%20improvements%29,require%20the%20improvements%20be%20made).
    Another potential mistake is if a landlord’s NOC **lists the wrong
    “owner”**. Here, the landlord wisely listed the tenant and
    identified the interest as
    leasehold[caselaw.findlaw.com](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/fl-district-court-of-appeal/1586479.html#:~:text=2,interest%20was%20a%20leasehold%20interest).
    If the NOC had improperly named the landlord as the fee owner
    without mention of the leasehold, it might have strengthened the
    contractor’s estoppel argument. Thus, **accuracy in NOC parties** is
    critical in lease situations to avoid
    ambiguity[caselaw.findlaw.com](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/fl-district-court-of-appeal/1586479.html#:~:text=2,interest%20was%20a%20leasehold%20interest).

  - **Impact:** *MHB Construction v. Waterway Shoppes* clarifies that a
    landlord can engage in NOC formalities without surrendering its
    statutory protections. It distinguishes the NOC’s notice role from
    the substantive property rights addressed in §713.10. The case
    serves as a guide: **Landlords** should always (1) include anti-lien
    clauses in leases for tenant work, (2) record a Notice of Lien
    Prohibition before lease
    execution[caselaw.findlaw.com](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/fl-district-court-of-appeal/1586479.html#:~:text=Landlord%20is%20the%20owner%20of,13%2C%20Florida%20Statutes%20%282010),
    and (3) if they must sign an NOC for permitting, **identify the
    tenant as the owner (leasehold)** to comply with
    §713.13[caselaw.findlaw.com](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/fl-district-court-of-appeal/1586479.html#:~:text=2,interest%20was%20a%20leasehold%20interest).
    **Contractors** working for tenants are reminded that they **cannot
    rely solely on an NOC to secure lien rights against the landlord**.
    They should check the lease and public records for a lien
    prohibition. If one exists (as in this case), the contractor should
    seek additional security (e.g. a payment bond or guaranty) or ensure
    direct payment arrangements, since their lien will only reach the
    tenant’s interest, which may be of little value if the tenant
    defaults[kpsds.com](https://kpsds.com/construction-law/does-landlords-filing-of-notice-of-commencement-give-contractor-hired-by-tenant-the-right-to-lien-landlords-property#:~:text=construction%2C%E2%80%9D%20exposing%20it%20to%20the,10)[caselaw.findlaw.com](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/fl-district-court-of-appeal/1586479.html#:~:text=level%20of%20protection%20afforded%20to,landlords%20under%20Chapter%20713).

## Trends in Judicial Interpretation of NOC Requirements

The case law reveals a clear **trend toward protecting diligent lienors
and enforcing the purpose of the lien law**, rather than rigidly voiding
liens for every technical NOC defect. Several themes emerge:

  - **Substantial Compliance vs. Strict Compliance:** While courts often
    recite that lien laws must be strictly
    construed[caselaw.findlaw.com](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/court/fl-district-court-of-appeal/2071581.html#:~:text=laws%20were%20enacted%20for%20the,substantially%20complied%20with%20the%20statutory),
    in practice Florida courts allow **substantial compliance** with the
    NOC requirements when the underlying purposes are met and no party
    is prejudiced. Especially in the context of NOCs, courts have been
    willing to forgive **minor defects** (missing signatures, slight
    inaccuracies) as long as the NOC effectively gives notice and
    information to
    lienors[taylordaylaw.com](https://www.taylordaylaw.com/2024/10/notices-of-commencement-for-florida-construction-jobs/#:~:text=,in%20the%20Notice%20of%20Commencement)[caselaw.findlaw.com](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/court/fl-district-court-of-appeal/2071581.html#:~:text=We%20find%20Sasso%20instructive,an%20updated%20title%20search%20through).
    The flip side is that the party seeking to invalidate a lien must
    show real prejudice from the NOC error – merely pointing out a
    technical flaw is not enough if the lienor did everything else right
    and the objecting party could have protected
    itself[caselaw.findlaw.com](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/court/fl-district-court-of-appeal/2071581.html#:~:text=In%20reaching%20its%20conclusion%2C%20the,the%20notice%20of%20commencement%20where)[caselaw.findlaw.com](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/court/fl-district-court-of-appeal/2071581.html#:~:text=the%20lienor%20to%20properly%20mail,4).
    The **modern trend** is epitomized by *Sasso* and *Edwin Taylor*: a
    NOC that is “regular and complete in all respects” except for an
    owner’s oversight will still fulfill its role, and the courts will
    treat it as
    effective[caselaw.findlaw.com](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/court/fl-district-court-of-appeal/2071581.html#:~:text=to%20the%20void%20notice%20of,had%20no%20control%2C%20failed%20to)[caselaw.findlaw.com](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/court/fl-district-court-of-appeal/2071581.html#:~:text=We%20find%20Sasso%20instructive,an%20updated%20title%20search%20through).
    This shift aligns with statutory tweaks that codify substantial
    compliance for lien notices (e.g. §713.06(2)(f) regarding NTO
    errors)[caselaw.findlaw.com](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/court/fl-district-court-of-appeal/2071581.html#:~:text=%28Fla,%282013),
    signalling judicial unwillingness to impose draconian results on
    lienors for owner errors.

  - **Owner’s Burden and Lienor’s Reliance:** Florida courts uniformly
    place the **burden of NOC compliance on the owner (or its agent)**,
    not on
    lienors[caselaw.findlaw.com](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/court/fl-district-court-of-appeal/2071581.html#:~:text=stead,to%20shift%20this%20risk%20to)[caselaw.findlaw.com](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/court/fl-district-court-of-appeal/2071581.html#:~:text=,the%20lack%20of%20authority%20for).
    An owner who fails to record a proper NOC (or any NOC at all) will
    find the courts decidedly unsympathetic to arguments that a lienor
    didn’t know who to notify or got some detail wrong as a
    result[acis-api.flcourts.gov](https://acis-api.flcourts.gov/courts/8f454fb9-4c7f-43df-856b-ab373e71c27f/cms/case/86ec4c23-2d4f-483b-a90f-e8d1c0dd327b/docketentrydocuments/2eebded0-ac17-44f6-b7b0-e4970dae5111#:~:text=Appraiser%E2%80%99s%20office%2C%20identified%20himself%20as,title%20search%20to%20file%2036)[caselaw.findlaw.com](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/court/fl-district-court-of-appeal/2071581.html#:~:text=relied%20on%20the%20information%20contained,which%20the%20owner%20took%20title).
    The decisions in *Roof Structures*, *S & S Air*, and *Symons*
    establish a form of **estoppel** against owners who are derelict in
    their NOC duty: such an owner cannot later use lack of notice or
    errors by the lienor (which stem from missing or bad NOC info) as a
    sword to defeat an otherwise valid
    lien[acis-api.flcourts.gov](https://acis-api.flcourts.gov/courts/8f454fb9-4c7f-43df-856b-ab373e71c27f/cms/case/86ec4c23-2d4f-483b-a90f-e8d1c0dd327b/docketentrydocuments/2eebded0-ac17-44f6-b7b0-e4970dae5111#:~:text=Appraiser%E2%80%99s%20office%2C%20identified%20himself%20as,title%20search%20to%20file%2036)[caselaw.findlaw.com](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/court/fl-district-court-of-appeal/2071581.html#:~:text=3,which%20the%20owner%20took%20title).
    In contrast, when a lienor fails to comply with *its own*
    obligations (like serving an NTO) and tries to excuse it by an NOC
    defect, courts are less forgiving – *Gulfside Properties Corp. v.
    Chapman* (Fla. 1st DCA 1999) illustrates that if a lienor makes **no
    good-faith effort** to meet the statutory requirements, a defective
    NOC will not save their
    lien[caselaw.findlaw.com](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/court/fl-district-court-of-appeal/2071581.html#:~:text=The%20materialman%20sought%20to%20deflect,713%20were%20met%2C%20which%20included)[caselaw.findlaw.com](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/court/fl-district-court-of-appeal/2071581.html#:~:text=the%20party%20seeking%20to%20enforce,statutory%20requirements%20of%20a%20lien%E2%80%9D).
    The initial burden is on the lienor to show they complied or
    substantially complied with the lien laws; an owner’s mistake
    doesn’t automatically cure a lienor’s complete
    noncompliance[caselaw.findlaw.com](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/court/fl-district-court-of-appeal/2071581.html#:~:text=the%20party%20seeking%20to%20enforce,statutory%20requirements%20of%20a%20lien%E2%80%9D).
    Thus, the case law draws a line: **good-faith reliance is
    protected**, but **laxity on the lienor’s part is not**.

  - **Equitable Considerations and No-Free-Ride for Third Parties:** A
    strong equitable undercurrent is evident. Courts are guided by the
    fundamental purpose described in *Crane Co. v. Fine* – to provide
    those improving property a fair chance to get
    paid[caselaw.findlaw.com](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/court/fl-district-court-of-appeal/2071581.html#:~:text=%E2%80%9C,The%20construction%20lien).
    They resist outcomes where an innocent lienor is blindsided by an
    owner’s or lender’s technicality. For example, both *Sasso* and
    *Edwin Taylor* stress that a **lender** who could have discovered an
    issue (via title search or insisting on NOC termination) should not
    gain a windfall from an owner’s mistake at the expense of a lienor
    who improved the
    property[caselaw.findlaw.com](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/court/fl-district-court-of-appeal/2071581.html#:~:text=In%20reaching%20its%20conclusion%2C%20the,the%20notice%20of%20commencement%20where)[caselaw.findlaw.com](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/court/fl-district-court-of-appeal/2071581.html#:~:text=the%20lienor%20to%20properly%20mail,4).
    The **“most able to protect itself”** should bear the risk – often
    that is the owner or lender, rather than the unwitting
    subcontractor[caselaw.findlaw.com](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/court/fl-district-court-of-appeal/2071581.html#:~:text=In%20reaching%20its%20conclusion%2C%20the,the%20notice%20of%20commencement%20where).
    Consequently, Florida courts have curtailed attempts by banks or
    owners to use NOC technical noncompliance as a **hyper-technical
    escape hatch** from paying. The only scenario where NOC
    noncompliance decisively works against a lienor is when the lienor’s
    own neglect is the primary issue (*e.g.*, failing to serve any
    notice to owner as in *Gulfside*, where the NOC defect was merely a
    sideshow)[caselaw.findlaw.com](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/court/fl-district-court-of-appeal/2071581.html#:~:text=The%20materialman%20sought%20to%20deflect,713%20were%20met%2C%20which%20included)[caselaw.findlaw.com](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/court/fl-district-court-of-appeal/2071581.html#:~:text=the%20party%20seeking%20to%20enforce,statutory%20requirements%20of%20a%20lien%E2%80%9D).

  - **Current Law Alignment:** Notably, the courts’ interpretations have
    generally been consistent with the current statute’s text and
    intent. Section 713.13 was amended over time (for instance, the
    threshold dollar amount for requiring an NOC has increased to $5,000
    for owner-occupied single-family
    jobs[floridabar.org](https://www.floridabar.org/the-florida-bar-journal/statutes-affecting-florida-construction/#:~:text=,not%20apply%20to%20maintenance%20work),
    and there’s a procedure for **amending NOCs** and **terminating NOCs
    early** in §713.13(5) and §713.132). However, none of the cited
    cases rely on outdated provisions – they interpret duties (owner
    signatory, info requirements, effect of noncompliance) that still
    exist in the statute today. The **legislature appears to approve of
    these decisions**, as subsequent amendments did not override the
    judicial rulings. In fact, legislative staff analyses cite *Edwin
    Taylor* and *Sasso* approvingly when discussing NOC
    issues[flsenate.gov](https://www.flsenate.gov/Session/Bill/2023/797/Analyses/h0797a.CJS.PDF#:~:text=19%20S,S).
    This suggests a stable understanding: the owner must get the NOC
    right, but minor lapses won’t defeat a lienor absent real harm, in
    line with the statute’s goals.

Overall, Florida courts have carved a pragmatic path: **ensure NOCs are
done right whenever possible, but do not let minor NOC faults thwart
meritorious lien claims**. The judicial message to owners and lenders is
clear – *take the NOC requirement seriously, or risk unintended
consequences*, whereas the message to contractors and subs is *do your
part (serve notices, file timely liens) and the courts will not lightly
strip away your rights due to someone else’s error*.

## Practical Recommendations for Compliance and Preserving Lien Rights

**For Property Owners (and Developers/Landlords):**

  - **Record the NOC Before Work Begins:** Always record a Notice of
    Commencement with the clerk **prior to the start of construction**
    (before the first inspection at the
    latest)[syfert.com](https://syfert.com/florida/statutes/0713.13.html#:~:text=WARNING%20TO%20OWNER%3A%20ANY%20PAYMENTS,RECORDING%20YOUR%20NOTICE%20OF%20COMMENCEMENT)[syfert.com](https://syfert.com/florida/statutes/0713.13.html#:~:text=,notice%20in%20the%20clerk%E2%80%99s%20office).
    Florida law makes this step mandatory (with limited exceptions), and
    failing to do so can expose you to liens attaching out of the blue
    with no recorded “anchor”
    date[taylordaylaw.com](https://www.taylordaylaw.com/2024/10/notices-of-commencement-for-florida-construction-jobs/#:~:text=Nevertheless%2C%20the%20standard%20here%20is,notice%20of%20commencement%20been%20filed).
    If construction commences without an NOC, any later-filed lien will
    attach as of its recording date – meaning it might take priority
    over mortgages or transfers you record in the
    interim[taylordaylaw.com](https://www.taylordaylaw.com/2024/10/notices-of-commencement-for-florida-construction-jobs/#:~:text=Nevertheless%2C%20the%20standard%20here%20is,notice%20of%20commencement%20been%20filed).
    Additionally, not having an NOC means you might **pay the wrong
    party** or miss critical information; it also deprives you of
    certain defenses in case of a
    dispute[acis-api.flcourts.gov](https://acis-api.flcourts.gov/courts/8f454fb9-4c7f-43df-856b-ab373e71c27f/cms/case/86ec4c23-2d4f-483b-a90f-e8d1c0dd327b/docketentrydocuments/2eebded0-ac17-44f6-b7b0-e4970dae5111#:~:text=Appraiser%E2%80%99s%20office%2C%20identified%20himself%20as,title%20search%20to%20file%2036).
    In short, treat the NOC as a first-day requirement (often, building
    permits won’t be issued until an NOC is filed and posted, under
    §713.135).

  - **Fill Out the NOC Completely and Correctly:** Use the statutory
    form in §713.13(1)(d) and provide *all required information*:
    correct legal description of the property, your full name (and
    anyone else with an ownership interest), the contractor’s name,
    lender’s name, surety/bond info (if any), etc. **Do not omit known
    data**. An inaccurate NOC can lead to misdirected notices and
    jeopardize the smooth handling of lien waivers or
    claims[caselaw.findlaw.com](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/court/fl-district-court-of-appeal/2071581.html#:~:text=stead,to%20shift%20this%20risk%20to)[caselaw.findlaw.com](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/court/fl-district-court-of-appeal/2071581.html#:~:text=,the%20lack%20of%20authority%20for).
    Double-check spelling of names and addresses. If a mistake is
    discovered after recording, file an **Amended NOC** (per §713.13(5))
    referencing the original recording and correct the info. Serve
    copies of the amended NOC on the contractor and any lienors who have
    already sent Notices to Owner, as required by law, to ensure
    everyone is on the same
    page[flsenate.gov](https://www.flsenate.gov/Session/Bill/2023/797/Analyses/h0797a.CJS.PDF#:~:text=8%20Such%20notice%20must%20be,interest%20in%20the%20property%3B%2010)[flsenate.gov](https://www.flsenate.gov/Session/Bill/2023/797/Analyses/h0797a.CJS.PDF#:~:text=o%20Persons%2C%20designated%20by%20the,12).
    Remember, as the owner you **carry the risk of errors** – a typo or
    wrong address won’t invalidate lienors’ claims, it will just become
    your problem to sort out
    later[caselaw.findlaw.com](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/court/fl-district-court-of-appeal/2071581.html#:~:text=stead,to%20shift%20this%20risk%20to).
    It is far better to get it right upfront.

  - **Ensure the NOC is Properly Signed**: Florida Statutes
    §713.13(1)(g) explicitly requires the owner’s signature on the
    NOC[caselaw.findlaw.com](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/court/fl-district-court-of-appeal/2071581.html#:~:text=The%20statute%20allows%20the%20owner,5th%20DCA%201988).
    *Do not delegate this lightly.* Although courts have upheld NOCs
    signed by someone else with authority (to avoid harming
    lienors)[caselaw.findlaw.com](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/court/fl-district-court-of-appeal/2071581.html#:~:text=Accordingly%2C%20we%20hold%20that%20a,lender%20on%20this%20basis%20was),
    it is risky and **not technically compliant**. If you are an
    individual owner, sign it yourself (and have it notarized as
    required). If the property is owned by an entity (LLC, corporation),
    an authorized officer or manager should sign. Never have the
    contractor or a third party sign *in place of the owner* unless
    absolutely necessary – and even then, be prepared to later attest
    that they had your
    authority[taylordaylaw.com](https://www.taylordaylaw.com/2024/10/notices-of-commencement-for-florida-construction-jobs/#:~:text=that%20is%20required%20to%20satisfy,2d%20DCA)[taylordaylaw.com](https://www.taylordaylaw.com/2024/10/notices-of-commencement-for-florida-construction-jobs/#:~:text=,complied%20with%20construction%20lien%20statute).
    An unsigned or improperly signed NOC could be challenged and at
    minimum will create factual disputes (as seen in *Edwin Taylor* and
    *Sasso* cases). **Bottom line:** sign the NOC to eliminate this
    point of contention.

  - **Post the NOC at the Job Site:** After recording, post a certified
    copy (or a notarized statement of recording along with a copy of the
    NOC) at the project site in a conspicuous
    place[syfert.com](https://syfert.com/florida/statutes/0713.13.html#:~:text=WARNING%20TO%20OWNER%3A%20ANY%20PAYMENTS,RECORDING%20YOUR%20NOTICE%20OF%20COMMENCEMENT)[syfert.com](https://syfert.com/florida/statutes/0713.13.html#:~:text=I%2C%20SECTION%20713,RECORDING%20YOUR%20NOTICE%20OF%20COMMENCEMENT).
    This is required by §713.13(1)(a) and helps all subcontractors and
    suppliers identify the project and obtain the NOC details even if
    they don’t immediately check county records. Failure to post doesn’t
    nullify the NOC, but it may mislead lienors or delay their notices.
    An unposted NOC could, for example, lead a lienor to think no NOC
    exists and rely on other info (increasing the chance of mistakes).
    It could also violate local building department rules (since
    inspectors look for the posted NOC). As an owner, make sure the
    contractor knows to maintain that posting throughout the job (post a
    new copy if the original is removed or weather-worn).

  - **Mind the Effective Period (Expiration):** An NOC by default
    expires 1 year from the date of recording unless otherwise
    stated[syfert.com](https://syfert.com/florida/statutes/0713.13.html#:~:text=9,).
    For long projects, you **can and should state a longer duration** in
    the NOC (e.g., “effective for 18 months” or for a specific
    date)[syfert.com](https://syfert.com/florida/statutes/0713.13.html#:~:text=,commencement%20are%20considered%20improper%20payments).
    If a project is delayed and will run beyond the NOC’s expiration,
    consider recording a new NOC or an extension before the original
    lapses. If the NOC expires and you continue making payments, those
    payments may be deemed “improper” (since lienors could arise whose
    priority is no longer
    anchored)[syfert.com](https://syfert.com/florida/statutes/0713.13.html#:~:text=WARNING%20TO%20OWNER%3A%20ANY%20PAYMENTS,RECORDING%20YOUR%20NOTICE%20OF%20COMMENCEMENT).
    In fact, the statutory warning on the NOC form says any payment made
    by the owner after expiration of the NOC is *“considered improper”*
    and could result in paying
    twice[syfert.com](https://syfert.com/florida/statutes/0713.13.html#:~:text=WARNING%20TO%20OWNER%3A%20ANY%20PAYMENTS,RECORDING%20YOUR%20NOTICE%20OF%20COMMENCEMENT).
    Cases like *Adamson v. First Federal* (Fla. 1st DCA 1988) illustrate
    that if a loan disburses after NOC expiration, intervening liens
    might gain priority, to the detriment of the owner or
    lender[flsenate.gov](https://www.flsenate.gov/Session/Bill/2023/797/Analyses/h0797a.CJS.PDF#:~:text=19%20S,S).
    **Tip:** Calendar the NOC expiration and don’t let it quietly lapse
    if work is ongoing.

  - **Attach the Payment Bond (if applicable):** If there is a payment
    bond for the project under §713.23, attach a copy of it to the NOC
    when
    recording[syfert.com](https://syfert.com/florida/statutes/0713.13.html#:~:text=,limits%20for%20serving%20any%20required).
    Not doing so doesn’t invalidate the NOC, but it **negates the bond’s
    protection** for the
    owner[syfert.com](https://syfert.com/florida/statutes/0713.13.html#:~:text=,limits%20for%20serving%20any%20required).
    (When a bond is properly attached and noticed, lienors must make
    claims on the bond instead of the property; if you fail to attach
    it, lienors can ignore the bond and file
    liens[syfert.com](https://syfert.com/florida/statutes/0713.13.html#:~:text=,limits%20for%20serving%20any%20required).)
    Attaching the bond also informs all subs of its existence. In short,
    to preserve the **bond exemption** provided in §713.02(6), always
    record the bond with the
    NOC[syfert.com](https://syfert.com/florida/statutes/0713.13.html#:~:text=,limits%20for%20serving%20any%20required).

  - **Leases – Use NOC to Your Advantage:** If you’re a landlord
    allowing a tenant’s construction, record an NOC **naming the tenant
    as the owner (lessee) and describing the leasehold
    interest**[caselaw.findlaw.com](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/fl-district-court-of-appeal/1586479.html#:~:text=2,interest%20was%20a%20leasehold%20interest).
    This complies with §713.13 and clarifies to lienors that your tenant
    is the contracting party. *Also*, record a **Notice of Lien
    Prohibition** (§713.10) in the public records *before* the lease or
    NOC, stating that improvements by the tenant won’t attach to your
    interest[kpsds.com](https://kpsds.com/construction-law/does-landlords-filing-of-notice-of-commencement-give-contractor-hired-by-tenant-the-right-to-lien-landlords-property#:~:text=landlord%E2%80%99s%20fee%20simple%20interest%20in,landlord%2C%20and%20the%20contractor%20appealed).
    (And include similar language in the lease
    agreement[caselaw.findlaw.com](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/fl-district-court-of-appeal/1586479.html#:~:text=1,a%20final%20release%20from%20MHB).)
    These steps, as shown in *MHB Construction*, will protect you from
    most liens if the tenant’s contractor isn’t
    paid[kpsds.com](https://kpsds.com/construction-law/does-landlords-filing-of-notice-of-commencement-give-contractor-hired-by-tenant-the-right-to-lien-landlords-property#:~:text=construction%2C%E2%80%9D%20exposing%20it%20to%20the,10).
    Conversely, not taking these steps could allow a contractor to argue
    that your property is lienable (for instance, if your lease required
    the improvements or you funded them significantly). In summary: use
    the NOC to properly identify roles, but rely on §713.10 for the
    actual protection of your fee interest.

  - **Communicate and Coordinate with Lenders:** If a construction loan
    is involved, coordinate with the lender to avoid NOC mishaps.
    Typically, a lender will want the NOC recorded at closing (often
    they handle it). Ensure that either you or the lender actually
    follows through immediately. *Napolitano* reminds us that a lender
    who drops the ball may be on the hook, but as the owner, you still
    want to avoid the fallout. If you refinance or add a new mortgage
    mid-construction, **do not record the new mortgage until** either
    (a) any existing NOC is terminated (if the project really ended), or
    (b) at least concurrently record a new NOC. Recording a mortgage in
    the gap between an old NOC and a new one could give lienors a
    priority edge. In *Edwin Taylor*, the lender could have protected
    itself by requiring the earlier NOC to be terminated before funding
    – consider that strategy if you’re stepping into a project
    mid-stream[caselaw.findlaw.com](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/court/fl-district-court-of-appeal/2071581.html#:~:text=the%20lienor%20to%20properly%20mail,4).

**For Contractors, Subcontractors, and Suppliers:**

  - **Obtain and Review the NOC:** At the project’s start, **insist on
    receiving a copy of the recorded NOC**. Many savvy subcontractors
    won’t even start work until there’s a posted NOC on-site or a copy
    from the owner. The NOC gives you the *legal names and addresses*
    you need for your Notices to Owner and eventual Claim of
    Lien[caselaw.findlaw.com](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/court/fl-district-court-of-appeal/2071581.html#:~:text=owner%20by%20providing%20a%20trigger,is%20to%20receive%20copies%20of).
    As soon as you get the NOC, verify a few things: is the property
    description accurate and does it match where you’re working? Who is
    listed as owner – is it the party who hired your customer, or
    someone else (e.g., a landlord or a different entity)? Any surprises
    here should prompt questions. For instance, if you contracted with a
    tenant, and the NOC shows the landlord as owner but also a “lien
    prohibition” note, you know to be cautious (perhaps consult an
    attorney about your lien rights). **If no NOC is provided or
    visible**, check the public records yourself. If indeed no NOC was
    recorded, be aware that the clock for your Notice to Owner is still
    ticking (it’s 45 days from first work, irrespective of NOC). You
    will need to rely on other sources (building permit info, property
    appraiser) for owner details. Document your search efforts – should
    a dispute arise, courts will look favorably on a lienor who tried
    diligently to get the right
    info[acis-api.flcourts.gov](https://acis-api.flcourts.gov/courts/8f454fb9-4c7f-43df-856b-ab373e71c27f/cms/case/86ec4c23-2d4f-483b-a90f-e8d1c0dd327b/docketentrydocuments/2eebded0-ac17-44f6-b7b0-e4970dae5111#:~:text=Appraiser%E2%80%99s%20office%2C%20identified%20himself%20as,title%20search%20to%20file%2036)[caselaw.findlaw.com](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/court/fl-district-court-of-appeal/2071581.html#:~:text=relied%20on%20the%20information%20contained,which%20the%20owner%20took%20title).

  - **Serve Notices to Owner Timely and Correctly:** NOC or no NOC, as a
    subcontractor or supplier, **you must serve a Notice to Owner
    (NTO)** within 45 days of first furnishing labor/materials (the
    sooner the better) per §713.06(2)(a). If an NOC exists, use the
    exact **name and address listed for the owner** (and any designated
    alternate) when sending your
    NTO[caselaw.findlaw.com](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/court/fl-district-court-of-appeal/2071581.html#:~:text=owner%20by%20providing%20a%20trigger,is%20to%20receive%20copies%20of)[caselaw.findlaw.com](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/court/fl-district-court-of-appeal/2071581.html#:~:text=3,which%20the%20owner%20took%20title).
    Florida courts will hold the owner to that information – if the
    address is wrong in the NOC but you used it, the owner is estopped
    from claiming lack of
    notice[caselaw.findlaw.com](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/court/fl-district-court-of-appeal/2071581.html#:~:text=3,which%20the%20owner%20took%20title).
    If no NOC, send the NTO to the owner’s address as shown on the
    latest deed or tax
    rolls[caselaw.findlaw.com](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/court/fl-district-court-of-appeal/2071581.html#:~:text=relied%20on%20the%20information%20contained,which%20the%20owner%20took%20title).
    Keep proof of mailing (certified mail receipts, etc.). The **common
    mistakes** to avoid here are: (a) failing to send an NTO at all
    (which is fatal to your lien except for direct contractors), or (b)
    sending it to a **completely incorrect party or place** when correct
    info was available. Minor errors in the NTO (spelling, etc.) won’t
    invalidate it if delivered correctly and the owner isn’t misled,
    according to §713.06(2)(f) and cases like *Trump Endeavor 12 LLC v.
    Fernich, Inc.*, 216 So.3d 704 (Fla. 3d DCA
    2017)[caselaw.findlaw.com](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/court/fl-district-court-of-appeal/2071581.html#:~:text=%28Fla,%282013).
    But sending it to “123 Wrong Street” because you didn’t check the
    NOC or records is not excusable. In short, use the NOC as your
    guide; if none, use public records diligently – this preserves your
    lien rights even if the owner tries to claim technical foul.

  - **Monitor NOC Expiration and Payments:** If the project lasts close
    to a year or more, be mindful of the NOC’s expiration. An owner may
    need to extend it; if they don’t, any payments they make after
    expiration might be deemed improper. This matters to you because if
    an owner unknowingly makes an improper payment (say, pays the
    contractor after NOC expired), and that money doesn’t trickle down
    to you, you still have lien rights and the owner may end up paying
    twice[syfert.com](https://syfert.com/florida/statutes/0713.13.html#:~:text=WARNING%20TO%20OWNER%3A%20ANY%20PAYMENTS,RECORDING%20YOUR%20NOTICE%20OF%20COMMENCEMENT).
    While this is the owner’s problem in one sense, it can complicate
    your collection. A practical tip: if you notice the NOC on file is
    about to expire and you’re still not paid in full, send a polite
    reminder to the owner and contractor. This might spur them to issue
    joint checks or handle payouts more cautiously, knowing the “proper
    payment” safeguards are fading. It also signals that you’re
    sophisticated and aware of your rights.

  - **Leverage NOC Information:** The NOC can alert you to useful
    things: **Is there a payment bond?** If yes, a copy should be
    attached[syfert.com](https://syfert.com/florida/statutes/0713.13.html#:~:text=,limits%20for%20serving%20any%20required).
    If a bond is present, you’ll need to send a Notice of Nonpayment and
    perhaps claim against the bond instead of a lien. **Who is the
    lender?** The NOC lists any construction lender – knowing the lender
    means you can send them a copy of your NTO as well (not required by
    law, but often prudent). Lenders will pressure the owner/contractor
    to resolve your claim to avoid liens that could prime their
    mortgage. Also, if progress payments stall, you might reach out to
    the lender (draw requests often require proof of downstream
    payments). All this info comes from the NOC, so treat it as a
    roadmap for collection
    strategy[caselaw.findlaw.com](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/court/fl-district-court-of-appeal/2071581.html#:~:text=owner%20by%20providing%20a%20trigger,is%20to%20receive%20copies%20of).

  - **Substantial Compliance and Good Faith:** If you ever find yourself
    in a grey area – for example, you discover the NOC had a typo in the
    owner’s name after you already sent the NTO to that name – don’t
    panic. Florida law, as shown above, protects lienors who make
    **good-faith efforts**. The courts have explicitly said they won’t
    punish a lienor for an owner’s mistake that caused detrimental
    reliance[acis-api.flcourts.gov](https://acis-api.flcourts.gov/courts/8f454fb9-4c7f-43df-856b-ab373e71c27f/cms/case/86ec4c23-2d4f-483b-a90f-e8d1c0dd327b/docketentrydocuments/2eebded0-ac17-44f6-b7b0-e4970dae5111#:~:text=Appraiser%E2%80%99s%20office%2C%20identified%20himself%20as,title%20search%20to%20file%2036)[caselaw.findlaw.com](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/court/fl-district-court-of-appeal/2071581.html#:~:text=3,which%20the%20owner%20took%20title).
    The key is to be **able to show your good faith**. Document
    everything: when you checked for an NOC, where you sent notices, any
    returned mail, conversations with the contractor or owner about
    correct addresses, etc. This evidence can be the difference between
    a judge finding you “substantially complied” versus not. Conversely,
    avoid bad-faith moves – e.g., don’t intentionally send notice to a
    wrong address to play games. That would undercut you. Florida’s lien
    law has some flexibility, but it assumes lienors are trying to
    follow the rules, not flout them.

  - **Stay Alert to NOC Changes:** An owner can file an **Amended NOC**
    or a **Notice of Termination** of NOC. If you see one recorded or
    receive one, read it carefully. An amended NOC might change the
    designated person to receive notices or other info. A Notice of
    Termination, if valid, can cut off the relation-back effect 30 days
    after recording, so any liens recorded after that date attach based
    on actual recording date, not the original NOC date (see §713.132).
    If you get a Notice of Termination and you’re unpaid, you may need
    to record your Claim of Lien **sooner than later** (before the
    termination’s effective date) to preserve the earlier
    priority[flsenate.gov](https://www.flsenate.gov/Session/Bill/2023/797/Analyses/h0797a.CJS.PDF#:~:text=1%20A%20lien%20is%20a,S)[flsenate.gov](https://www.flsenate.gov/Session/Bill/2023/797/Analyses/h0797a.CJS.PDF#:~:text=Inc,direct%20contracts%20for%20%242500%20or).
    Owners can’t terminate an NOC early unless certain conditions are
    met (like all lienors being paid or
    consent)[flsenate.gov](https://www.flsenate.gov/Session/Bill/2023/797/Analyses/h0797a.CJS.PDF#:~:text=10%20o%20Surety11%20on%20a,12),
    but if they do and it’s facially valid, act accordingly and
    investigate if it was proper.

By adhering to these recommendations, owners can **avoid common
pitfalls** that lead to litigation over NOC compliance, and
contractors/lienors can better **protect their right to get paid**. The
Florida courts have shown they will enforce the spirit of the law –
rewarding those who follow the proper steps and not allowing
opportunistic avoidance of obligations. Ultimately, a properly handled
Notice of Commencement (from both the owner’s and contractor’s
standpoint) sets the stage for a smoother construction project, where
everyone knows the procedural ground rules and their rights are
preserved under Chapter 713. Compliance may require some extra up-front
attention, but as the cases above demonstrate, the cost of
non-compliance (or even perceived non-compliance) can be far higher in
the end.
