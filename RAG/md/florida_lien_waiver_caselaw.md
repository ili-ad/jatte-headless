# Florida’s Statutory Lien Waiver Forms in Court Decisions

## Timeliness & Conditional-Payment Language

Florida law prohibits waiving lien rights **in advance** of work being
performed (no pre-work waivers). A lienor can waive rights only for
labor or materials **already furnished**, not for future
work[codes.findlaw.com](https://codes.findlaw.com/fl/title-xl-real-and-personal-property/fl-st-sect-713-20/#:~:text=,made%20in%20advance%20is%20unenforceable).
In practice, this means each waiver should cover only the period of work
actually paid for. Courts emphasize that a partial (progress) release
should waive lien claims **only to the extent of payment received**. A
common error is signing a waiver through the current date even though
payment covers only earlier work – effectively giving up lien rights for
unpaid work performed after the billing cutoff. Unless the lienor can
prove mutual mistake or misconduct by the payor to reform the document,
such overbroad waivers are enforced as written.

Florida’s statutory forms do **not** themselves state any condition on
payment, so a signed form is, by default, an **unconditional** waiver of
lien rights for the specified period or final
payment[codes.findlaw.com](https://codes.findlaw.com/fl/title-xl-real-and-personal-property/fl-st-sect-713-20/#:~:text=WAIVER%20AND%20RELEASE%20OF%20LIEN,UPON%20PROGRESS%20PAYMENT)[codes.findlaw.com](https://codes.findlaw.com/fl/title-xl-real-and-personal-property/fl-st-sect-713-20/#:~:text=WAIVER%20AND%20RELEASE%20OF%20LIEN,UPON%20FINAL%20PAYMENT).
To protect themselves when payment is not yet assured (e.g. if paid by
check), lienors should add **conditional language**. The statute
expressly allows a lienor who executes a waiver “in exchange for a
check” to **condition the waiver on the check
clearing**[codes.findlaw.com](https://codes.findlaw.com/fl/title-xl-real-and-personal-property/fl-st-sect-713-20/#:~:text=,5).
Courts have approved adding such conditions. For example, in *Klein
Development v. Ellis K. Phelps & Co.* a subcontractor faxed a signed
lien release in exchange for a check that later bounced; the
subcontractor never delivered the original release and testified it
intended the faxed copy to be conditional until actual
payment[caselaw.findlaw.com](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/fl-district-court-of-appeal/1165642.html#:~:text=In%20addition%20to%20Morin%27s%20affidavit%2C,waiting%20for%20Cadenhead%27s%20check%20to)[caselaw.findlaw.com](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/fl-district-court-of-appeal/1165642.html#:~:text=clear,Phelps%27%20intent%20was%20not%20contradicted).
The court held the lien was not waived – noting the owner had **not
relied** by paying out any funds – and accepted that the release was not
intended to be binding until
payment[caselaw.findlaw.com](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/fl-district-court-of-appeal/1165642.html#:~:text=Klein%27s%20actions%20also%20show%20that,the%20letter%2C%20Klein%20was%20conducting)[caselaw.findlaw.com](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/fl-district-court-of-appeal/1165642.html#:~:text=Klein%20argues%20on%20appeal%20that,reliance%20on%20the%20release%20of).
This outcome illustrates that a waiver given **before** payment can be
deemed ineffective if the parties understood it to be conditional and
the owner did not act in reliance. However, this is the exception rather
than the rule. Generally, to avoid litigation over intent, lienors
should **explicitly write** any conditions (e.g. “This release is
conditioned upon actual receipt of payment”) into the waiver document.
Such conditional modifications are permitted by Florida law and do not
violate the “substantially similar” form requirement.

It’s important to note that a lienor’s contract with a contractor can
affect this dynamic. If the subcontract explicitly **requires
unconditional releases** in exchange for progress payments, the lienor
may be contractually bound to furnish an unconditional waiver before
getting paid. In *Team Land Development, Inc. v. Anzac Contractors,
Inc.*, a subcontractor’s right to payment was conditioned on providing
an unconditional final lien release (the statutory final
form)[law.justia.com](https://law.justia.com/cases/florida/third-district-court-of-appeal/2003/3d02-2904.html#:~:text=FLETCHER%2C%20J,2).
The court enforced that requirement, treating the delivery of a proper
final waiver as a **condition precedent** to
payment[law.justia.com](https://law.justia.com/cases/florida/third-district-court-of-appeal/2003/3d02-2904.html#:~:text=FLETCHER%2C%20J,2).
In practice, then, lienors should negotiate or be aware of such clauses:
while the statute allows conditional waivers, agreeing by contract to
give up that protection means the lienor must comply or risk nonpayment.
The key is timing and clarity – a lien waiver should only be executed
for the period that has been paid, and if payment is not secure, it
should be made conditional to preserve rights.

## Statutory-Form Deviations and Overbroad Waivers

Florida’s lien waiver forms are set forth in Fla. Stat. §713.20(4)
(progress payment) and §713.20(5) (final payment), and **must be
“substantially” in those forms** when a waiver is required as a
condition of
payment[codes.findlaw.comcodes.findlaw.com](https://codes.findlaw.com/fl/title-xl-real-and-personal-property/fl-st-sect-713-20/#:~:text=,in%20substantially%20the%20following%20form).
The legislative intent behind the 1996 adoption of mandatory forms was
to curb the practice of some owners and contractors demanding waivers
that included **extra obligations or waivers beyond lien
rights**[floridabar.org](https://www.floridabar.org/the-florida-bar-journal/the-1996-amendments-to-the-florida-construction-lien-law/#:~:text=The%20apparent%20intent%20of%20the,warranties%2C%20indemnification%2C%20and%20other%20issues).
Before then, owners often insisted on “lien waivers” that also released
other claims (contract rights, warranties, indemnities, etc.) as a
condition of
payment[floridabar.org](https://www.floridabar.org/the-florida-bar-journal/the-1996-amendments-to-the-florida-construction-lien-law/#:~:text=The%20apparent%20intent%20of%20the,warranties%2C%20indemnification%2C%20and%20other%20issues).
Now, under §713.20(6), a lienor cannot be *forced* to sign a waiver form
that diverges from the statutory
text[codes.findlaw.com](https://codes.findlaw.com/fl/title-xl-real-and-personal-property/fl-st-sect-713-20/#:~:text=,5).
In other words, an owner or contractor may request only the statutory
“Waiver and Release of Lien Upon Progress Payment” or “...Final
Payment” language (or a form substantially similar). If they demand a
broader or different form, they violate the statute.

Importantly, however, if a lienor **voluntarily signs a nonconforming
form**, it is enforceable according to its
terms[codes.findlaw.com](https://codes.findlaw.com/fl/title-xl-real-and-personal-property/fl-st-sect-713-20/#:~:text=,lien%20waiver%20or%20lien%20release).
Section 713.20(8) provides that even a waiver not substantially similar
to the statutory forms will be given effect as a contract. Thus, Florida
courts treat a signed lien waiver like any other contract: the parties
are free to agree to modifications, and once signed, the waiver will
generally be enforced strictly. The downside for lienors is that
overbroad language or deviations often operate against the lienor’s
interests. For example, waiver forms that inadvertently waive lien
rights for “all work through the date of this release” can **extinguish
rights for unpaid work** unless exceptions are carved out. The Florida
courts have consistently held that an unambiguous release **means what
it says** – courts will not rewrite a clear release to save a lienor who
mistakenly gave up more rights than intended. In *Frank Maio General
Contractor, Inc. v. Consolidated Electric Supply, Inc.* a supplier’s
release was so broad that it barred the supplier’s claim, even though
the supplier argued it only meant to release some invoices. The Fourth
DCA flatly rejected any attempt to avoid the **unambiguous terms of the
release**, noting a unilateral mistake by the lienor is not grounds to
undo the release. Likewise, in *Spectrum Interiors, Inc. v. Exterior
Walls, Inc.*, a subcontractor’s execution of **partial lien waivers up
to certain dates** was held to assign its claims to the extent of those
dates, stripping the sub of standing to recover for work performed
before those dates. The waiver forms in *Spectrum Interiors* were signed
under oath stating the sub had been paid in full through a date – the
court enforced them and reversed a judgment for the sub, since any
damages prior to the cutoff were waived. These cases underscore that
**courts do not inquire into intent** when a release’s language is
clear. Any **ambiguity** in a waiver is construed *against* the lienor
as the drafting party, further emphasizing the need for precise language
(citing *First Atlantic Bldg. Corp. v. Neubauer Constr. Co.*, a release
dispute where ambiguity was resolved against the subcontractor).

Because of §713.20(2)’s ban on advance waivers, any attempt to waive
lien rights for future work is invalid by
law[codes.findlaw.com](https://codes.findlaw.com/fl/title-xl-real-and-personal-property/fl-st-sect-713-20/#:~:text=,made%20in%20advance%20is%20unenforceable).
But overly broad **“through-date” clauses** can come close to waiving
future work if misused. For example, if payment is intended to cover
work through July 31, the waiver should list that date – not the later
date of signature – and should **exclude any work not paid** (common
exclusions are retainage, pending change orders, or specifically
described claims). The statutory **progress payment form** fortunately
includes a line: “This waiver and release does not cover any retention
or labor, services, or materials furnished after the date
specified”[codes.findlaw.com](https://codes.findlaw.com/fl/title-xl-real-and-personal-property/fl-st-sect-713-20/#:~:text=The%20undersigned%20lienor%2C%20in%20consideration,to%20the%20following%20property).
That automatically protects retainage and future work *if the form is
properly filled out with a cutoff date*. A drafting error would be to
alter or omit that sentence; a lienor who signs a custom form *without*
that retention savings clause or with an inappropriate date might
inadvertently waive retainage. In sum, Florida decisions teach that
lienors must be meticulous: use the statutory forms or *truly* similar
language, and if using a different form, **preserve your rights in
writing** at the time of signing (by limiting the scope to the payment
received, and explicitly excepting any unpaid items). Owners and
contractors, on the other hand, have an interest in broad waivers – but
they cannot *compel* a broadened form by
statute[codes.findlaw.com](https://codes.findlaw.com/fl/title-xl-real-and-personal-property/fl-st-sect-713-20/#:~:text=,5).
Their best protection is to stick to the statutory text (which is
balanced to protect both sides) or obtain separate documents for any
additional warranties or indemnities outside the lien law context.

## Owner/Lender Reliance & the “Proper-Payment” Defense

Florida’s Construction Lien Law is designed so that an owner who
**properly pays** for a project will not have to pay for the same work
twice[floridabar.org](https://www.floridabar.org/the-florida-bar-journal/the-1996-amendments-to-the-florida-construction-lien-law/#:~:text=contract%20price,21)[floridabar.org](https://www.floridabar.org/the-florida-bar-journal/the-1996-amendments-to-the-florida-construction-lien-law/#:~:text=%C2%A7713,22).
The “proper payment” defense essentially means that if the owner pays
the general contractor and obtains lien waivers/releases from all
lienors who have served a Notice to Owner (or who are listed in the
contractor’s final affidavit) for the work covered, the owner’s property
is protected from those lienors’
claims[floridabar.org](https://www.floridabar.org/the-florida-bar-journal/the-1996-amendments-to-the-florida-construction-lien-law/#:~:text=contract%20price,21)[floridabar.org](https://www.floridabar.org/the-florida-bar-journal/the-1996-amendments-to-the-florida-construction-lien-law/#:~:text=Thus%2C%20in%20order%20to%20avoid,waivers%20or%20releases%20will%20be).
In practice, owners and construction lenders **require signed waivers**
for each progress payment precisely to invoke this protection. Florida
courts have long upheld that an owner may rely on such releases to bar
later lien claims. For instance, in *Gulf Stream Lumber Co. v. Lathrop*
(Fla. 2d DCA 1959), an owner who paid the contractor in reliance on
subcontractors’ lien releases was shielded from those subs’ liens – the
subs were estopped because the owner had parted with funds on the
strength of the
releases[caselaw.findlaw.com](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/fl-district-court-of-appeal/1165642.html#:~:text=Klein%20argues%20on%20appeal%20that,reliance%20on%20the%20release%20of)[caselaw.findlaw.com](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/fl-district-court-of-appeal/1165642.html#:~:text=subcontractors%27%20releases%20of%20lien,Phelps%20released%20its%20lien%20rights).
This principle was earlier recognized by the Florida Supreme Court in
*Lehman v. Snyder* (1955) (also cited in *Klein*) as a fundamental
fairness rule: an owner who **acts in good faith**, paying the contract
price out to the contractor while obtaining the required waivers, is
entitled to finality and will not be forced to pay again to subs or
suppliers who have waived their lien rights.

However, owner reliance must be **actual and reasonable**. The *Klein*
case demonstrates that mere possession of a signed waiver is not enough
if the owner didn’t *do* anything in reliance. In *Klein*, the developer
(owner) received a faxed lien release from the sub-subcontractor but
then *withheld payment* to the contractor due to an unrelated
dispute[caselaw.findlaw.com](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/fl-district-court-of-appeal/1165642.html#:~:text=Phelps%20performed%20its%20obligations%20under,When%20Cadenhead%20refused%20to%20make)[caselaw.findlaw.com](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/fl-district-court-of-appeal/1165642.html#:~:text=Klein%20argues%20on%20appeal%20that,reliance%20on%20the%20release%20of).
Because no money was disbursed in reliance on the sub-sub’s release, the
court found the owner could not claim any equitable benefit from the
release[caselaw.findlaw.com](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/fl-district-court-of-appeal/1165642.html#:~:text=cases%20that%20purport%20to%20stand,case%2C%20Klein%27s%20mere%20possession%20of).
In fact, the owner’s own conduct showed it **didn’t treat the fax as
final** – two weeks later the owner demanded original signed releases
(including from that sub-sub), implicitly recognizing the fax copy was
insufficient[caselaw.findlaw.com](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/fl-district-court-of-appeal/1165642.html#:~:text=Klein%27s%20actions%20also%20show%20that,the%20letter%2C%20Klein%20was%20conducting).
Under those facts, the court allowed the sub-subcontractor’s lien, since
the owner had not been misled into any payment by the premature
release[caselaw.findlaw.com](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/fl-district-court-of-appeal/1165642.html#:~:text=subcontractors%27%20releases%20of%20lien,Phelps%20released%20its%20lien%20rights).
The takeaway is that a waiver can be invalidated for lack of true intent
*and* lack of reliance, but owners generally avoid this scenario by
**insisting on original, unconditional waivers before releasing funds**.
Owners and lenders should ensure that the waiver documents they receive
are executed by the correct party and in the required form (notarization
is not required by statute, but many use it as an extra safeguard). A
forged or fraudulent lien waiver would not bind the true lienor, so
prudent owners verify the authenticity of each release. If an owner
unknowingly pays based on a forged waiver, the innocent lienor can still
claim a lien and the owner may be forced to pay again – hence, verifying
signatures and using project payment management systems (like requiring
lienors to sign waivers in person or via e-signature systems) can
prevent costly mistakes.

For construction lenders, lien waivers are equally critical. Lenders
financing projects often require the owner and contractor to furnish
interim lien releases and a final release from all lienors as a
condition of funding draws or final payments. This protects the
**priority of the construction mortgage**. One illustrative scenario
arose in *Stock Building Supply of Fla., Inc. v. Soares Da Costa Constr.
Servs., LLC*, 76 So. 3d 313 (Fla. 3d DCA 2011). There, an owner
terminated the original contractor and needed new financing to complete
the project. The court noted it was necessary for the owner to file a
statutory Notice of Termination of the original Notice of Commencement –
which required that all lienors be paid in full – **before** the new
lender’s mortgage would have first
priority[jimersonfirm.com](https://www.jimersonfirm.com/blog/2021/02/top-five-construction-lien-law/#:~:text=Because%20construction%20liens%20relate%20back,when%20it%20subsequently%20recorded%20the)[jimersonfirm.com](https://www.jimersonfirm.com/blog/2021/02/top-five-construction-lien-law/#:~:text=has%20priority,%E2%80%9D).
In practical terms, that meant the owner had to obtain final lien
releases (or payoff all liens) from everyone on the job to date. Thus,
final payment waivers serve not only to protect owners from direct
liability, but also to clear title for refinancing or sale of the
property. An owner who makes proper use of lien waivers can raise the
defense that all lienors have been paid (or waived liens) up to the
amount of the contract, per Fla. Stat. §713.06(3). Conversely, if an
owner fails to secure the required waivers, they **lose the statutory
shield** and may have to pay subcontractors or suppliers despite having
paid the general contractor. This is the classic “double payment”
nightmare that the lien law’s notice and waiver structure is designed to
prevent. The case law thus strongly incentivizes owners to *strictly
comply* with the waiver-and-payment process: those who do so “will never
have to pay twice” for the same
work[floridabar.org](https://www.floridabar.org/the-florida-bar-journal/the-1996-amendments-to-the-florida-construction-lien-law/#:~:text=contract%20price,21)[floridabar.org](https://www.floridabar.org/the-florida-bar-journal/the-1996-amendments-to-the-florida-construction-lien-law/#:~:text=Thus%2C%20in%20order%20to%20avoid,waivers%20or%20releases%20will%20be),
but those who take shortcuts risk unextinguished liens.

## Fraud, Mistake, or Duress in Waiver Execution

Florida courts treat a lien waiver like any contract – it can be voided
or reformed in cases of fraud, duress, or mutual mistake, but such
relief is rare. The bar for **duress** is high: the typical pressure of
“sign this release or you won’t get paid” is not duress in the legal
sense, because the contractor/owner has a lawful right to insist on a
lien release upon payment. Only improper threats or deception (e.g. a
contractor misrepresenting that a release is “just a receipt” or
physically coercing a signature) would qualify as duress or fraud to
invalidate a waiver. While published Florida decisions on duress in lien
waivers are scant, courts would likely apply general contract principles
– a release signed under **fraudulent pretenses** or **forgery** is
void. An example of fraud would be if a contractor presented a lienor
with a document represented as a partial release for one invoice, but it
was actually an unconditional final waiver of all rights, and the lienor
was tricked into signing. In such a case, the lienor could argue the
waiver is void due to fraud. Similarly, if a release was **materially
altered** after the lienor signed it, it would not be enforceable. These
scenarios, however, are the exception.

More commonly, lienors seek relief by claiming **mistake** – for
instance, that they did not intend the release to cover certain work or
sums. Florida courts have been largely unsympathetic to unilateral
mistake claims. In *Frank Maio* (discussed above), the supplier’s claim
of mistakenly signing too broad a release was not a valid excuse. The
courts require a **mutual** mistake (both parties shared a wrong
assumption of fact) or a unilateral mistake induced by the other party’s
inequitable conduct to set aside a clear waiver. The Florida Bar has
noted that a lienor would need to convince a court there was *either* a
mutual mistake about the release’s scope *or* a unilateral mistake
coupled with misconduct by the payor, in order to reform a release. That
is a difficult showing. One avenue lienors have attempted is to claim an
“understanding” that the release was conditional or not effective until
payment. The *Klein* case demonstrates that evidence of such an
understanding can prevail, but the facts were compelling: the lienor’s
internal practice of holding the original until the check cleared, the
affidavit testifying to conditional intent, and the owner’s own lack of
reliance all aligned to show the faxed waiver should not
count[caselaw.findlaw.com](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/fl-district-court-of-appeal/1165642.html#:~:text=In%20addition%20to%20Morin%27s%20affidavit%2C,waiting%20for%20Cadenhead%27s%20check%20to)[caselaw.findlaw.com](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/fl-district-court-of-appeal/1165642.html#:~:text=clear,Phelps%27%20intent%20was%20not%20contradicted).
Notably, *Klein* was decided at summary judgment, suggesting no factual
dispute that the release was meant to be conditional. If the owner in
*Klein* had paid the contractor after receiving the fax, the outcome
likely would have been different – the owner’s reliance would trump the
lienor’s secret intent. Florida law thus tends to favor the party who
**relies on the face of a document** over one who signs it but later
protests it didn’t mean what it said.

In sum, a lien waiver procured by actual fraud or signed due to a mutual
mistake can be attacked in court, but absent such extraordinary
circumstances, the waiver is final. “Innocent” mistakes by a lienor –
like forgetting to exclude retainage or dating the waiver wrong – are
not grounds to invalidate the waiver after the fact. The practical
lesson for lienors is to **raise any objections or corrections before
signing**. Once a release is executed, any ambiguity will be resolved
against the lienor and any clear waiver of rights will be upheld. Courts
will not entertain testimony that “I didn’t really mean to waive that”
if the document is unambiguous. Thus, except in cases of proven fraud or
mutual mistake, a signed lien waiver is treated as a **conclusive
extinguishment** of the lien rights stated in it.

## Final-Payment vs. Progress-Payment Waiver Distinctions

Both statutory forms – the interim **Waiver and Release of Lien Upon
Progress Payment** and the **Waiver and Release of Lien Upon Final
Payment** – function similarly, and courts have generally construed them
under the same principles, with attention to their different scope.
**Progress payment waivers** are **partial**; they waive lien rights
only for work done up to a specified date (the form provides a blank to
fill in the “through” date) in exchange for a stated
sum[codes.findlaw.com](https://codes.findlaw.com/fl/title-xl-real-and-personal-property/fl-st-sect-713-20/#:~:text=WAIVER%20AND%20RELEASE%20OF%20LIEN,UPON%20PROGRESS%20PAYMENT).
Any labor or materials furnished after that date remain lienable, and
retainage is explicitly not covered by the
release[codes.findlaw.com](https://codes.findlaw.com/fl/title-xl-real-and-personal-property/fl-st-sect-713-20/#:~:text=The%20undersigned%20lienor%2C%20in%20consideration,to%20the%20following%20property).
In contrast, a **final payment waiver** typically signifies that the
project is complete (for that lienor’s work) and all payments due have
been made. The final waiver form waives lien rights for **all labor,
services, or materials furnished to the job** in exchange for a final
payment
amount[codes.findlaw.com](https://codes.findlaw.com/fl/title-xl-real-and-personal-property/fl-st-sect-713-20/#:~:text=WAIVER%20AND%20RELEASE%20OF%20LIEN,UPON%20FINAL%20PAYMENT).
By its nature, a final release is intended to **extinguish any and all
lien rights on the project** for that lienor.

One key distinction in case law is that issues unique to final waivers
often involve timing of final payment and project completion. For
example, if a lienor signs a final waiver but then is asked to perform
additional punch-list work or corrective work, can they lien for that?
Generally, work performed *after* the date of a final release (or not
encompassed by the original contract) might give rise to a new lien
right, since the final release covers only what was furnished up to its
execution. As one court observed, a “final” lien release will not waive
lien rights for **work performed after its
execution**[lexology.com](https://www.lexology.com/library/detail.aspx?g=65445bb8-8e7d-42b0-89de-36bc0c38ddc4#:~:text=upon%20completion%20of%20a%20project%2C,3d%202011)[lexology.com](https://www.lexology.com/library/detail.aspx?g=65445bb8-8e7d-42b0-89de-36bc0c38ddc4#:~:text=prohibit%20you%20from%20any%20claim,3d%202011).
In *Stock Building Supply v. Soares Da Costa*, the Third DCA noted that
even though a “final payment” was made and releases obtained, subsequent
work under a new contract or after a Notice of Termination could be
separately liened because it was outside the scope of the original
contract covered by the final
waivers[lexology.com](https://www.lexology.com/library/detail.aspx?g=65445bb8-8e7d-42b0-89de-36bc0c38ddc4#:~:text=upon%20completion%20of%20a%20project%2C,3d%202011)[lexology.com](https://www.lexology.com/library/detail.aspx?g=65445bb8-8e7d-42b0-89de-36bc0c38ddc4#:~:text=prohibit%20you%20from%20any%20claim,3d%202011).
Thus, final really means final *as to that contract or scope of work*.
Lienors and owners should be careful that, at project close-out, the
final payment truly accounts for all work done. If there are pending
change orders or disputes, a lienor should either **exclude those items
in the final release** or refuse to sign a “final” release until they
are resolved. Otherwise, a final waiver will bar any lien claim for
those unresolved items.

Another area where final vs. progress waivers diverge is **delivery and
condition precedents**. Owners and lenders often will not release the
final payment (including retainage) until a final lien waiver is
delivered – effectively making the final waiver a condition precedent to
the owner’s performance. Florida courts have upheld this practice: as
noted earlier, *Team Land Development v. Anzac Contractors* enforced a
contract term requiring the subcontractor to submit the executed
final-payment waiver (the §713.20(5) form) before the owner had to pay
the
balance[law.justia.com](https://law.justia.com/cases/florida/third-district-court-of-appeal/2003/3d02-2904.html#:~:text=FLETCHER%2C%20J,2).
Progress payment waivers, on the other hand, occur throughout the job
and typically every progress payment will be conditioned on the
subcontractor (and its subs) giving a release for that payment’s worth
of work. Both forms are **unconditional** on their face (unless modified
as discussed), so once signed they immediately waive the lienor’s rights
for the period covered.

In case law, there are fewer unique disputes about the **final** form
itself — mostly, a final waiver marks the end of the lien rights, and
any lien filed after a true final waiver will be summarily extinguished
(absent fraud or mistake). Courts do not hesitate to cancel a lien if a
final waiver was signed: for example, if a subcontractor signs the final
release upon receiving a final check, and then the check clears, any
later claim of lien by that subcontractor is invalid because the lien
was waived. By contrast, disputes over **progress waivers** have
generated more case law, often about whether the waiver’s effective date
was intended to cover certain work or not (like in *Spectrum Interiors*
or the hypothetical in the Florida Bar Journal article). But the legal
principles applied are the same. Both types of waivers are construed
strictly according to their text. Both can be conditioned on actual
payment (despite the text) if explicitly
stated[codes.findlaw.com](https://codes.findlaw.com/fl/title-xl-real-and-personal-property/fl-st-sect-713-20/#:~:text=,5).
And both are protected by the statutory rule that no one can force a
lienor to sign more expansive language than the forms
provide[codes.findlaw.com](https://codes.findlaw.com/fl/title-xl-real-and-personal-property/fl-st-sect-713-20/#:~:text=,5).

In summary, Florida courts view the **progress payment waiver** as a
routine partial relinquishment of lien rights – a snapshot in time –
whereas the **final payment waiver** is the definitive closure of lien
rights on a job. Lienors should treat a “final” release with utmost
gravity: it is effectively an acknowledgment that they have been paid in
full for the project. If that’s not the case, the document should not be
signed (or should be qualified with any exceptions in writing).
Conversely, owners and lenders rely on final waivers to safely disburse
final funds and confidently conclude that no liens can thereafter
encumber the property. By understanding how courts have interpreted
these waivers, all parties can act to preserve their rights: lienors
will carefully tailor or condition waivers to match what they’ve been
paid, and owners will insist on proper waivers (in the statutory format)
to guard against any lingering lien exposure. The consistent theme from
Florida decisions is that a statutory lien waiver, when executed in
connection with a payment, is **powerful and conclusive** – it can
preserve an owner’s proper-payment defense and extinguish a lienor’s
rights completely, so it must be handled with precision and good faith
by everyone involved.
