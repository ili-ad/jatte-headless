# Florida’s Notice to Owner (NTO) Requirement – Key Cases and Compliance Principles

## Background: Purpose and Strict Nature of NTO Requirements

Under Florida’s Construction Lien Law, lienors not in direct privity
with the owner (e.g. subcontractors, sub-subs, material suppliers)
**must serve a Notice to Owner (NTO)** as a prerequisite to preserving
lien
rights[law.justia.com](https://law.justia.com/cases/florida/supreme-court/1992/76925-0.html#:~:text=As%20a%20prerequisite%20to%20perfecting,recognized%2C%20the%20notice%20requirement%20is).
The NTO alerts the property owner that these parties are working on the
project and may look to the owner for payment if
unpaid[law.justia.com](https://law.justia.com/cases/florida/supreme-court/1992/76925-0.html#:~:text=713,double%20payment%2C%20such%20notice%20will).
This protects owners from “double payment” – paying the general
contractor and then being forced to pay subs/suppliers who remain
unpaid[law.justia.com](https://law.justia.com/cases/florida/supreme-court/1992/76925-0.html#:~:text=713,double%20payment%2C%20such%20notice%20will).
Florida courts emphasize that the lien law is strictly construed, and
**strict compliance** with statutory NTO requirements is “indispensable”
for a valid
lien[law.justia.com](https://law.justia.com/cases/florida/supreme-court/1991/76234-0.html#:~:text=Electric%20an%20electrical%20contractor%20filed,Home)[caselaw.findlaw.com](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/court/fl-district-court-of-appeal/1165504.html#:~:text=argument%20that%20its%20facsimile%20transmission,was%20fatal%20to%20establishing%20its).
Although minor, non-misleading errors might be forgiven, substantial
noncompliance (missed deadlines, missing statutory warning language,
improper service, etc.) will *defeat lien
rights*[caselaw.findlaw.com](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/court/fl-district-court-of-appeal/1165504.html#:~:text=argument%20that%20its%20facsimile%20transmission,was%20fatal%20to%20establishing%20its)[buildersnotice.com](https://www.buildersnotice.com/blog-post/top-mistakes-to-avoid-when-filing-a-notice-to-owner#:~:text=recipients).
The cases below illustrate how Florida courts have enforced the NTO
rules (focusing on the current statute post-2023, which retains the
strict amendments made in 1991). For each case, we note the parties
involved, the NTO issue, the outcome, the court’s reasoning, common
compliance errors, and the impact on lien enforceability.

## Aetna Cas. & Sur. Co. v. Buck (Fla. Supreme Court 1992) – **Common-Identity Privity Excuses Notice**

**Parties:** A subcontractor (Buck, d/b/a American Metal Fabricating)
contracted with a general contractor (Pappalardo Construction) to supply
materials for a condo project. Pappalardo was also the principal of the
owning joint venture (Bay Colony Land Co.), effectively making the owner
and contractor the *same person*. Buck recorded a lien after nonpayment
but **never served an NTO on the
owner**[law.justia.com](https://law.justia.com/cases/florida/supreme-court/1992/76925-0.html#:~:text=Vincent%20J,withheld%20payment%20for%20the%20materials)[law.justia.com](https://law.justia.com/cases/florida/supreme-court/1992/76925-0.html#:~:text=owns%20the%20property%20under%20construction,as%20owner%20of%20the%20property).  
**Issue:** Whether the NTO requirement can be waived because the owner
and contractor were essentially the same entity (common identity),
putting the sub in “privity” with the owner despite no direct contract.
Normally, a sub not in privity must serve an
NTO[law.justia.com](https://law.justia.com/cases/florida/supreme-court/1992/76925-0.html#:~:text=As%20a%20prerequisite%20to%20perfecting,recognized%2C%20the%20notice%20requirement%20is).
Buck argued that common ownership/identity established privity, excusing
the notice.  
**Outcome:** The Florida Supreme Court agreed with the subcontractor. It
held that **when “the owner and contractor share a common identity,” the
NTO is not
required**[law.justia.com](https://law.justia.com/cases/florida/supreme-court/1992/76925-0.html#:~:text=Although%20we%20agree%20with%20the,work%20or%20when%20the%20owner).
This created a narrow exception: privity (and thus no notice) exists
either when the owner has expressly assumed the sub’s obligation *or*
when, for all practical purposes, the owner and contractor are the same
entity[law.justia.com](https://law.justia.com/cases/florida/supreme-court/1992/76925-0.html#:~:text=Although%20we%20agree%20with%20the,work%20or%20when%20the%20owner).
Here, because Pappalardo was on both sides (owner’s principal and
contractor), Buck was deemed in privity and his lien was enforceable
despite no NTO. The Court noted that in such cases the owner is
obviously aware of the sub and its claim (since the “owner” is the
contractor)[law.justia.com](https://law.justia.com/cases/florida/supreme-court/1992/76925-0.html#:~:text=common%20identity%20exists%20between%20the,either%20situation%2C%20notice%20is%20not).  
**Reasoning:** Although mechanics’ liens are strictly statutory and
usually require notice, the Court extended the concept of privity to
common-identity situations, citing earlier cases that had reached
similar results in owner-as-contractor
scenarios[law.justia.com](https://law.justia.com/cases/florida/supreme-court/1992/76925-0.html#:~:text=Although%20we%20agree%20with%20the,work%20or%20when%20the%20owner).
The purpose of the NTO – warning owners of unknown
subs[law.justia.com](https://law.justia.com/cases/florida/supreme-court/1992/76925-0.html#:~:text=713,double%20payment%2C%20such%20notice%20will)
– wasn’t defeated here because the owner knew of and controlled the
subcontract. *However, a concurring justice cautioned that this “bails
out” a subcontractor who forgot to file an NTO and creates uncertainty
about how much common ownership is
enough[law.justia.com](https://law.justia.com/cases/florida/supreme-court/1992/76925-0.html#:~:text=In%20an%20effort%20to%20bail,common%20identity).*
In practice, this exception is **limited** to situations with nearly
complete overlap between owner and contractor.  
**Compliance Pitfall:** The subcontractor’s failure to serve any NTO
would normally be fatal. Buck only avoided disaster because of the
uncommon fact of identical ownership. Relying on an “identity of
interest” is risky – absent a clear unity between owner and contractor,
lack of notice remains a complete defense to a lien.  
**Impact on Lien:** Buck’s lien was deemed valid and enforceable (and
the owner’s payment bond surety was liable) despite no NTO, due to the
privity-by-identity
finding[law.justia.com](https://law.justia.com/cases/florida/supreme-court/1992/76925-0.html#:~:text=Although%20we%20agree%20with%20the,work%20or%20when%20the%20owner).
This case underscores that **direct contract (or its equivalent) = no
NTO needed**, but it is *not* a general leniency rule. Courts still
require notice in typical subcontractor situations where the owner is a
separate entity. Practitioners should err on the side of serving an NTO
unless truly in privity; do not assume an owner’s knowledge or common
management will excuse compliance.

## Superfos Constr. (U.S.), Inc. v. **Hajoca Corp.** (Fla. 2d DCA 1998) – **Timing: Final Payment vs. 45-Day Rule**

**Parties:** A subcontractor (“Superfos”) vs. a property owner
(“Hajoca”). Superfos started work late in the project and did not
serve its NTO until after the job was done. The owner **had already
issued final payment to the general contractor** (on July 1, 1996)
before Superfos served the NTO on July 10,
1996[mkpalaw.com](https://www.mkpalaw.com/articles-forms/articles/notice-to-owner-issues/#:~:text=concern%20will%20be%20the%20timing,2d%20DCA%201998)[mkpalaw.com](https://www.mkpalaw.com/articles-forms/articles/notice-to-owner-issues/#:~:text=Hajoca%20was%20the%20project%20owner,Owner%20had%20made%20final%20payment).
The NTO was within 45 days of Superfos’s first work (so timely in that
sense) but came *after* the owner paid the contractor in full.  
**Issue:** Florida’s statute requires NTO service *within 45 days of
commencing work* **and** *“before the date of the owner’s disbursement
of final payment* **after** *the contractor’s final
affidavit”*[mkpalaw.com](https://www.mkpalaw.com/articles-forms/articles/notice-to-owner-issues/#:~:text=Florida%20Statutes%2C%20section%20713,relevant%20part%20that%20an%20NTO).
What if the owner makes a final payment without obtaining the
contractor’s final payment affidavit as required by law? Is an NTO
served after such a payment effective? The owner argued Superfos’s
notice was untimely since it came after he paid the GC. The sub argued
the owner’s payment was improper (no affidavit), so the statutory cutoff
hadn’t actually been
triggered[mkpalaw.com](https://www.mkpalaw.com/articles-forms/articles/notice-to-owner-issues/#:~:text=Hajoca%20was%20the%20project%20owner,Owner%20had%20made%20final%20payment)[mkpalaw.com](https://www.mkpalaw.com/articles-forms/articles/notice-to-owner-issues/#:~:text=Owner%20had%20made%20final%20payment,general%20contractor%E2%80%99s%20final%20payment%20affidavit).  
**Outcome:** The Second DCA sided with the subcontractor, **holding the
NTO was valid**. Because the owner failed to get the contractor’s
final-payment affidavit before disbursing final payment, the statutory
protection for the owner did not
apply[mkpalaw.com](https://www.mkpalaw.com/articles-forms/articles/notice-to-owner-issues/#:~:text=Owner%20had%20made%20final%20payment,general%20contractor%E2%80%99s%20final%20payment%20affidavit).
In fact, the statute punishes an owner who pays the contractor
prematurely: the owner’s property remains subject to liens of which the
owner has notice if final payment was not properly conditioned on the
affidavit[mkpalaw.com](https://www.mkpalaw.com/articles-forms/articles/notice-to-owner-issues/#:~:text=When%20final%20payment%20has%20become,furnishes%20his%20or%20her%20affidavit).
The appellate court famously stated that by making final payment without
the required affidavit, the owner **“subjected itself to the claim of
any subcontractor who…filed a notice to owner within forty-five days of
commencing
work.”**[mkpalaw.com](https://www.mkpalaw.com/articles-forms/articles/notice-to-owner-issues/#:~:text=Unfortunately%2C%20the%20trial%20court%20bought,had%20an%20enforceable%20construction%20lien)
Superfos’s NTO (served 9 days after final pay) was within 45 days of its
start, so it counted – and the lien was
enforceable[mkpalaw.com](https://www.mkpalaw.com/articles-forms/articles/notice-to-owner-issues/#:~:text=Unfortunately%2C%20the%20trial%20court%20bought,had%20an%20enforceable%20construction%20lien).  
**Reasoning:** The court read the statute’s plain language: an NTO must
come before final payment *after* the contractor’s
affidavit[mkpalaw.com](https://www.mkpalaw.com/articles-forms/articles/notice-to-owner-issues/#:~:text=Florida%20Statutes%2C%20section%20713,relevant%20part%20that%20an%20NTO).
Here the “final payment” contemplated by the statute never legally
occurred because the affidavit wasn’t given. The owner essentially
jumped the gun, so the door was still open for lien notices. The
decision aligns with the policy that owners who don’t follow the
affidavit procedure can be exposed to lien
claims[mkpalaw.com](https://www.mkpalaw.com/articles-forms/articles/notice-to-owner-issues/#:~:text=When%20final%20payment%20has%20become,furnishes%20his%20or%20her%20affidavit)[mkpalaw.com](https://www.mkpalaw.com/articles-forms/articles/notice-to-owner-issues/#:~:text=Unfortunately%2C%20the%20trial%20court%20bought,had%20an%20enforceable%20construction%20lien).  
**Compliance Pitfall:** The subcontractor narrowly avoided losing its
lien rights because of the owner’s mistake. **Common error:** serving a
late NTO on a nearly finished project. If the owner had properly
obtained the affidavit and disbursed final payment, any notice served
afterward would be invalid even if within 45 days. Subs who start work
near the end of a project risk the owner’s final payment cutting off
their lien rights. The lesson is to **serve the NTO as early as
possible**, ideally immediately upon first providing labor/materials –
waiting even a few weeks can be dangerous if the job is ending. As the
attorneys observed, “the closer to project completion, the greater the
likelihood” final payment (with affidavit) will occur; thus, **“it is
wise to serve your NTO as soon as
possible.”**[mkpalaw.com](https://www.mkpalaw.com/articles-forms/articles/notice-to-owner-issues/#:~:text=subcontractors%20and%20suppliers%20rarely%20know,NTO%20as%20soon%20as%20possible)  
**Impact on Lien:** Superfos’s lien was upheld as valid. Importantly,
however, this case does *not* create an exception to the 45-day deadline
– it simply enforces the existing statutory caveat about final payments.
Current law still mandates **service within 45 days** and *before* a
proper final payment. Practitioners should not view this as a loophole,
but rather a reminder: if you’re late in giving notice, an owner who
follows the rules will defeat your lien. Always meet the 45-day window,
and assume final payment could happen on day 46.

## Mirror & Shower Door Products, Inc. v. **Seabridge, Inc.** (Fla. 4th DCA 1993) – **Statutory Content: Missing Warning Language**

**Parties:** A material supplier (Mirror & Shower Door) vs. a property
owner (Seabridge). The supplier did send a Notice to Owner, but **the
written notice did not include certain warning language required by
statute**[floridabar.org](https://www.floridabar.org/the-florida-bar-journal/subcontractors-suppliers-beware-form-over-substance/#:~:text=Mirror%20and%20Shower%20Door%20Products%2C,holding%20that).
(Florida Statutes §713.06(2)(c) provides a form for the NTO with
specific warnings to the owner about potential liens, such as the
“Important Information for…” heading and cautionary statements.) In
this case, the supplier’s form omitted some of the mandated warnings –
the supplier apparently **gave those warnings orally** instead of in
writing[floridabar.org](https://www.floridabar.org/the-florida-bar-journal/subcontractors-suppliers-beware-form-over-substance/#:~:text=Mirror%20and%20Shower%20Door%20Products%2C,holding%20that).  
**Issue:** Does an NTO that *omits* required statutory warnings (even if
the owner might have been told the information verbally) satisfy the
“substantial compliance” standard? The 1991 amendments to §713.06 made
the statutory form mandatory (“must substantially follow” the form,
including its warnings). The question was whether the supplier’s notice,
lacking the printed warnings, was fatally defective despite otherwise
notifying the owner of the supplier’s involvement.  
**Outcome:** The Fourth DCA refused to enforce the lien. It held that
**failing to include the written statutory warnings on the NTO form is a
critical defect**, and the supplier’s lien was invalid as a
result[floridabar.org](https://www.floridabar.org/the-florida-bar-journal/subcontractors-suppliers-beware-form-over-substance/#:~:text=Mirror%20and%20Shower%20Door%20Products%2C,holding%20that)[caselaw.findlaw.com](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/court/fl-district-court-of-appeal/1165504.html#:~:text=argument%20that%20its%20facsimile%20transmission,was%20fatal%20to%20establishing%20its).
The court noted that only *“immaterial errors or omissions in the form”*
of an NTO might be excused – and missing the required warning text is
*material*, not a trivial
error[caselaw.findlaw.com](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/court/fl-district-court-of-appeal/1165504.html#:~:text=argument%20that%20its%20facsimile%20transmission,was%20fatal%20to%20establishing%20its).
Simply telling the owner the information was not enough; the *notice
document itself* must contain the statutory language.  
**Reasoning:** After the 1991 changes, the law demands that an NTO
**include the exact protective warnings outlined in §713.06(2)(c)**. The
court treated the omission of those warnings as a failure to
substantially comply with the form. It cited the Legislature’s “plain
intent” that a **written notice including the statutory warnings is a
prerequisite** to a
lien[caselaw.findlaw.com](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/court/fl-district-court-of-appeal/1165504.html#:~:text=argument%20that%20its%20facsimile%20transmission,was%20fatal%20to%20establishing%20its).
In other words, the form’s content is important to ensure owners are
formally and clearly warned of potential
liens[caselaw.findlaw.com](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/court/fl-district-court-of-appeal/1165504.html#:~:text=argument%20that%20its%20facsimile%20transmission,was%20fatal%20to%20establishing%20its).
Without the printed warnings, the notice does not fulfill its statutory
purpose, so the lien cannot be
perfected[caselaw.findlaw.com](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/court/fl-district-court-of-appeal/1165504.html#:~:text=argument%20that%20its%20facsimile%20transmission,was%20fatal%20to%20establishing%20its).
(This decision was later echoed and reinforced by other courts, as we’ll
see below.)  
**Compliance Pitfall:** The supplier used a nonconforming notice form –
**omitting mandatory language** – which is a common mistake when parties
draft their own notices or use unapproved templates. The result was loss
of lien rights. This case underscores that **substantial compliance has
limits**: you cannot omit or substantially alter the statutorily
required content. Only truly minor deviations (e.g. a slight typo in the
owner’s name that doesn’t mislead) might be forgiven, but not leaving
out the core
warnings[caselaw.findlaw.com](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/court/fl-district-court-of-appeal/1165504.html#:~:text=argument%20that%20its%20facsimile%20transmission,was%20fatal%20to%20establishing%20its).  
**Impact on Lien:** Mirror & Shower Door’s lien was dismissed for
noncompliance. The decision made clear that as of the current statute,
**an NTO must** *substantially follow the statutory form***, including
all cautionary statements**. This remains the law today. Practically,
suppliers and subcontractors should use the **exact statutory NTO form**
or a very faithful reproduction of it. Any “creative” changes risk being
deemed material omissions. Courts have little tolerance for form defects
after the 1991 amendment from “may use form” to **“must use”** the
statutory
form[floridabar.org](https://www.floridabar.org/the-florida-bar-journal/subcontractors-suppliers-beware-form-over-substance/#:~:text=1991,word%20%E2%80%9Cprecisely%E2%80%9D%20in%20its%20place).

## Allstar Building Materials, Ltd. v. **Kronauer** (Fla. 5th DCA 1998) – **Strict Form Compliance (Font Size & Additional Text)**

**Parties:** A material supplier (Allstar Building Materials) vs. a
property owner (Kronauer, presumably a homeowner). Allstar did serve an
NTO, but they **used a modified notice form**. Notably, *some of the
warning text was not in the same format* – **the supplier had reduced
the font size of certain portions**, and **omitted the title “Important
Information for Your Protection”** that appears in the statutory
form[floridabar.org](https://www.floridabar.org/the-florida-bar-journal/subcontractors-suppliers-beware-form-over-substance/#:~:text=Even%20more%20interesting%20is%20the,warnings%20required%20in%20order%20to).
Additionally, the supplier’s notice was addressed to both the owner and
the general contractor. (It’s unclear if the form combined notices or
just included the GC in the salutation, but the court pointed out it was
sent to the contractor *as well as* the
owner[floridabar.org](https://www.floridabar.org/the-florida-bar-journal/subcontractors-suppliers-beware-form-over-substance/#:~:text=this%20case%2C%20the%20court%20refused,%E2%80%9D7).)  
**Issue:** Whether these deviations from the statutory NTO form –
smaller print and missing heading language (and possibly extra
addressing) – meant the notice failed to “substantially follow” the
required form. Essentially, how strictly must a lienor adhere to the
exact layout and wording of §713.06(2)(c)?  
**Outcome:** The Fifth DCA ruled the lien unenforceable, affirming
dismissal of Allstar’s foreclosure suit. **Even seemingly minor changes
to the form were not tolerated.** The court noted three specific
defects: “(1) the notice was sent to the contractor, as well as the
owner; (2) some of the statutory warning text was printed in
reduced-size type; and (3) the notice omitted the boldfaced heading
‘Important Information for Your Protection’” that the statute
includes[floridabar.org](https://www.floridabar.org/the-florida-bar-journal/subcontractors-suppliers-beware-form-over-substance/#:~:text=Even%20more%20interesting%20is%20the,warnings%20required%20in%20order%20to).
These alterations meant the notice did *not* substantially comply. The
court went so far as to suggest that **any form other than the one the
legislature provided is likely insufficient**, stating: *“the form
itself is important and… the form utilized by the legislature better
conveys the warnings required in order to justify a lien on the property
of one not in
privity.”*[floridabar.org](https://www.floridabar.org/the-florida-bar-journal/subcontractors-suppliers-beware-form-over-substance/#:~:text=this%20case%2C%20the%20court%20refused,%E2%80%9D7)
In short, Allstar should have used the exact statutory form – by
deviating, it lost its lien.  
**Reasoning:** The 5th DCA emphasized the legislative intent behind
prescribing a form. Because the construction lien law puts owners’
property at risk for debts they didn’t directly incur, the law requires
clear, conspicuous warnings to the owner. The statutory form was crafted
to ensure owners understand these rights (hence the bold heading and
specified font size). The court believed that altering those aspects
might dilute the effectiveness of the
warning[floridabar.org](https://www.floridabar.org/the-florida-bar-journal/subcontractors-suppliers-beware-form-over-substance/#:~:text=3,%E2%80%9D7).
The inclusion of the contractor as a recipient also wasn’t required by
the statute (though sending a copy to the contractor is common, the
primary notice must go to the owner). By strictly construing the form
requirement, the court reinforced that **form matters as much as
substance** under the lien law. This decision, along with *Seabridge*
and *Chapman* (below), signaled to practitioners that **virtually
literal compliance with the statutory NTO form is the safest
course**[floridabar.org](https://www.floridabar.org/the-florida-bar-journal/subcontractors-suppliers-beware-form-over-substance/#:~:text=this%20case%2C%20the%20court%20refused,%E2%80%9D7).  
**Compliance Pitfall:** Allstar’s mistakes were in the **format and
completeness of the notice**. Common errors of this kind include:
leaving out the exact title or any of the protective language, adding
extraneous content, or changing the font/print such that required text
is not as legible. After 1991, such errors are usually deemed
*material*. The court here did not even inquire whether the owner was
confused or prejudiced by the changes – it enforced the form requirement
rigidly[floridabar.org](https://www.floridabar.org/the-florida-bar-journal/subcontractors-suppliers-beware-form-over-substance/#:~:text=Even%20more%20interesting%20is%20the,warnings%20required%20in%20order%20to)[floridabar.org](https://www.floridabar.org/the-florida-bar-journal/subcontractors-suppliers-beware-form-over-substance/#:~:text=this%20case%2C%20the%20court%20refused,%E2%80%9D7).
The clear message is that lienors should **not “reinvent the
wheel”**[leagle.com](https://www.leagle.com/decision/19981340724so2d61611238#:~:text=Although%20there%20is%20perhaps%20a,reinvent%20the%20wheel).
Use the form provided in the statute (or a pre-approved form from
reliable sources) to avoid any challenge.  
**Impact on Lien:** Allstar’s lien was invalidated. *Kronauer* remains
good law, illustrating that current §713.06 will be enforced to the
letter on form/content issues. For modern practitioners, the takeaway
is: **don’t alter the notice’s mandated text or format**. Ensure the NTO
you serve is a clone of the statutory form (including the bold heading
and all warning paragraphs in at least the minimum font size). Even
cosmetic or well-intentioned adjustments can cost you your lien rights.

## Gulfside Properties Corp. v. **Chapman Corp.** (Fla. 1st DCA 1999) – **Complete Failure to Serve Proper NTO**

**Parties:** A subcontractor (Chapman) vs. a property owner/developer
(Gulfside Properties). Gulfside hired a general contractor (Willis
Construction) to build beach villas. Willis filed a Notice of
Commencement (NOC) naming Gulfside as owner but **Willis mistakenly
signed the NOC where the owner should sign**, rendering the NOC
technically
defective[caselaw.findlaw.com](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/court/fl-district-court-of-appeal/1165504.html#:~:text=Villas%2C%20entered%20into%20an%20agreement,posted%20on%20the%20job%20site)[caselaw.findlaw.com](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/court/fl-district-court-of-appeal/1165504.html#:~:text=immaterial%20errors%20and%20omissions%20in,judgment%20in%20favor%20of%20Gulfside).
Chapman, a subcontractor to Willis, **never served a Notice to Owner on
Gulfside at all**. Instead, Chapman sent its invoices and job proposal
to the general contractor (Willis) by fax, and later recorded a lien for
unpaid
amounts[caselaw.findlaw.com](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/court/fl-district-court-of-appeal/1165504.html#:~:text=materials%20for%20the%20project,could%20not%20be%20made%20subject)[caselaw.findlaw.com](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/court/fl-district-court-of-appeal/1165504.html#:~:text=Chapman%20recorded%20a%20construction%20lien,case%20on%20written%20briefs%20and).
Gulfside paid the GC in full (relying on lien waivers from the GC) and
was unaware Chapman was
unpaid[caselaw.findlaw.com](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/court/fl-district-court-of-appeal/1165504.html#:~:text=of%20%24906,23)[caselaw.findlaw.com](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/court/fl-district-court-of-appeal/1165504.html#:~:text=Gulfside%20with%20lien%20waivers%20representing,23).
When Chapman sued to foreclose the lien, Gulfside argued the lien was
invalid due to Chapman’s **failure to serve an
NTO**[caselaw.findlaw.com](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/court/fl-district-court-of-appeal/1165504.html#:~:text=On%20appeal%2C%20Gulfside%20argues%20that,of%20its%20claim%20of%20lien).
Chapman responded that the flawed NOC misled it about who the owner was,
or should excuse notice, and alternatively that its invoices to the GC
“amounted to” notice to the
owner[caselaw.findlaw.com](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/court/fl-district-court-of-appeal/1165504.html#:~:text=Chapman%20counters%20that%20section%20713,in%20Gulfside%27s%20notice%20of%20commencement).  
**Issue:** Whether a subcontractor’s **failure to serve any written NTO
on the owner** can be excused because the owner’s Notice of Commencement
was defectively signed or because the sub sent documents to the
contractor. In essence, can a sub avoid the statutory notice requirement
due to an owner/GC paperwork defect or by claiming the owner had
constructive knowledge via invoices? This case squarely tested the
absolute nature of the NTO requirement.  
**Outcome:** The First DCA emphatically ruled against the subcontractor
and **discharged the lien**. Chapman’s lack of a proper NTO was a
“complete defense” under the
statute[caselaw.findlaw.com](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/court/fl-district-court-of-appeal/1165504.html#:~:text=a%20complete%20defense%20to%20enforcement,failed%20to%20carry%20its%20burden)[caselaw.findlaw.com](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/court/fl-district-court-of-appeal/1165504.html#:~:text=immaterial%20errors%20and%20omissions%20in,judgment%20in%20favor%20of%20Gulfside).
The court held that *even a defective notice of commencement by the
owner does not waive the subcontractor’s obligation to serve the owner
with a Notice to
Owner*[caselaw.findlaw.com](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/court/fl-district-court-of-appeal/1165504.html#:~:text=MacIntyre%2C%20334%20So,failed%20to%20carry%20its%20burden).
It also dismissed the idea that faxing an invoice to the contractor
could substitute for the statutory notice to the
owner[caselaw.findlaw.com](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/court/fl-district-court-of-appeal/1165504.html#:~:text=notice%20to%20owner).
Chapman “failed to carry its burden” to show
compliance[caselaw.findlaw.com](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/court/fl-district-court-of-appeal/1165504.html#:~:text=a%20complete%20defense%20to%20enforcement,failed%20to%20carry%20its%20burden).
The court reiterated the **“plain language”** of §713.06: a written NTO
containing the statutory warnings *must* be served on the owner as a
prerequisite to a
lien[caselaw.findlaw.com](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/court/fl-district-court-of-appeal/1165504.html#:~:text=argument%20that%20its%20facsimile%20transmission,was%20fatal%20to%20establishing%20its).
Chapman served no such notice (invoices are not in the required form or
directed to the owner), so its lien was invalid. The opinion echoed
*Mirror & Shower Door*, noting only *immaterial* errors have ever been
excused and that Chapman’s total lack of a written, warning-containing
notice was obviously not
immaterial[caselaw.findlaw.com](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/court/fl-district-court-of-appeal/1165504.html#:~:text=argument%20that%20its%20facsimile%20transmission,was%20fatal%20to%20establishing%20its).  
**Reasoning:** The court was unwilling to create any exception that
isn’t in the statute. The NOC’s defect (owner not signing) did not
appear in §713.06 as an excuse for not serving notice. (Indeed, the
court footnoted that while the NOC was invalid under §713.13, all the
correct owner info *was* in it, so Chapman knew or could easily find the
true owner’s
identity[caselaw.findlaw.com](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/court/fl-district-court-of-appeal/1165504.html#:~:text=1,310%2C%20Laws%20of%20Fla).
Nothing prevented Chapman from serving Gulfside.) The decision
underscores that **actual or constructive knowledge of the owner is
irrelevant if the formal notice wasn’t given** – Florida courts do not
accept “substantial compliance” in the form of “well, the owner should
have known I was on the job.” Only the statutory notice, properly
served, counts. The court also reinforced that the NTO must contain the
statutory warnings: Chapman’s faxes lacked them, which was *“fatal to
establishing its
lien.”*[caselaw.findlaw.com](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/court/fl-district-court-of-appeal/1165504.html#:~:text=indicates%20the%20legislature%27s%20intent%20that,reasons%2C%20we%20hold%20that%20the).
In short, **no written notice = no lien**, end of story.  
**Compliance Pitfall:** Chapman’s case reflects two common compliance
failures: (1) **not serving any NTO at all**, often due to confusion or
oversight, and (2) **serving the wrong party or address** (Chapman sent
paperwork to the GC instead of the owner). The court made clear that a
**defective NOC or misinformation does not excuse the lienor’s duty** –
the onus remains on the lienor to figure out the correct owner and serve
them. Florida law provides tools (the recorded NOC, property records,
etc.) to identify owners, and lienors proceed at their peril if they
don’t use them. Another implicit point: sending invoices or other
documents is *not* the same as an NTO. Only a document that
substantially complies with §713.06(2)(c) and is delivered per
§713.06(2)(a)/(d) counts. Chapman’s failure demonstrates that even
equitable arguments (like “the owner misled me” or “the owner wasn’t
harmed because they knew of my claim”) usually **fall on deaf ears** in
lien
cases[caselaw.findlaw.com](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/court/fl-district-court-of-appeal/1165504.html#:~:text=argument%20that%20its%20facsimile%20transmission,was%20fatal%20to%20establishing%20its)[caselaw.findlaw.com](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/court/fl-district-court-of-appeal/1165504.html#:~:text=immaterial%20errors%20and%20omissions%20in,judgment%20in%20favor%20of%20Gulfside).
The statutes are unforgiving on this point.  
**Impact on Lien:** Chapman’s lien was void and Gulfside won
outright[caselaw.findlaw.com](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/court/fl-district-court-of-appeal/1165504.html#:~:text=immaterial%20errors%20and%20omissions%20in,judgment%20in%20favor%20of%20Gulfside)[caselaw.findlaw.com](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/court/fl-district-court-of-appeal/1165504.html#:~:text=lien%20against%20the%20subject%20property,judgment%20in%20favor%20of%20Gulfside).
This case (and others like it) confirm the current law’s stance:
**Strict compliance is king.** An owner can defeat a lien if the lienor
cannot prove timely and proper service of a compliant NTO. For
practitioners, *Gulfside v. Chapman* is often cited as a cautionary
tale: never assume you have an excuse for not serving the notice. If
there’s any doubt about owner information (e.g. the NOC is incomplete or
signed by the wrong person), **take additional steps to find the owner’s
correct name and address** (corporate records, deed records, etc.) and
serve the notice within 45 days. Courts will expect you to do so.

## Consistent Judicial Interpretations of §713.06 NTO Compliance

The above cases, spanning Florida’s Supreme Court and multiple District
Courts of Appeal, reveal a *consistent judicial approach*: **Florida
courts strictly enforce the NTO requirements** in the current
construction lien statute. Key interpretations include:

  - **Timeliness is Absolute:** The 45-day deadline to serve the NTO
    (from first furnishing labor/material) is a hard stop, not extended
    for weekends or
    holidays[buildersnotice.com](https://www.buildersnotice.com/blog-post/top-mistakes-to-avoid-when-filing-a-notice-to-owner#:~:text=Don%27t%20be%20fooled%20into%20thinking,when%20you%20send%20an%20invoice).
    A notice served even one day late will doom the
    lien[buildersnotice.com](https://www.buildersnotice.com/blog-post/top-mistakes-to-avoid-when-filing-a-notice-to-owner#:~:text=you%20furnish%20labor%20or%20materials,when%20you%20send%20an%20invoice).
    The statute explicitly mandates strict compliance with
    timing[floridabar.org](https://www.floridabar.org/the-florida-bar-journal/subcontractors-suppliers-beware-form-over-substance/#:~:text=1991,word%20%E2%80%9Cprecisely%E2%80%9D%20in%20its%20place),
    and courts do not craft exceptions (except the narrow final-payment
    scenario noted in *Superfos v. Hajoca*, which is built into the
    statute). In practice, this means *mailing* by the 45th day is not
    enough – the owner must receive the notice by day 45 under most
    interpretations. Lienors who miss the deadline **“lose their lien
    rights”**
    entirely[buildersnotice.com](https://www.buildersnotice.com/blog-post/top-mistakes-to-avoid-when-filing-a-notice-to-owner#:~:text=you%20furnish%20labor%20or%20materials,when%20you%20send%20an%20invoice).

  - **Proper Service and Delivery:** Florida law dictates acceptable
    service methods (e.g. certified or registered mail, overnight
    delivery, or hand delivery, per §713.18). Courts expect lienors to
    use these methods to ensure the owner actually receives notice. In
    one appellate case, serving the notice to the wrong address (or
    wrong party) was held a fatal
    error[caselaw.findlaw.com](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/court/fl-district-court-of-appeal/1165504.html#:~:text=notice%20to%20owner)[caselaw.findlaw.com](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/court/fl-district-court-of-appeal/1165504.html#:~:text=immaterial%20errors%20and%20omissions%20in,judgment%20in%20favor%20of%20Gulfside).
    Failing to serve *the correct statutory recipients* is equally
    fatal. **All required parties must get the NTO** – typically the
    property owner and, if a construction lender is listed in the Notice
    of Commencement, the lender as
    well[buildersnotice.com](https://www.buildersnotice.com/blog-post/top-mistakes-to-avoid-when-filing-a-notice-to-owner#:~:text=Imagine%20sending%20a%20crucial%20letter,intent%20to%20file%20a%20lien)[buildersnotice.com](https://www.buildersnotice.com/blog-post/top-mistakes-to-avoid-when-filing-a-notice-to-owner#:~:text=recipients).
    (The prime contractor should receive a copy too, though a failure to
    copy the contractor might not invalidate the lien against the owner
    if the owner received notice. Still, best practice: serve everyone
    listed on the NOC.) The overarching theme: the burden is on the
    lienor to get the notice into the owner’s hands in the authorized
    manner. An owner who genuinely never receives an NTO has a complete
    defense. Evidence of proper mailing (certified mail receipt, etc.)
    is crucial, because if an owner later claims lack of notice, the
    lienor must prove it was sent correctly.

  - **Statutory Content Must Be Included:** The NTO must **substantially
    follow the form prescribed in §713.06(2)(c)**. Florida courts have
    consistently held that the statutory warning language (such as the
    boldface heading and the explanations that this is not a lien but
    could become one, etc.) is a *mandatory component*. Cases like
    *Mirror & Shower Door*, *Kronauer*, and *Chapman* all declare that
    omitting the required warnings or altering them beyond recognition
    will invalidate the
    notice[caselaw.findlaw.com](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/court/fl-district-court-of-appeal/1165504.html#:~:text=argument%20that%20its%20facsimile%20transmission,was%20fatal%20to%20establishing%20its)[floridabar.org](https://www.floridabar.org/the-florida-bar-journal/subcontractors-suppliers-beware-form-over-substance/#:~:text=this%20case%2C%20the%20court%20refused,%E2%80%9D7).
    Only **immaterial, non-misleading errors** are excused. For
    instance, a minor typo in the owner’s name or a slight error in the
    legal description *might* be overlooked if it doesn’t mislead anyone
    and the statute’s savings clause
    applies[floridabar.org](https://www.floridabar.org/the-florida-bar-journal/subcontractors-suppliers-beware-form-over-substance/#:~:text=owner,to%20vacate%20the%20lien%2C%20stating)[floridabar.org](https://www.floridabar.org/the-florida-bar-journal/subcontractors-suppliers-beware-form-over-substance/#:~:text=In%20cases%20upholding%20the%20sufficiency,4).
    But any omission or change that could affect the owner’s
    understanding is not “immaterial.” The post-1991 case law trend is
    toward zero tolerance for deviation on the core content. In sum, the
    courts interpret “substantially similar” to mean **“nearly
    identical”** to the statutory form in substance and format. As one
    court bluntly put it, the legislative form is presumed to convey the
    warnings properly, so lienors shouldn’t
    deviate[floridabar.org](https://www.floridabar.org/the-florida-bar-journal/subcontractors-suppliers-beware-form-over-substance/#:~:text=this%20case%2C%20the%20court%20refused,%E2%80%9D7).

  - **No Notice = No Lien (except true privity):** If a lienor required
    to give notice fails to do so, the lien is invalid –
    period[caselaw.findlaw.com](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/court/fl-district-court-of-appeal/1165504.html#:~:text=immaterial%20errors%20and%20omissions%20in,judgment%20in%20favor%20of%20Gulfside).
    The only exception upheld by courts is when the lienor is considered
    already in privity with the owner, such as being directly contracted
    or the owner effectively being the contractor (common
    identity)[law.justia.com](https://law.justia.com/cases/florida/supreme-court/1992/76925-0.html#:~:text=Although%20we%20agree%20with%20the,work%20or%20when%20the%20owner).
    In all other situations, **actual knowledge by the owner is
    irrelevant** if the formal notice wasn’t served. Courts routinely
    reject arguments like “the owner knew we were on the job” or “the
    owner saw our trucks/materials, so they weren’t prejudiced.” The
    statutory scheme is intended to *formally notify* the owner that the
    lienor is looking to them for
    payment[law.justia.com](https://law.justia.com/cases/florida/supreme-court/1992/76925-0.html#:~:text=Boux%20v,4th%20DCA%201976).
    Thus, Florida decisions consistently hold that an owner’s knowledge
    or a contractor’s promise to inform the owner **do not excuse the
    lienor’s failure to comply**. For example, even where an owner’s
    agent (the GC) signed the notice of commencement incorrectly, the
    sub was not excused from serving the
    owner[caselaw.findlaw.com](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/court/fl-district-court-of-appeal/1165504.html#:~:text=There%20is%20no%20support%20in,a%20proper%20notice%20to%20owner).
    The only safe harbor is being in direct contract with the owner (or
    falling under the narrow common-identity rule). Note: Laborers are
    statutorily exempt from the NTO
    requirement[law.justia.com](https://law.justia.com/cases/florida/supreme-court/1992/76925-0.html#:~:text=As%20a%20prerequisite%20to%20perfecting,recognized%2C%20the%20notice%20requirement%20is),
    so a laborer’s lien isn’t defeated by lack of notice; but all other
    lienors (subcontractors, sub-subs, suppliers, equipment lessors,
    etc.) must serve one.

  - **Precedent and Statutory Interpretation:** Courts frequently cite
    earlier decisions to reinforce these points, creating a robust chain
    of precedent. *Stresscon v. Madiedo* (Fla. 1991) is often cited for
    the principle that **strict compliance is required unless the
    statute explicitly allows substantial
    compliance**[floridabar.org](https://www.floridabar.org/the-florida-bar-journal/subcontractors-suppliers-beware-form-over-substance/#:~:text=The%20fact%20that%20no%20prejudice,%28emphasis%20supplied%298)[floridabar.org](https://www.floridabar.org/the-florida-bar-journal/subcontractors-suppliers-beware-form-over-substance/#:~:text=substance%2C%20the%20Stresscon%20court%20noted,10).
    In the NTO context, §713.06 does allow “substantial” compliance
    *with content* so long as no one is adversely
    affected[floridabar.org](https://www.floridabar.org/the-florida-bar-journal/subcontractors-suppliers-beware-form-over-substance/#:~:text=%E2%80%9Csubstantially%E2%80%9D%20follow%20the%20statutory%20form,%E2%80%9D1)[floridabar.org](https://www.floridabar.org/the-florida-bar-journal/subcontractors-suppliers-beware-form-over-substance/#:~:text=Since%20both%20the%20notice%20to,%E2%80%9D%20The%20court),
    but the 1991 change from “may” to “must” use the statutory form
    heightened the
    strictness[floridabar.org](https://www.floridabar.org/the-florida-bar-journal/subcontractors-suppliers-beware-form-over-substance/#:~:text=1991,word%20%E2%80%9Cprecisely%E2%80%9D%20in%20its%20place).
    Consequently, post-1991 cases (Chapman, Kronauer, etc.) have hewed
    to the letter of the form. Courts also refer back to the purpose of
    the law – protecting owners from hidden lien claims – when
    interpreting ambiguities, invariably resolving doubts in favor of
    the owner’s protection (which translates to requiring clear, timely
    notice from
    lienors)[law.justia.com](https://law.justia.com/cases/florida/supreme-court/1992/76925-0.html#:~:text=713,double%20payment%2C%20such%20notice%20will)[caselaw.findlaw.com](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/court/fl-district-court-of-appeal/1165504.html#:~:text=argument%20that%20its%20facsimile%20transmission,was%20fatal%20to%20establishing%20its).
    The consistent thread is that Florida’s appellate courts want
    lienors to **follow the statute exactly**: meet the deadlines, use
    the proper form, and serve the right parties. Failure to do so is a
    self-inflicted forfeiture of lien rights, not something the courts
    will rescue.

## Practical Recommendations to Preserve Lien Rights under §713.06

For subcontractors, suppliers, and other lienors in Florida, the
following best practices are recommended to ensure compliance with the
Notice to Owner requirement and safeguard your lien rights:

  - **Serve the Notice Early – Don’t Wait:** Mark your calendar for the
    45-day deadline from your first work or delivery on the project, but
    plan to serve the NTO well *before* that. In fact, it’s wise to send
    the notice **immediately when you start work** (or even just before
    starting)[mkpalaw.com](https://www.mkpalaw.com/articles-forms/articles/notice-to-owner-issues/#:~:text=subcontractors%20and%20suppliers%20rarely%20know,NTO%20as%20soon%20as%20possible).
    This avoids any risk of missing the deadline or the owner making
    final payment before you notice. Remember that **all days count** –
    the 45-day clock runs through weekends and
    holidays[buildersnotice.com](https://www.buildersnotice.com/blog-post/top-mistakes-to-avoid-when-filing-a-notice-to-owner#:~:text=Don%27t%20be%20fooled%20into%20thinking,when%20you%20send%20an%20invoice).
    There is no grace period. If Day 45 falls on a Sunday, your notice
    still must be received by then. Late delivery (Day 46, etc.) means
    *no lien*. As one industry guide flatly warns, **“if you fail to
    file a Notice to Owner within the 45-day period, your right to file
    a lien
    vanishes.”**[buildersnotice.com](https://www.buildersnotice.com/blog-post/top-mistakes-to-avoid-when-filing-a-notice-to-owner#:~:text=you%20furnish%20labor%20or%20materials,when%20you%20send%20an%20invoice)

  - **Use the Statutory NTO Form (Don’t Deviate):** Obtain a copy of the
    exact Notice to Owner form as set out in Fla. Stat. §713.06(2)(c)
    (many Florida construction law resources and the statutes provide
    this form). **Use it verbatim.** Fill in the project-specific blanks
    (your name, description of work, property description, etc.) but do
    not omit or paraphrase the statutory text. The notice should
    prominently bear the title and warnings exactly as required. *Do not
    shrink or fine-print the warning language.* Courts have invalidated
    liens because the notice’s font was too small or the title was
    missing[floridabar.org](https://www.floridabar.org/the-florida-bar-journal/subcontractors-suppliers-beware-form-over-substance/#:~:text=Even%20more%20interesting%20is%20the,warnings%20required%20in%20order%20to).
    Keep the formatting similar (e.g., title in bold caps). While slight
    imperfections in non-critical details (like a minor spelling error)
    might be overlooked, there’s no benefit to testing that – the safest
    route is a *faithful reproduction* of the statutory form. By using
    the proper form, you ensure all required warnings (such as the
    statement that “THIS IS NOT A LIEN” and other protections) are
    conveyed as the law
    intends[floridabar.org](https://www.floridabar.org/the-florida-bar-journal/subcontractors-suppliers-beware-form-over-substance/#:~:text=this%20case%2C%20the%20court%20refused,%E2%80%9D7).

  - **Identify All Required Recipients and Correct Addresses:** *Before
    sending your NTO*, determine exactly who must receive it. This
    typically includes: **the owner** (as listed on the building permit
    or deed – if multiple owners, each should get a notice), **the
    general contractor** (to comply with §713.06(2)(a) which says give a
    copy to the contractor if you’re a sub), and **the construction
    lender** if one is identified in the Notice of Commencement. The
    Notice of Commencement is a valuable source – it is usually posted
    at the site or recorded in county records, and it provides the
    owner’s name and address, the contractor’s name, and any lender’s
    name/address[buildersnotice.com](https://www.buildersnotice.com/blog-post/top-mistakes-to-avoid-when-filing-a-notice-to-owner#:~:text=The%20Notice%20of%20Commencement%20is,NTO%20to%20all%20required%20recipients).
    Use that information. Make sure to address your notice exactly as
    those parties are named. If the NOC designates an owner’s agent to
    receive notices, send it there as well. Failing to send the NTO to
    **even one required party (owner, contractor, or listed lender)**
    can invalidate your notice and thus your
    lien[buildersnotice.com](https://www.buildersnotice.com/blog-post/top-mistakes-to-avoid-when-filing-a-notice-to-owner#:~:text=recipients).
    For example, if a bank financing the project is listed, send them a
    copy – owners have successfully argued a lien was invalid when a
    required notice to a lender was omitted. Bottom line: cast a **wide
    net** – it’s better to over-include recipients (where statute says
    to) than to miss one. Keep records of *who* you sent to and *where*.

  - **Serve via Certified Mail (and/or Other Statutory Methods) with
    Proof:** Florida’s lien law permits service of the NTO by several
    methods, but **Certified Mail with Return Receipt Requested is the
    gold
    standard**[buildersnotice.com](https://www.buildersnotice.com/blog-post/top-mistakes-to-avoid-when-filing-a-notice-to-owner#:~:text=Incorrectly%20Serving%20the%20Notice%20to,Owner).
    It provides a paper trail – you’ll have a mailing receipt and an
    electronic delivery confirmation or a signed green card showing when
    the owner (or their agent) received the notice. Commercial courier
    with tracking (e.g. FedEx) or actual hand delivery (with a signed
    acknowledgment) are also acceptable, but certified mail is most
    common. Always mail to the **correct address** – use the owner’s
    address from the NOC or their official address (and consider also
    sending to the property address if different). If mail is returned
    unclaimed, Florida law may deem the attempt sufficient if properly
    sent, but you might then try an alternate method or address to
    ensure actual notice (and document those efforts). The key is to be
    able to later **prove** in court that you served the notice within
    the 45 days and in accordance with §713.18. Keep copies of the NTO,
    certified mail receipts, tracking printouts, etc., for your records.
    If possible, do **not rely on regular mail or verbal notice** – they
    won’t satisfy the statute if contested.

  - **Double-Check Your Content and Details:** Before sending out the
    NTO, review it carefully. Verify that: (1) **Property identification
    is accurate** – use the legal description or at least the street
    address as required by the form, and ensure it’s the correct project
    (many liens are lost due to notices referencing the wrong lot or
    project, especially when contractors handle many jobs); (2)
    **Owner’s name is correct** – exactly as on record (if the owner
    is “XYZ Investments, LLC,” don’t just write “XYZ Investments” or a
    trade name); (3) **Your information is correct** – name and address
    of the lienor, general description of services or materials, and the
    party who hired you (contractor or subcontractor) should all be
    filled in; and (4) **All the statutory warning text is present** –
    including the headline and the body text that begins “WARNING TO
    OWNER…” etc. Any omission or obvious error could give the owner an
    argument that your notice was deficient. While the statute allows
    “substantial” compliance, remember the courts’ interpretation is
    very strict on the warnings. Thus, an NTO that, say, mistakenly
    leaves out the contractor’s name or has a minor typo in the legal
    description *might* still be enforceable if no one was misled, but
    these are avoidable errors. It’s best not to rely on the mercy of a
    court. Essentially, treat the NTO with the same precision as you
    would a legal contract or a recorded lien – because it is just as
    critical to your rights.

  - **Be Mindful of Final Payment Affidavits:** If you’re joining a
    project late or it’s nearing completion, be extremely prompt with
    your NTO. Under §713.06(2)(a), if the owner has received a
    contractor’s final payment affidavit and paid the contractor
    *before* you serve your notice, your lien rights are cut off. You
    usually won’t know exactly when the contractor submits that final
    affidavit, so **assume your real deadline is much earlier than 45
    days if the job is finishing**. An owner who follows the proper
    procedure (final affidavit + final payment) will have an ironclad
    defense against any NTO served afterward. The *Superfos* case
    demonstrated a lucky scenario for the sub when the owner skipped the
    affidavit[mkpalaw.com](https://www.mkpalaw.com/articles-forms/articles/notice-to-owner-issues/#:~:text=Owner%20had%20made%20final%20payment,general%20contractor%E2%80%99s%20final%20payment%20affidavit)[mkpalaw.com](https://www.mkpalaw.com/articles-forms/articles/notice-to-owner-issues/#:~:text=Unfortunately%2C%20the%20trial%20court%20bought,had%20an%20enforceable%20construction%20lien),
    but you cannot count on owner error. Thus, if you start work near
    the end of a project, **serve your NTO immediately** – even *before*
    you actually begin work if possible (the statute allows service
    before commencing). This preserves your rights in case the project
    wraps up quickly. Likewise, if you find out the contractor has been
    terminated or is in trouble financially, treat that as a red flag to
    get your notice in right away – final payments and changes can
    happen fast in such situations.

  - **Laborers and Prime Contractors:** If you’re a laborer (individual
    performing labor who is not an architect, engineer, or other
    professional), note that the law exempts you from the NTO
    requirement – your lien rights are not contingent on giving notice.
    Still, it may be a good practice for laborer crews to notify the
    owner informally of their presence to avoid issues, but legally it’s
    not required. If you’re a **general contractor in direct privity
    with the owner**, you also do *not* give an NTO (the contract itself
    serves as notice). However, general contractors must be mindful of
    other requirements (like the contractor’s final payment affidavit)
    but that’s outside the NTO scope. For everyone else, assume you must
    send an NTO.

  - **Document and Preserve Evidence:** Finally, treat the Notice to
    Owner process as a formal, significant step – because courts will.
    Keep meticulous evidence that you prepared and sent the notice
    correctly and on time. If a dispute arises, you want to be able to
    lay out proof of compliance (e.g., a copy of the NTO, the certified
    mail tracking showing delivered on Day X, etc.). Many lienors use
    professional notice services or software to manage this; that’s a
    worthwhile investment if you handle many projects. Courts have no
    sympathy for spurious “the notice got lost” arguments without proof
    – the burden is on you as the lien claimant to show you did it
    right[caselaw.findlaw.com](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/court/fl-district-court-of-appeal/1165504.html#:~:text=a%20complete%20defense%20to%20enforcement,failed%20to%20carry%20its%20burden).

By following these practices – **timely service, correct form, proper
recipients, and thorough documentation** – subcontractors, suppliers,
and other lien claimants can confidently preserve their lien rights.
Florida’s courts have made it clear that those who **diligently comply
with §713.06** will be protected, but those who slip up on notice will
find their liens invalid. In summary, current law and case decisions
send a simple message: **follow the lien statute to the letter**. If you
do so, you avoid the common pitfalls (late notices, faulty notices, or
no notices) that have tripped up others, and you position yourself to
successfully enforce your construction lien when
needed[caselaw.findlaw.com](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/court/fl-district-court-of-appeal/1165504.html#:~:text=immaterial%20errors%20and%20omissions%20in,judgment%20in%20favor%20of%20Gulfside)[buildersnotice.com](https://www.buildersnotice.com/blog-post/top-mistakes-to-avoid-when-filing-a-notice-to-owner#:~:text=you%20furnish%20labor%20or%20materials,when%20you%20send%20an%20invoice).
Remember that a mechanics’ lien can be a powerful tool – but only if
you’ve preserved it through meticulous NTO compliance. As the Florida
courts have shown time and again, an once of prevention (serving a
proper NTO) is worth far more than a pound of cure in this area of law.
