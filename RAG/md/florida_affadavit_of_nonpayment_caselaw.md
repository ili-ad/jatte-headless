## Understanding Florida’s Affidavit of Nonpayment Mechanism

**What is an “Affidavit of Nonpayment”?** In Florida’s construction lien
context, this term refers to a sworn statement a lienor can record to
*nullify a previously delivered conditional lien waiver* when the
promised payment was never actually received. By filing such an
affidavit and giving notice to interested parties, an unpaid contractor
or supplier aims to *reinstill* its lien rights despite having issued a
lien release conditioned on payment. Florida’s lien statute does not
explicitly label this document an “affidavit of nonpayment,” but the
concept is recognized in practice through case law and contract
conditions[cobbgonzalez.com](https://www.cobbgonzalez.com/construction-lien-releases-in-florida/#:~:text=prohibited,of%20its%20lien%20rights%20if)[caselaw.findlaw.com](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/court/fl-district-court-of-appeal/1581927.html#:~:text=The%20undersigned%20lienor%2C%20in%20consideration,Biscayne%20Boulevard%2C%20Miami%2C%20Florida).
Essentially, if a lienor conditionally waived its lien in exchange for a
check or promised payment, and that payment fails, the lienor may “undo”
the waiver by timely recording an affidavit attesting nonpayment –
thereby reviving the lien.

**Statutory Basis and Timing:** Florida law *permits* conditional lien
waivers and releases. Section 713.20(7), Florida Statutes, expressly
authorizes a lienor who takes a check as payment to **condition** the
waiver on the check
clearing[cobbgonzalez.com](https://www.cobbgonzalez.com/construction-lien-releases-in-florida/#:~:text=prohibited,of%20its%20lien%20rights%20if).
Furthermore, §713.20(8) provides that any waiver not in the exact
statutory form “is enforceable in accordance with its terms,” meaning
parties can agree to custom conditional
language[cobbgonzalez.com](https://www.cobbgonzalez.com/construction-lien-releases-in-florida/#:~:text=%C2%A7%20713,20%288).
Unlike states such as Georgia (which require filing an affidavit of
nonpayment within 60–90 days of the waiver), Florida’s statute does
**not** impose a specific deadline by which such an affidavit must be
recorded[ww3.gcpay.com](https://ww3.gcpay.com/blog/lien-waiver-requirements-by-state/#:~:text=State,the%20waiver%20from%20becoming).
**However, Florida courts have emphasized that lienors must act within
the overall lien deadlines and** *without undue delay*. In practice,
this means the affidavit of nonpayment (or equivalent notice) should be
recorded *promptly* after the payment default and, in any event, before
the 90-day window to record a Claim of Lien
expires[gray-robinson.com](https://www.gray-robinson.com/docs/Georgia%20Lien%20Law%20-%202015.PPTX#:~:text=Is%20that%20it%3F%20No%2C%20Affidavit,furnishing%20if%20still%20not%20paid).
If the lienor misses the 90-day post-final-work deadline to record its
lien (see §713.08), no affidavit can save those lien rights.

Florida decisions underscore the importance of timing. In **Lehman v.
Snyder**, a material supplier gave the owner a lien waiver marked “paid”
in exchange for the contractor’s check – which later bounced. The
supplier did not promptly notify the owner of the nonpayment and instead
waited until filing a lien claim months later. The Florida Supreme Court
held the lien was *extinguished* by the waiver: the owner had *no
knowledge* of the bad check when settling with the contractor, and the
“failure of the materialman to receive payment…resulted from his own
doings”[case-law.vlex.com](https://case-law.vlex.com/vid/lehman-v-snyder-894143381#:~:text=Lehman,were%20those%20listed%20in%20the)[case-law.vlex.com](https://case-law.vlex.com/vid/lehman-v-snyder-894143381#:~:text=When%20the%20building%20was%20completed,bill%20and%20the%20release%20of).
Similarly, in **Gulf Stream Lumber Co. v. Lathrop**, a supplier’s
receipt/release was delivered for a check that was returned NSF; the
supplier failed to alert the owners until long after they paid the
contractor. The Second DCA ruled the supplier’s lien was barred by
**equitable estoppel** – the owners relied on the release and had no
notice of nonpayment until they were served with the lien notice months
later[case-law.vlex.com](https://case-law.vlex.com/vid/gulf-stream-lumber-co-885439995#:~:text=A%20short%20time%20after%20giving,Plaintiff%20replied)[case-law.vlex.com](https://case-law.vlex.com/vid/gulf-stream-lumber-co-885439995#:~:text=financial%20trouble%2C%20so%20they%20demanded,3%2C%201956%2C%20apparently%20making%20their).
These cases illustrate that a lienor *cannot sit on its rights*: to
rescind a conditional waiver, the lienor must record and **serve** an
affidavit of nonpayment (or otherwise inform the owner) *quickly*,
ideally as soon as the payment default occurs. Waiting too long or past
the project’s completion can forfeit the lien.

**Form and Content Requirements:** Florida has **no mandatory statutory
form** for an affidavit of nonpayment, but certain best practices emerge
from case law. The affidavit should recite the lienor’s identity, the
property and project, reference the prior conditional waiver (by date
and amount), and **swear that the specified payment was never
received**. It must be signed and notarized to be a true “affidavit.” It
is prudent (though not explicitly required by statute) to serve a copy
on the owner and any interested parties (contractor, lender) to provide
actual notice of the nullification. In **Georgia** (for comparison), the
law requires serving the owner within 7 days of filing such an
affidavit; while Florida’s statute is silent on service, Florida owners
frequently insist contractually that they be notified of any affidavit
of nonpayment within a short window. Failing to promptly *serve the
owner* can be fatal: the older *Gulf Stream* case shows the owner’s
first notice of the issue came by mail after the lien was filed, too
late for the owner to withhold
funds[case-law.vlex.com](https://case-law.vlex.com/vid/gulf-stream-lumber-co-885439995#:~:text=A%20short%20time%20after%20giving,Plaintiff%20replied).
Today, a diligent lienor will not only record the affidavit in the
county records, but also send it (with a copy of the prior waiver) to
the owner by certified mail *within a few days*. This guards against the
argument that the owner paid out further money in good faith reliance on
the waiver.

**Courts’ Interpretation of Affidavit Effect:** When properly executed
and timely, an affidavit of nonpayment **suspends or nullifies the
conditional waiver, reinstating the lienor’s rights** to the same extent
as if the waiver had never been given. Florida courts have acknowledged
this effect – often through their treatment of conditional waiver
language. For example, in **Klein Development v. Ellis K. Phelps &
Co.**, a subcontractor faxed an executed lien release to the contractor
in exchange for a payment check. The check was never honored, and the
subcontractor then recorded its Claim of Lien. The owner raised the
release as a defense, but the Second DCA found *the subcontractor never
intended the faxed release to be binding without payment*. It upheld
summary judgment for the lienor, effectively treating the release as
void due to
nonpayment[case-law.vlex.com](https://case-law.vlex.com/vid/klein-development-v-ellis-891044842#:~:text=request,claim%2C%20Phelps%20sued%20Klein%20for).
Although the court didn’t use the phrase “affidavit of nonpayment,” the
subcontractor’s actions (withholding the original waiver and later
swearing in court that payment was not received) served the same purpose
– the lien was **enforced** because the condition for waiver
failed[case-law.vlex.com](https://case-law.vlex.com/vid/klein-development-v-ellis-891044842#:~:text=transmission%20in%20exchange%20for%20Cadenhead%27s,for%20foreclosure%20of%20the%20lien).
By contrast, if a lienor signs an *unconditional* release (one *not*
expressly conditioned on actual payment) and hands it over, Florida
courts will generally deem it immediately binding – *even if the check
bounces*. The harsh rule, established in cases like **Lehman** and its
progeny, is that a **returned check still counts as consideration to
support a release** absent a protective
clause[jimersonfirm.com](https://www.jimersonfirm.com/blog/resources/faqs/construction-law/#:~:text=Q%3A%20What%20can%20I%20do,return%20for%20a%20worthless%20check)[jimersonfirm.com](https://www.jimersonfirm.com/blog/resources/faqs/construction-law/#:~:text=returned%20check%20for%20insufficient%20funds,or%20words%20of%20similar%20effect).
In other words, a waiver stating “$X received” will be enforced on its
face; the lienor’s remedy is to sue for breach of contract or under the
worthless check statute, but *not* to file a
lien[cobbgonzalez.com](https://www.cobbgonzalez.com/construction-lien-releases-in-florida/#:~:text=of%20the%20check,tool%20%E2%80%93%20the%20construction%20lien).
Thus, the affidavit of nonpayment (or a conditional waiver clause) is
crucial to avoid this outcome.

Florida courts strictly construe lien waivers according to their plain
language. Any **errors in drafting** the waiver or affidavit can be
consequential. A common mistake is failing to include clear conditional
language. For instance, simply writing “upon receipt of payment” may be
too vague; best practice is to state the specific check or transaction
and that the release is “**void if payment is not received within \_\_\_
days.**” In one instructive federal case applying Florida law,
*Beck-Ford Constr., LLC v. TCA Global Credit*, the court noted that
execution of a *“valid release”* extinguishes all rights covered by
it[cobbgonzalez.com](https://www.cobbgonzalez.com/construction-lien-releases-in-florida/#:~:text=%E2%80%9CIn%20Florida%2C%20it%20is%20well,regarding%20construction%20releases%20in%20Florida).
Florida’s state courts likewise hold that an unambiguous release
**cannot be contradicted by parol evidence of conditional
intent**[cobbgonzalez.com](https://www.cobbgonzalez.com/construction-lien-releases-in-florida/#:~:text=unambiguous%20terms%20of%20a%20release,is%20clear%20on%20its%20face).
Thus, the onus is on the lienor to *write* the condition into the
document or accompany the waiver with a sworn statement of nonpayment.
When that is done properly, courts honor the parties’ intent. For
example, in **Klein v. Phelps**, the evidence of the conditional nature
of the faxed release led the court to conclude Phelps “never released
its lien rights” in the first
place[case-law.vlex.com](https://case-law.vlex.com/vid/klein-development-v-ellis-891044842#:~:text=Klein%20Development%20,its%20lien%20rights%2C%20we%20affirm)[case-law.vlex.com](https://case-law.vlex.com/vid/klein-development-v-ellis-891044842#:~:text=request,claim%2C%20Phelps%20sued%20Klein%20for).
Similarly, the Second DCA in *Klein* approvingly cited §713.20(7)–(8)
allowing modified forms, reinforcing that **conditional waivers are
permissible and
effective**[cobbgonzalez.com](https://www.cobbgonzalez.com/construction-lien-releases-in-florida/#:~:text=prohibited,of%20its%20lien%20rights%20if)[cobbgonzalez.com](https://www.cobbgonzalez.com/construction-lien-releases-in-florida/#:~:text=%C2%A7%20713,20%288).

**Recurring Mistakes and Consequences:**

  - **Providing an Unconditional Waiver Without Actual Payment:** By far
    the most frequent pitfall for subcontractors and suppliers is
    signing a broad, unconditional lien release *before* the funds have
    actually cleared. Florida law does not require lien waivers to be
    notarized or in statutory form, so owners and contractors sometimes
    present very expansive release
    forms[cobbgonzalez.com](https://www.cobbgonzalez.com/construction-lien-releases-in-florida/#:~:text=It%20is%20important%20to%20first,1996)[cobbgonzalez.com](https://www.cobbgonzalez.com/construction-lien-releases-in-florida/#:~:text=Section%20713,20%288).
    If a lienor signs such a release and doesn’t get paid, their lien is
    extinguished *on the spot*. As the Fourth District warned, “the
    unambiguous terms of a release may not be avoided on the basis of
    unilateral mistake” or
    nonpayment[cobbgonzalez.com](https://www.cobbgonzalez.com/construction-lien-releases-in-florida/#:~:text=unambiguous%20terms%20of%20a%20release,is%20clear%20on%20its%20face).
    The **Consolidated Electric Supply v. Frank Maio** case exemplifies
    this: a material supplier signed a release through a certain date,
    later realizing it waived more than intended, but the court enforced
    it
    strictly[cobbgonzalez.com](https://www.cobbgonzalez.com/construction-lien-releases-in-florida/#:~:text=Consol,the%20Court%20reasoned%20that%20the).
    The lienor’s only recourse was a breach of contract claim, since the
    lien had been waived. In short, **signing an unconditional final
    release too early is a fatal error** – it cannot be retracted absent
    fraud or mutual mistake. The Florida Bar has noted that even a $0
    “final” waiver can bar any further lien, so lienors must *only
    waive to the extent
    paid*[justanswer.com](https://www.justanswer.com/law/s5bcf-received-final-payment-affidavit-gc-quit.html#:~:text=Are%20Zero%20Dollar%20Lien%20Waivers,0%2C%20as%20it%20waives).

  - **Failing to Include/Invoke the Conditional Payment Clause:**
    Florida’s statutory forms themselves *do not* contain language
    voiding the release if a check
    bounces[cobbgonzalez.com](https://www.cobbgonzalez.com/construction-lien-releases-in-florida/#:~:text=A%20lienor%20is%20only%20required,%E2%80%9D)[cobbgonzalez.com](https://www.cobbgonzalez.com/construction-lien-releases-in-florida/#:~:text=prohibited,of%20its%20lien%20rights%20if).
    The statute lets the lienor *add* such language, but if you neglect
    to do so, the courts will treat the waiver as **absolute**. For
    example, in **Lehman v. Snyder**, because the supplier’s lien
    release contained no reservation, the Supreme Court held the owner
    was entitled to rely on it despite the contractor’s NSF
    check[case-law.vlex.com](https://case-law.vlex.com/vid/lehman-v-snyder-894143381#:~:text=Lehman,were%20those%20listed%20in%20the)[case-law.vlex.com](https://case-law.vlex.com/vid/lehman-v-snyder-894143381#:~:text=lien%20by%20delivering%20the%20Lehman,the%20%27bad%20check%27%20before%20making).
    To avoid this, the lienor should either (a) hand over only a
    *conditional* release (e.g. “effective *only upon* actual receipt of
    $**”) or (b) hold the release in escrow until paid. Many Florida
    construction attorneys recommend writing on the face of the waiver:
    \*“This release is conditioned upon clearance of Check No.** *in the
    amount $*\_\_; if said payment is not received, this release is null
    and
    void”\*[jimersonfirm.com](https://www.jimersonfirm.com/blog/resources/faqs/construction-law/#:~:text=Q%3A%20What%20can%20I%20do,return%20for%20a%20worthless%20check)[jimersonfirm.com](https://www.jimersonfirm.com/blog/resources/faqs/construction-law/#:~:text=returned%20check%20for%20insufficient%20funds,or%20words%20of%20similar%20effect).
    Failing to use such conditional wording is a common drafting mistake
    that can cost a lienor its rights.

  - **Not Recording or Serving the Affidavit in Time:** As discussed,
    speed is critical. If a lienor waits beyond 60 days or more to
    record an affidavit of nonpayment, the owner or lender may have
    changed position (paid out retainage, etc.) on the assumption the
    lien was waived. At that point, courts may refuse to revive the lien
    on equitable grounds. In *Gulf Stream Lumber*, the supplier’s lien
    was filed \~4 months after the false “paid” receipt was given, and
    the court barred the lien, noting the owner had “subsequently paid
    all outstanding bills” and the supplier’s delay misled
    them[case-law.vlex.com](https://case-law.vlex.com/vid/gulf-stream-lumber-co-885439995#:~:text=presented,the%20last%20week%20in%20March)[case-law.vlex.com](https://case-law.vlex.com/vid/gulf-stream-lumber-co-885439995#:~:text=A%20short%20time%20after%20giving,Under).
    The clear lesson: **serve notice of nonpayment before the owner
    makes final payment.** An affidavit recorded *after* the owner has
    disbursed final funds or issued a lender’s title clearance will
    likely be ineffectual. Also, the affidavit itself does not create a
    lien – the lienor must still record a **Claim of Lien** within 90
    days of last work to enforce the lien (the affidavit simply
    preserves the right to do
    so)[gray-robinson.com](https://www.gray-robinson.com/docs/Georgia%20Lien%20Law%20-%202015.PPTX#:~:text=Is%20that%20it%3F%20No%2C%20Affidavit,furnishing%20if%20still%20not%20paid).
    A GrayRobinson construction law update bluntly noted: an affidavit
    of nonpayment “is not a lien; \[you\] still must file a Claim of
    Lien within 90 days of final furnishing if still not
    paid”[gray-robinson.com](https://www.gray-robinson.com/docs/Georgia%20Lien%20Law%20-%202015.PPTX#:~:text=%5BPPT%5D%20Slide%201%20,furnishing%20if%20still%20not%20paid).
    Forgetting to timely record the lien (perhaps because one assumes
    the affidavit alone suffices) is a fatal procedural mistake.

  - **Recording in the Wrong County or with Defective Details:** While
    no published Florida case specifically recounts this, a practical
    error is mis-recording the affidavit (e.g. in the wrong county’s
    records or with an incorrect legal description of the property).
    Such missteps would undermine the affidavit’s effect because third
    parties (or the owner’s title search) may never discover it. The
    affidavit should be recorded in the *same county* as the project’s
    Notice of Commencement and should reference the property identically
    to the lien waiver and NOC. Florida’s general rule of strict
    construction of lien documents means any material error in the
    affidavit’s property description or identity of the contracting
    parties could render it insufficient to revive the lien. No case was
    found where this mistake decided the outcome, likely because prudent
    lienors ensure accuracy, but it remains a point of caution.

  - **Failure to Verify the Affidavit:** An “affidavit” by definition
    must be sworn. A minor but important detail is to sign before a
    notary and include a jurat. An unverified statement might not meet
    the statutory predicate (for example, Florida requires a Claim of
    Lien to be a sworn statement (§713.08); similarly a nonpayment
    affidavit should be sworn to lend it credibility). Always
    double-check that the document is properly notarized before
    recording. Again, while no case explicitly turned on this, a savvy
    opposing counsel could attack an unsworn notice of nonpayment as a
    nullity.

**Impacts on Lien and Bond Rights:** When done right, filing an
affidavit of nonpayment **allows the lienor to enforce its lien or bond
claim as if the waiver had never been given**. The conditional waiver is
essentially suspended until actual payment is received. In *Georgia*
(whose statute is more explicit on this point), the filing of an
affidavit of nonpayment *within the statutory period* renders the prior
waiver
“void”[dri.org](https://www.dri.org/docs/default-source/webdocs/other/2021/april/construction-law-case-update.pdf?sfvrsn=2#:~:text=any%20longer%2C%20and%20only%20the,filed%20suit).
Florida courts have reached analogous results through contract
interpretation: a conditional release means “lien rights cannot be
waived prior to receipt of payment for the amount waived,” so if payment
never comes, the waiver never takes
effect[troutman.com](https://www.troutman.com/insights/revisiting-lien-waivers-you-may-be-releasing-more-than-you-bargained-for.html#:~:text=Owner%20and%20Prime%20Contractor%20demands,forms%20and%20provides%20conditional%20and).
In **Klein v. Phelps**, the outcome was that Phelps’s previously
delivered release *did not bar* its foreclosure suit – the affidavit
(and other evidence of nonpayment) “successfully reinstated” Phelps’s
lien rights, and Phelps ultimately won the lien foreclosure
judgment[case-law.vlex.com](https://case-law.vlex.com/vid/klein-development-v-ellis-891044842#:~:text=Klein%20Development%20,its%20lien%20rights%2C%20we%20affirm)[case-law.vlex.com](https://case-law.vlex.com/vid/klein-development-v-ellis-891044842#:~:text=request,claim%2C%20Phelps%20sued%20Klein%20for).
By contrast, if the affidavit of nonpayment is *invalid or late*, the
conditional waiver becomes final and *irrevocable*, and the lienor is
left without lien or bond security. In **Spectrum Interiors, Inc. v.
Exterior Walls, Inc.**, a subcontractor’s unconditional final release
was enforced to bar its remaining contract claims – the Fifth DCA held
that when a release unambiguously covers all work through a date, the
court “may not indulge in construction of a release that is clear on its
face”[cobbgonzalez.com](https://www.cobbgonzalez.com/construction-lien-releases-in-florida/#:~:text=unambiguous%20terms%20of%20a%20release,is%20clear%20on%20its%20face).
The subcontractor who failed to except certain pending change orders
lost not only lien rights but even the right to sue for that work. This
highlights that a mistaken release can wipe out *all* remedies (in
Georgia, courts have similarly held that not filing the affidavit of
nonpayment in time can waive *both* lien and breach of contract claims –
a cautionary tale for Florida practitioners).

On **bonded projects**, Florida’s Mechanics’ Lien Law has parallel
provisions. A lien waiver can also waive claims against a payment bond
(see §713.23 and §713.235). Section 713.23(5) (formerly §713.235(5))
essentially mirrors §713.20(8): a bond claim waiver not in statutory
form is enforceable as per its
terms[flsenate.gov](https://www.flsenate.gov/Session/Bill/2020/868/Analyses/2020s00868.pre.ju.PDF#:~:text=,this%20section%20is%20enforceable).
Courts apply the same logic: a conditional waiver of bond rights can be
nullified by an affidavit of nonpayment if payment fails. However,
lienors on bonded jobs must also comply with *notice of nonpayment*
requirements (a different “nonpayment” notice – statutorily required to
perfect a bond claim). In **Stock Building Supply of Fla., Inc. v.
Soares Da Costa Constr. Servs., LLC**, the Third DCA noted that after a
project restarted under a payment bond, the supplier *did* send the
surety a formal Notice of Nonpayment when it wasn’t paid for new
deliveries[caselaw.findlaw.com](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/court/fl-district-court-of-appeal/1581927.html#:~:text=On%20August%2022%2C%202005%2C%20K,under%20Soares%20Da%20Costa%27s%20order)[caselaw.findlaw.com](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/court/fl-district-court-of-appeal/1581927.html#:~:text=Project%20under%20the%20order%20of,23%281%29%28d%29%2C%20Florida%20Statutes%20%282005).
Unfortunately, the supplier had failed to serve a new Notice to
Contractor for those items, so its bond claim was denied on other
grounds[caselaw.findlaw.com](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/court/fl-district-court-of-appeal/1581927.html#:~:text=Costa,previously%2C%20in%20the%20First%20Notice)[caselaw.findlaw.com](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/court/fl-district-court-of-appeal/1581927.html#:~:text=subcontractor%20who%20remains%20unpaid,Law%27s%20notice%20requirements%20as%20follows).
The key takeaway is that on bonded jobs, a lienor must preserve *both*
its bond notice rights and use conditional waivers or affidavits to
avoid an unintended bond claim waiver. If a lien waiver form explicitly
waives “lien and bond rights,” an affidavit of nonpayment would need to
address both (and practically, the lienor should promptly send the
statutory Notice of Nonpayment to the surety within 90 days of each
unpaid delivery, per §713.23(1)(d)). Failing that, the bond claim will
be lost just as a lien would be.

**Judicial Trends and Practice Pointers:** Modern Florida courts
continue to uphold conditional waivers and emphasize strict compliance
with procedural requirements. A 2011 Florida Bar Journal article
observed that a statutory “Final Release” upon final payment does
**not** waive lien rights for labor performed afterward – reinforcing
that lien rights cannot be waived in advance of the
work[floridabar.org](https://www.floridabar.org/the-florida-bar-journal/when-is-a-final-construction-lien-release-really-final/#:~:text=inability%20to%20waive%20lien%20rights,is%20not%20a%20safe%20harbor).
Thus, if additional work is done, a prior “final” release isn’t actually
final for that subsequent work. The lesson for owners is to obtain a new
release covering any extra work or change orders. For lienors, this
means *do not sign a final release until truly finished and paid*. The
courts have also approved creative solutions like combining a final
release with an **assignment-back and indemnification** (to cover
post-release
work)[floridabar.org](https://www.floridabar.org/the-florida-bar-journal/when-is-a-final-construction-lien-release-really-final/#:~:text=Given%20this%20conundrum%2C%20this%20author,final%20payment%20is%20truly%20final)[floridabar.org](https://www.floridabar.org/the-florida-bar-journal/when-is-a-final-construction-lien-release-really-final/#:~:text=The%20undersigned%20hereby%20assigns%20its,at%20subject%20project%20to),
showing the lengths practitioners go to balance the anti-advance-waiver
rule with owners’ need for finality.

After Florida’s **2013 lien law amendments (HB 331)**, which tweaked
some notice procedures (though leaving §713.20(8) intact), practitioners
feared confusion about non-statutory waiver forms. Some proposed
deleting §713.20(8) to *mandate* use of statutory
forms[hammerngavel.com](https://hammerngavel.com/blog/proposed-statute-would-make-use-of-statutory-construction-lien-release-forms-mandatory#:~:text=Proposed%20Statute%20Would%20Make%20Use,lien%20waiver%20or%20lien%20release),
but that did not occur. Post-HB 331, no major case has undermined the
effectiveness of affidavits of nonpayment – in fact, courts construing
§713.20(8)-(9) continue to hold that a lien waiver **“is enforceable in
accordance with its terms”**, meaning a waiver stating it is conditional
will be enforced as
conditional[cobbgonzalez.com](https://www.cobbgonzalez.com/construction-lien-releases-in-florida/#:~:text=%C2%A7%20713,20%288).
The upshot: Florida courts give effect to *what the document says*. If
you want the ability to nullify a waiver, it must *say so clearly*.

**Practical Guidance for Construction Practitioners:**

  - *For Lienors (Contractors, Subs, Suppliers):* **Never sign an
    unconditional release if you haven’t been paid**. Insist on using
    the statutory forms *or adding a conditional payment clause*.
    Florida law protects you here: §713.20(6) prohibits anyone from
    *requiring* a different (non-statutory) form as a condition of
    payment[cobbgonzalez.com](https://www.cobbgonzalez.com/construction-lien-releases-in-florida/#:~:text=modified%20to%20best%20protect%20the,4%29%20and).
    If an upstream party presents an onerous waiver, you can object –
    but do so *before* signing. As one court noted, signing signifies
    consent to its
    terms[cobbgonzalez.com](https://www.cobbgonzalez.com/construction-lien-releases-in-florida/#:~:text=The%20statute%20basically%20allows%20parties,terms%20contained%20in%20the%20release).
    So negotiate to include conditional language (e.g. “through date X,
    *provided payment is received*”). If you unfortunately gave an
    unconditional waiver and payment is not received on time, act
    **immediately**: prepare an affidavit of nonpayment and record it in
    the county records, and **serve it on the owner (and contractor and
    surety, if applicable)** within days. Promptly follow up by
    recording your Claim of Lien within the 90-day
    period[gray-robinson.com](https://www.gray-robinson.com/docs/Georgia%20Lien%20Law%20-%202015.PPTX#:~:text=Is%20that%20it%3F%20No%2C%20Affidavit,furnishing%20if%20still%20not%20paid).
    By giving the owner early notice, you preserve the owner’s ability
    to withhold funds from the party who failed to pay you. This cuts
    off any “prejudice” argument and strengthens your case that the
    waiver should be deemed void for nonpayment. In court, be prepared
    to show that your waiver was expressly conditional or that the other
    side clearly understood payment was a condition (affidavits, emails,
    etc., as in *Klein v. Phelps*). Also remember: a conditional lien
    waiver does **not** eliminate the need to send a Notice to Owner or
    record a lien – those must still be done on time if payment isn’t
    forthcoming.

  - *For Owners and Lenders:* **Implement protocols to track conditional
    waivers and any ensuing affidavits of nonpayment.** When you receive
    a progress or final release that is conditional (or obviously given
    in exchange for a not-yet-cleared form of payment), do not assume
    the matter is closed until the payment clears the bank. It is wise
    to wait the stated period (e.g. “within 30 days of this waiver”)
    before disbursing further funds. Owners should *promptly notify the
    contractor* if an affidavit of nonpayment comes in from a sub, and
    consider **withholding an equivalent amount** from the next payment
    to the contractor, as permitted by
    §713.20(7)[law.justia.com](https://law.justia.com/codes/florida/title-xl/chapter-713/part-i/section-713-20/#:~:text=,any%20such%20condition%20is%20satisfied)[law.justia.com](https://law.justia.com/codes/florida/title-xl/chapter-713/part-i/section-713-20/#:~:text=condition%20the%20waiver%20and%20release,any%20such%20condition%20is%20satisfied).
    Florida law actually allows an owner, in the absence of a payment
    bond, to retain from the prime contract any amount equal to a
    lienor’s unpaid check until it’s
    satisfied[law.justia.com](https://law.justia.com/codes/florida/title-xl/chapter-713/part-i/section-713-20/#:~:text=,any%20such%20condition%20is%20satisfied).
    This protects owners from double payment. If a lienor records an
    affidavit of nonpayment against your project, you should treat it as
    a red flag: it means a conditional release is being rescinded.
    Immediately **verify the facts** – was the lienor truly not paid? –
    and then handle it like a potential lien: you may secure a lien
    release bond or joint check the lienor, etc., to avoid cloud on
    title.

  - *Avoiding Common Errors:* **Accuracy and consistency** are key.
    Ensure the project name, property description, and parties’ names in
    any affidavit of nonpayment match those in the lien waiver and
    Notice of Commencement. Any deviation can create arguments for the
    other side. Likewise, use a notary and keep copies of everything
    (you may need to exhibit the conditional waiver and affidavit
    together to prove the lien’s validity). Another tip: when possible,
    **deliver releases only in exchange for actual payment** – e.g.,
    hand the release *simultaneously* upon receipt of a wire transfer or
    cashier’s check. Many contractors use escrow arrangements: the
    signed release is held until the bank confirms payment, then
    released to the payor. This eliminates the need for an affidavit of
    nonpayment altogether.

In summary, Florida courts will *enforce lien waivers as written*. They
have upheld the effectiveness of an affidavit of nonpayment when it is
the agreed-upon method to nullify a conditional waiver for
nonpayment[case-law.vlex.com](https://case-law.vlex.com/vid/klein-development-v-ellis-891044842#:~:text=request,claim%2C%20Phelps%20sued%20Klein%20for).
But they have also refused to rescue lienors from the consequences of a
poorly drafted or unconditional release where no timely affidavit was
filed[jimersonfirm.com](https://www.jimersonfirm.com/blog/resources/faqs/construction-law/#:~:text=returned%20check%20for%20insufficient%20funds,or%20words%20of%20similar%20effect)[case-law.vlex.com](https://case-law.vlex.com/vid/gulf-stream-lumber-co-885439995#:~:text=A%20short%20time%20after%20giving,Plaintiff%20replied).
The recurring theme in the case law is **strict compliance** – both with
the contract language and the timing rules of the lien law. As one court
quipped, the lien law “presumes a fiction” that subcontractors get paid
when releases are
exchanged[jimersonfirm.com](https://www.jimersonfirm.com/blog/resources/faqs/construction-law/#:~:text=A%3A%20The%20timing%20of%20releases,the%20nonpayment%20to%20the%20subcontractors);
if you sign the release, the law assumes you *were paid*. It’s up to the
lienor to rebut that fiction by swiftly recording an affidavit of
nonpayment or otherwise preserving its rights. By understanding these
nuances and planning ahead, practitioners can avoid the common missteps
(like unwittingly waiving lien/bond rights) and ensure that
**conditional waivers truly remain conditional** – with the affidavit of
nonpayment as the safety valve if the payment never comes.
