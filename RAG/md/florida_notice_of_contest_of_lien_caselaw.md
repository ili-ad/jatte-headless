# Florida’s Notice of Contest of Lien – Judicial Interpretation & Pitfalls

## Timeliness & the 60-Day Foreclosure Trigger

Florida’s Construction Lien Law generally gives a lienor one year to
file a foreclosure lawsuit on a recorded
lien[caselaw.findlaw.com](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/fl-district-court-of-appeal/1460985.html#:~:text=An%20owner%20may%20%E2%80%9Cshorten%E2%80%9D%20the,The%20statute%20provides).
A properly recorded **Notice of Contest of Lien**, however, shortens
that deadline to **60 days from service** of the
notice[caselaw.findlaw.com](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/fl-district-court-of-appeal/1460985.html#:~:text=The%20lien%20of%20any%20lienor,notice%20shall%20be%20extinguished%20automatically).
Courts strictly enforce this 60-day limitations period. If the lienor
fails to **“institute a suit to enforce \[the\] lien within 60 days”**,
the lien **“shall be extinguished automatically”** by operation of
law[caselaw.findlaw.com](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/fl-district-court-of-appeal/1460985.html#:~:text=to%20enforce%20a%20lien%20not,without%20intervention%20of%20the%20court%E2%80%9D).
In other words, on the 60th day the lien is discharged without any court
order
needed[caselaw.findlaw.com](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/fl-district-court-of-appeal/1460985.html#:~:text=v,without%20intervention%20of%20the%20court%E2%80%9D).

Florida courts have been emphatic that **“when the Florida lien law says
‘60 days,’ it means 60 days.”** No extensions or equitable exceptions
will save an untimely lien foreclosure. For example, in *Rabil v.
Seaside Builders*, a contractor did not sue the bond surety within 60
days after the owners contested the lien, and thus “the lien was
automatically extinguished by operation of law” on day
60[caselaw.findlaw.com](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/fl-district-court-of-appeal/1872693.html#:~:text=The%20legislature%20has%20provided%20a,LLC%2C%2084).
The clerk of court was obligated to release the lien transfer bond in
that case, confirming that expiration of the 60 days terminates the lien
rights as a matter of
law[caselaw.findlaw.com](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/fl-district-court-of-appeal/1872693.html#:~:text=The%20legislature%20has%20provided%20a,LLC%2C%2084).
Florida’s lien deadlines are considered part of the lien right itself –
if the time elapses, the lien right **“becomes extinguished and is gone
forever”**[caselaw.findlaw.com](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/court/fl-district-court-of-appeal/1731298.html#:~:text=the%20transfer%20results%20in%20the,%E2%80%9D).
This strict approach serves the statutory purpose of giving owners,
buyers, and lenders certainty that lien claims will be resolved or
expire by a date
certain[caselaw.findlaw.com](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/court/fl-district-court-of-appeal/1731298.html#:~:text=,is%20of%20course%20allowable%2C%20but)[linkedin.com](https://www.linkedin.com/pulse/sixty-days-means-court-reiterates-construction-lien-law-thomas-wert#:~:text=,file%20the%20lien%20or%20suit).

**Computing the 60 days:** The 60-day clock starts on the day **after**
the Notice of Contest is served on the lienor (the service date is
excluded) and includes the last day of the
period[floridabar.org](https://www.floridabar.org/the-florida-bar-journal/special-statutory-proceedings-for-the-discharge-of-construction-liens/#:~:text=lien%20requiring%20the%20lien%20claimant,referenced%20the%20rule%20of%20procedure).
Courts apply the “uniform rule” of time computation, as the Fifth DCA
noted, meaning if an owner serves a contest on July 1, the 60 days begin
July 2 and end on August 30 (unless day 60 falls on a weekend or
holiday, in which case Florida’s general procedural rule may allow
filing on the next business
day)[floridabar.org](https://www.floridabar.org/the-florida-bar-journal/special-statutory-proceedings-for-the-discharge-of-construction-liens/#:~:text=lien%20requiring%20the%20lien%20claimant,referenced%20the%20rule%20of%20procedure).
Lienors must carefully track this deadline. In *Lehmann Development
Corp. v. Nirenblatt*, the court allowed a one-year lien deadline to roll
to Monday when day 365 fell on a Sunday, applying the civil procedure
rule for time
computation[floridabar.org](https://www.floridabar.org/the-florida-bar-journal/special-statutory-proceedings-for-the-discharge-of-construction-liens/#:~:text=In%20Lehmann%20Development%20Corporation%20v,of%20an%20action%20on%20the)[floridabar.org](https://www.floridabar.org/the-florida-bar-journal/special-statutory-proceedings-for-the-discharge-of-construction-liens/#:~:text=fell%20on%20a%20Sunday%20so,timely%20filing%20the%20following%20Monday).
However, because a Notice of Contest triggers a **“special statutory
proceeding”**, lienors should not assume any grace period beyond the
strict 60
days[floridabar.org](https://www.floridabar.org/the-florida-bar-journal/special-statutory-proceedings-for-the-discharge-of-construction-liens/#:~:text=The%20horns%20of%20the%20dilemma,In%20fact%2C%20absent%20a%20specific)[floridabar.org](https://www.floridabar.org/the-florida-bar-journal/special-statutory-proceedings-for-the-discharge-of-construction-liens/#:~:text=Rules%20of%20Civil%20Procedure%20do,).
The safest course is to act well **before** the deadline to avoid any
dispute[floridabar.org](https://www.floridabar.org/the-florida-bar-journal/special-statutory-proceedings-for-the-discharge-of-construction-liens/#:~:text=The%20safest%20practice%2C%20of%20course%2C,tested%20on%20a%20substantive%20basis).

**Strict foreclosure cutoff:** Once an owner records a Notice of
Contest, the lienor must not only file the foreclosure lawsuit in 60
days but also meet any other pre-suit conditions within that same
period. Courts have held that all statutory prerequisites to foreclosure
remain applicable on the shortened timeline. For instance, a contractor
is normally required to serve a **contractor’s final payment affidavit**
at least 5 days before filing suit (per §713.06(3)(d)). In *Pierson D.
Construction, Inc. v. Yudell*, the owner recorded a Notice of Contest
and the contractor did file the lien foreclosure suit within 60 days.
However, the contractor did **not** furnish the final payment affidavit
until after the 60-day window had run. The Fourth DCA affirmed dismissal
of the lien foreclosure, ruling that the final affidavit must be served
within the same 60-day period triggered by the Notice of
Contest[caselaw.findlaw.com](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/fl-district-court-of-appeal/1460985.html#:~:text=On%20December%205%2C%202000%2C%20the,court%20later%20dismissed%20the%20case)[caselaw.findlaw.com](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/fl-district-court-of-appeal/1460985.html#:~:text=Pierson%20contends%20that%20its%20suit,2000%20filing%20of%20the%20lawsuit).
In short, a lienor cannot commence an action in time but finish the
required steps later – **everything** needed to perfect the foreclosure
must occur before the 60 days
expire[caselaw.findlaw.com](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/fl-district-court-of-appeal/1460985.html#:~:text=Pierson%20contends%20that%20its%20suit,2000%20filing%20of%20the%20lawsuit).
Even an amended pleading filed after 60 days will not save the lien.
Florida’s Supreme Court long ago made clear that a lienor cannot evade
the deadline by amending or re-filing the lien or lawsuit; such changes
**“do not toll the statutory times”** for
enforcement[caselaw.findlaw.com](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/court/fl-district-court-of-appeal/1731298.html#:~:text=others%20who%20rely%20on%20the,file%20the%20lien%20or%20suit)[floridaconstructionlegalupdates.com](https://www.floridaconstructionlegalupdates.com/an-amended-lien-does-not-delay-the-60-day-window-to-foreclose-a-lien-when-a-notice-of-contest-of-lien-is-recorded/#:~:text=This%20exact%20scenario%20was%20addressed,Contest%20of%20Lien%20is%20recorded).
Thus, a lienor has only one bite at the apple within the fixed period.
As the Supreme Court explained in *Jack Stilson & Co. v. Caloosa Bayview
Corp.*, allowing serial amendments after a Notice of Contest would
destroy the certainty the statute requires – a lienor could otherwise
“file over and over ad infinitum… and thereby defeat the purpose of
the statute for a speedy
conclusion”[case-law.vlex.com](https://case-law.vlex.com/vid/foy-v-mangum-no-885480257#:~:text=in%20section%20713.22%20%282%29.,Palmer%20at%20510).

**Common timing pitfalls:** A review of the case law shows lienors (or
their attorneys) occasionally miscalculate or mishandle the 60-day
deadline, often with fatal results. Some assume that filing any lawsuit
(even if incomplete) will preserve the lien, or that a pending
foreclosure suit can be amended at leisure. This is a mistake. In
*Hiller v. Phoenix*, Phoenix sued the owner timely, but after the lien
was transferred to a bond and contested, Phoenix waited more than 60
days to add the surety. The Second DCA held the lien rights were lost,
strictly applying the 60-day limit despite the already-filed
suit[linkedin.com](https://www.linkedin.com/pulse/sixty-days-means-court-reiterates-construction-lien-law-thomas-wert#:~:text=the%20real%20property%20lien,Jomar%20Properties)[linkedin.com](https://www.linkedin.com/pulse/sixty-days-means-court-reiterates-construction-lien-law-thomas-wert#:~:text=,file%20the%20lien%20or%20suit).
Lienors have also tried to argue that an amended claim of lien resets or
extends the deadline. This too is incorrect – an **amended lien**
recorded after a Notice of Contest does **not** restart the
clock[floridaconstructionlegalupdates.com](https://www.floridaconstructionlegalupdates.com/an-amended-lien-does-not-delay-the-60-day-window-to-foreclose-a-lien-when-a-notice-of-contest-of-lien-is-recorded/#:~:text=This%20exact%20scenario%20was%20addressed,Contest%20of%20Lien%20is%20recorded).
The foreclosure action must still be filed (or amended to include the
new lien amount) within the original 60 days from the contest notice.
The upshot is that courts show **zero tolerance** for late filings. A
lienor who misses the 60-day window (even by a day) or who forecloses
without completing required pre-suit steps will find the lien
**automatically
void**[caselaw.findlaw.com](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/fl-district-court-of-appeal/1872693.html#:~:text=The%20legislature%20has%20provided%20a,LLC%2C%2084).
The lienor can still pursue a breach of contract claim against the
responsible party, but the powerful leverage of the construction lien is
forfeited[caselaw.findlaw.com](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/court/fl-district-court-of-appeal/1731298.html#:~:text=lien%20lapsed%20when%20the%20lienor,3d%20DCA%201991)[caselaw.findlaw.com](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/court/fl-district-court-of-appeal/1731298.html#:~:text=timely%20commencing%20an%20action%20against,purpose%20behind%20the%20special%20statutory).

## Statutory-Form Compliance & Common Drafting Defects

**Form of the Notice:** The Notice of Contest of Lien is a creature of
statute, and Florida law provides a specific template for its content.
Section 713.22(2) prescribes that the notice identify the lien (often by
referring to the recorded lien’s book and page or instrument number) and
state, in substance, that the lien will be extinguished unless suit is
filed within 60
days[jimersonfirm.com](https://www.jimersonfirm.com/blog/2024/06/contesting-construction-lien-claims-contractors-beware-of-the-notice-of-contest-of-lien-during-litigation/#:~:text=,service%2C%20the%20clerk%20shall%20serve).
It must be recorded with the clerk of court for the county where the
property is located, and the clerk in turn will serve a copy on the
lienor (as described in the next
section)[jimersonfirm.com](https://www.jimersonfirm.com/blog/2024/06/contesting-construction-lien-claims-contractors-beware-of-the-notice-of-contest-of-lien-during-litigation/#:~:text=,services%20as%20provided%20by%20law).
In practice, most clerks have a form document that owners or their
attorneys can fill out and record. It typically includes the lienor’s
name and address, the property description or parcel information, the
recording details of the lien, and the statutory warning about the
60-day deadline.

Because the Notice of Contest is part of a **“special statutory
proceeding,”** courts require **strict compliance** with the statutory
form and
content[case-law.vlex.com](https://case-law.vlex.com/vid/foy-v-mangum-no-885480257#:~:text=in%20section%20713.22%20%282%29.,Palmer%20at%20510)[caselaw.findlaw.com](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/court/fl-district-court-of-appeal/1731298.html#:~:text=%E2%80%9C,Bergeron).
Florida courts do not tolerate material deviations or omissions in any
lien law documents, and the Notice of Contest is no exception. In *Foy
v. Mangum*, the Fifth DCA reiterated that the requirements of the
mechanic’s lien statute **“must be strictly construed. Strict compliance
is an indispensable prerequisite for one seeking affirmative relief
under the
statute.”**[case-law.vlex.com](https://case-law.vlex.com/vid/foy-v-mangum-no-885480257#:~:text=in%20section%20713.22%20%282%29.,Palmer%20at%20510).
Although that case dealt with a lien timing issue, the principle applies
across Chapter 713 filings – a notice that fails to conform to the
statute could be deemed ineffective. For example, if an owner uses the
wrong form or omits key information (such as the official record
book/page of the lien or the lienor’s name and address), a lienor could
later argue that the 60-day clock never started due to a defective
notice. Likewise, recording the notice in the wrong county or against an
incorrect property would not actually contest the intended lien. There
appear to be no reported appellate cases where a Notice of Contest was
invalidated for minor technical defects, likely because most owners use
the statutory language. But given the courts’ strict construction,
**any** deviation from the statute’s form or procedure is at the owner’s
peril.

**Recurring owner errors:** Practitioners note a few pitfalls that have
arisen in trial courts or could easily occur. One is using the wrong
document entirely – for instance, an owner (or inexperienced attorney)
might mistakenly record a **“Notice to Owner/Contractor”** form (which
is a different pre-lien notice used by lienors) instead of a Notice of
Contest of Lien. Such a misfiling would not trigger §713.22(2)’s
effects, as it is not the statutory contest notice. Another error is
failing to properly **identify the lien** being contested. If multiple
liens are recorded on the property, the notice should clearly reference
which lien (by lienor name and recording number) it is contesting. Using
an incorrect name for the lienor or wrong recording number could nullify
the notice or cause confusion. Similarly, not **signing and notarizing**
the notice (as required for recordable instruments) will prevent it from
being recorded or given legal effect. While these mistakes are
avoidable, they occur on occasion. Florida law provides no grace for
substantial errors – an incorrectly drafted or recorded notice might be
deemed a legal nullity, leaving the one-year lien period intact. Owners
should therefore double-check that the recorded notice contains all
information exactly as the statute and clerk require.

**No “harmless error” doctrine:** Florida courts generally refuse to
excuse even minor technical defects in lien law compliance. The
rationale is that lien rights are statutory privileges in derogation of
common law, so parties must strictly adhere to statutory
mandates[case-law.vlex.com](https://case-law.vlex.com/vid/foy-v-mangum-no-885480257#:~:text=in%20section%20713.22%20%282%29.,Palmer%20at%20510).
For instance, in the lien context, a slight mistake in a Notice to Owner
or a Claim of Lien can invalidate the lien. We see a similar philosophy
with Notices of Contest. In *Palmer Electric Services, Inc. v. Filler*,
the Second DCA rejected a lien claim for failure to meet the statutory
prescription, emphasizing that courts are **not** free to overlook
noncompliance[case-law.vlex.com](https://case-law.vlex.com/vid/foy-v-mangum-no-885480257#:~:text=protect%20the%20owner,Palmer%20at%20510).
Thus, an owner who attempts to contest a lien must ensure the notice is
**perfectly drafted and filed**. There is no reported case of a court
forgiving a flawed notice of contest on the theory that the lienor
“understood” it or wasn’t prejudiced. On the contrary, the burden is
on the owner to follow the statute to the letter. As one court put it,
**“the statutory requirements for enforcing mechanic’s liens against
property must be strictly construed”** in favor of the property owner’s
protection[case-law.vlex.com](https://case-law.vlex.com/vid/foy-v-mangum-no-885480257#:~:text=in%20section%20713.22%20%282%29.,Palmer%20at%20510)
– but that protection only applies when the owner also strictly complies
with the statute’s prescribed method.

**Practical pointers for owners:** Common drafting oversights include
omitting the **official record reference** for the lien or using an
outdated legal description of the property. Always use the exact
information from the recorded Claim of Lien (names, recording numbers,
property details) on the Notice of Contest. Ensure the notice uses the
statutory wording about the lien being extinguished after 60 days; the
safest approach is to use the form provided by the clerk or statute.
Have the notice signed by the property owner (or its authorized
agent/attorney) and properly notarized before recording. Finally, pay
the clerk’s service fee and follow up to confirm that the clerk sends
out the notice to the lienor. As discussed below, the clerk’s certified
mailing is what starts the 60-day
countdown[jimersonfirm.com](https://www.jimersonfirm.com/blog/2024/06/contesting-construction-lien-claims-contractors-beware-of-the-notice-of-contest-of-lien-during-litigation/#:~:text=,services%20as%20provided%20by%20law),
so a notice that is recorded but never served on the lienor due to an
administrative mistake might not achieve its purpose. In summary, small
mistakes in form or recording can completely undermine a Notice of
Contest, so owners (and their attorneys) should treat the preparation of
this notice with the same exacting care as any court pleading – because
courts will.

## Service Requirements & Due-Process Challenges

Recording the Notice of Contest is only half the battle – it must be
**served on the lienor** in accordance with the statute to effectively
shorten the lien. Florida Statutes §713.22(2) directs that upon
recording the notice, **“\[t\]he clerk shall serve, in accordance with
s. 713.18, a copy of the notice of contest on the lienor at the address
shown in the claim of lien… and shall certify to such service and the
date of service on the face of the notice and record the
notice.”**[jimersonfirm.com](https://www.jimersonfirm.com/blog/2024/06/contesting-construction-lien-claims-contractors-beware-of-the-notice-of-contest-of-lien-during-litigation/#:~:text=,services%20as%20provided%20by%20law)
In practice, this means the clerk’s office will send a certified copy of
the notice (often by certified mail, return receipt requested) to the
lienor’s address as given on the lien document. The clerk then stamps
the recorded notice with a certificate indicating when and how it was
served. This clerk-issued certificate and service date are crucial: the
60-day period runs from that certified **date of
service**[jimersonfirm.com](https://www.jimersonfirm.com/blog/2024/06/contesting-construction-lien-claims-contractors-beware-of-the-notice-of-contest-of-lien-during-litigation/#:~:text=,service%2C%20the%20clerk%20shall%20serve).
If the lienor is not properly served, the clock does not start.

Florida courts have held that **as long as the clerk follows the
statutory service procedure, due process is satisfied**. Notably, the
statute does *not* require that the owner or clerk serve the lienor’s
attorney, if any – only the lienor at the address in the claim of
lien[caselaw.findlaw.com](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/fl-district-court-of-appeal/1872693.html#:~:text=The%20homeowners%20maintain%20that%2C%20when,See%20id)[caselaw.findlaw.com](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/fl-district-court-of-appeal/1872693.html#:~:text=No%20one%20disputes%20the%20homeowners,We%20reject%20those%20arguments).
In *Rabil*, the contractor’s attorney argued it was unfair that he was
not separately notified of the contest since the case was in litigation,
and that the notice form did not explicitly warn the contractor about
needing to sue the bond
surety[caselaw.findlaw.com](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/fl-district-court-of-appeal/1872693.html#:~:text=homeowners%27%20failure%20to%20send%20copies,this%20order%2C%20the%20homeowners%20appeal)[caselaw.findlaw.com](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/fl-district-court-of-appeal/1872693.html#:~:text=No%20one%20disputes%20the%20homeowners,We%20reject%20those%20arguments).
The Fourth DCA squarely rejected these due process challenges. The court
noted that the owners **“followed the statutory scheme. They posted a
lien transfer bond and recorded a notice of contest of the lien. The
clerk properly noticed the contractor \[at the lien’s address\]. Because
the contractor did not file suit against the surety within sixty days,
the lien was automatically extinguished by operation of
law.”**[caselaw.findlaw.com](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/fl-district-court-of-appeal/1872693.html#:~:text=those%20arguments)
The statutory method of mailing to the lienor’s address was deemed
sufficient notice, even if the lienor’s counsel or corporate officers
weren’t personally
served[caselaw.findlaw.com](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/fl-district-court-of-appeal/1872693.html#:~:text=which%20it%20did).
In short, Chapter 713 spells out exactly how notice must be given, and
compliance with those steps meets any due process
requirement[caselaw.findlaw.com](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/fl-district-court-of-appeal/1872693.html#:~:text=No%20one%20disputes%20the%20homeowners,We%20reject%20those%20arguments).
The courts have emphasized that they **“must strictly construe”** the
statute and cannot add extra notice requirements beyond what the
legislature
provided[caselaw.findlaw.com](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/fl-district-court-of-appeal/1872693.html#:~:text=No%20one%20disputes%20the%20homeowners,We%20reject%20those%20arguments).

**Service pitfalls:** The common issues here usually arise from either
the lienor’s failure to receive/accept the mailed notice or the owner’s
failure to ensure the clerk had the correct lienor address. In *Rabil*,
the contractor claimed that although the notice was mailed, it was
signed for by a secretary and never made it to management or counsel
before the 60 days
ran[caselaw.findlaw.com](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/fl-district-court-of-appeal/1872693.html#:~:text=which%20it%20did).
The court was unmoved: the statute requires service on the lienor (not
necessarily actual knowledge by a particular person), and the risk of
internal mishandling lies with the lienor. Similarly, if a lienor
changes address or goes out of business after recording the lien,
service to the last address of record is still effective – lienors are
responsible for keeping their address updated in any recorded lien
amendments or satisfactions. Owners should, however, exercise caution if
they know the lienor’s listed address is incorrect (e.g. a typo on the
lien). While not legally required, an owner in such a case might choose
to send a courtesy copy of the notice to any other known address or to
the lienor’s attorney to avoid later claims of non-receipt. The statute
doesn’t mandate this, but it can provide practical protection. Indeed,
the trial judge in *Rabil* was concerned the owners hadn’t sent copies
directly to the contractor’s
lawyer[caselaw.findlaw.com](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/fl-district-court-of-appeal/1872693.html#:~:text=At%20the%20hearing%20on%20the,this%20order%2C%20the%20homeowners%20appeal),
though the appellate court made clear no such service is
required[caselaw.findlaw.com](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/fl-district-court-of-appeal/1872693.html#:~:text=No%20one%20disputes%20the%20homeowners,We%20reject%20those%20arguments).
The lesson is that **the clerk’s certified mail to the lienor’s address
is the critical service** – owners must ensure the clerk does it, and
lienors must monitor their mail.

From a due process standpoint, Florida courts have upheld §713.22(2)
against constitutional attack. The procedure of mailing a notice and
cutting off lien rights in 60 days is viewed as a legislative balance
between lienors’ rights and owners’ property rights. The lienor has
already received notice (often via a Notice to Owner or other prelien
steps) and had an opportunity to secure their lien; the contest simply
accelerates the enforcement requirement. In *Rabil*, the contractor
argued the statute was unconstitutional because it did not require
informing the lienor of the need to sue the surety or involve the court
in discharging the
lien[caselaw.findlaw.com](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/fl-district-court-of-appeal/1872693.html#:~:text=No%20one%20disputes%20the%20homeowners,We%20reject%20those%20arguments).
The Fourth DCA dismissed this, citing longstanding precedent that lien
laws are strictly statutory and create no rights beyond those given on
paper[caselaw.findlaw.com](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/fl-district-court-of-appeal/1872693.html#:~:text=that%20the%20lienor%20be%20informed,We%20reject%20those%20arguments).
So long as the statutory notice was sent, the lienor’s failure to act is
on them. Other courts have noted that a lienor who loses a lien for want
of timely action is **“not left without a remedy”** – they can still sue
in contract, but **“may no longer encumber \[the owner’s\] property”**
after the statutory time
lapses[caselaw.findlaw.com](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/court/fl-district-court-of-appeal/1731298.html#:~:text=lien%20lapsed%20when%20the%20lienor,3d%20DCA%201991).
This underscores that the Notice of Contest procedure is considered
fair: it doesn’t erase the debt, only the lien, and it gives the lienor
a clear chance (60 days) to assert their claim in court. In practice,
then, an owner need not fear that a minor service technicality will
unravel a contest notice, as long as the clerk did what the statute
says. Conversely, a lienor cannot evade the 60-day deadline by claiming
“I didn’t actually see the notice” if the record shows it was sent
correctly. Due process is satisfied by adherence to the statute’s
service provisions, and courts will enforce the consequences
strictly[caselaw.findlaw.com](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/fl-district-court-of-appeal/1872693.html#:~:text=The%20legislature%20has%20provided%20a,LLC%2C%2084).

**Ensuring proper service:** For owners and their counsel, a key step
after recording a Notice of Contest is to **verify the clerk’s
service**. Obtain the certified mail tracking or a copy of the clerk’s
certificate of service to confirm when and where it went. If the clerk
errs (e.g. mails to a wrong address or delays sending), be prepared to
argue the 60 days should count from actual service date or to re-serve
if possible. Fortunately, such clerk errors are rare. Lienors, for their
part, should treat any received notice with utmost urgency – even if it
comes to an unexpected address or looks unfamiliar. The notice might not
spell out every implication (for instance, it may not explicitly mention
a bond or list all steps to enforce), but courts expect lienors to know
the law or seek counsel immediately upon receiving a contest
notice[floridaconstructionlegalupdates.com](https://www.floridaconstructionlegalupdates.com/notice-of-contest-of-lien-is-a-powerful-tool/#:~:text=As%20an%20example%20of%20the,appeal%2C%20the%20Fourth%20District%20reversed)[jimersonfirm.com](https://www.jimersonfirm.com/blog/2024/06/contesting-construction-lien-claims-contractors-beware-of-the-notice-of-contest-of-lien-during-litigation/#:~:text=If%2C%20as%20was%20the%20case,lien%20and%20or%20bond%20rights).
The bottom line is that the statutory mailing is both necessary and
sufficient: everyone involved should focus on that mailing and the
ticking clock it starts.

## Interaction with Show-Cause Proceedings and Transfer-to-Bond

Florida law gives owners *multiple tools* to hasten the resolution of a
lien, and the Notice of Contest is one of them. Two other related
mechanisms are often discussed in tandem: the **show-cause procedure**
under §713.21(4) and the **transfer of lien to a security/bond** under
§713.24. Courts have addressed how the Notice of Contest interacts with
these procedures.

**Contest vs. Show Cause:** Section 713.21(4) allows an owner to file a
motion (in court) requiring a lienor to **show cause within 20 days**
why the lien should not be enforced or else be discharged. This is
essentially a fast-track judicial summons to prompt a foreclosure suit.
It is an alternative to the Notice of Contest, which is a non-judicial,
clerk-driven process giving 60 days. An owner may choose either route
(but typically not both at the same time). In either case, the lienor
must act by the deadline or lose the lien. Case law indicates that the
20-day show-cause period is even more **“unyielding”** than the 60-day
contest period, since it’s shorter and
court-supervised[floridabar.org](https://www.floridabar.org/the-florida-bar-journal/special-statutory-proceedings-for-the-discharge-of-construction-liens/#:~:text=statutory%20proceedings,)[floridabar.org](https://www.floridabar.org/the-florida-bar-journal/special-statutory-proceedings-for-the-discharge-of-construction-liens/#:~:text=Very%20recently%2C%20the%20Fourth%20District,%E2%80%9D).
Courts treat show-cause proceedings as special and **strictly enforce
the 20-day limit**, without extensions for things like obtaining counsel
or procedural
motions[floridabar.org](https://www.floridabar.org/the-florida-bar-journal/special-statutory-proceedings-for-the-discharge-of-construction-liens/#:~:text=In%20a%20series%20of%20rulings%2C,to%20discharge%20the%20lien%2C%20stating)[floridabar.org](https://www.floridabar.org/the-florida-bar-journal/special-statutory-proceedings-for-the-discharge-of-construction-liens/#:~:text=In%20Matrix%2C%20the%20lienor%20was,%E2%80%9D).
For example, in *Matrix Construction Corp. v. Mecca Construction*, a
lienor sought more time to get new counsel during a show-cause, but the
Third DCA held no enlargement of time was permitted – the lien was
discharged when the 20 days ran with no
action[floridabar.org](https://www.floridabar.org/the-florida-bar-journal/special-statutory-proceedings-for-the-discharge-of-construction-liens/#:~:text=In%20a%20series%20of%20rulings%2C,to%20discharge%20the%20lien%2C%20stating)[floridabar.org](https://www.floridabar.org/the-florida-bar-journal/special-statutory-proceedings-for-the-discharge-of-construction-liens/#:~:text=In%20Matrix%2C%20the%20lienor%20was,itself%20unless%20the%20rules%20provide).
The rules of civil procedure explicitly yield to the statute’s strict
timeline in such special
proceedings[floridabar.org](https://www.floridabar.org/the-florida-bar-journal/special-statutory-proceedings-for-the-discharge-of-construction-liens/#:~:text=The%20procedures%20of%20the%20above,established%20by%20the%20legislature%20and)[floridabar.org](https://www.floridabar.org/the-florida-bar-journal/special-statutory-proceedings-for-the-discharge-of-construction-liens/#:~:text=statutory%20proceedings,).
Thus, whether an owner gives a lienor 20 days (via show cause) or 60
days (via contest), the courts will hold the lienor to those deadlines
rigidly. The two procedures can work in sequence: if an owner first
contests a lien (60 days) and the lienor timely files suit, the owner
might still later use a show-cause motion within that suit to push the
case to conclusion if it’s languishing. However, once a lienor has filed
the foreclosure action, a §713.21(4) show-cause order is generally
unnecessary, as the matter will proceed as a normal lawsuit (subject to
standard litigation timelines or any court management). In any event,
both tools share the purpose of expediting lien enforcement, and courts
have repeatedly affirmed that both must be applied as written, **“not
subject to the ordinary exercise of judicial discretion”** to extend
times[floridabar.org](https://www.floridabar.org/the-florida-bar-journal/special-statutory-proceedings-for-the-discharge-of-construction-liens/#:~:text=certain%20other%20groups%2C%20the%20special,)[floridabar.org](https://www.floridabar.org/the-florida-bar-journal/special-statutory-proceedings-for-the-discharge-of-construction-liens/#:~:text=subject%20to%20the%20ordinary%20exercise,).

**Transfer to bond & contest:** Many of the modern cases involve liens
that have been **transferred to a bond or other security**. Under
§713.24, an owner (or contractor or other interested party) can
transfer a lien from the real property to a replacement security
(usually a surety bond or cash deposit) by filing the bond with the
clerk. The lien then attaches to the bond, and the property is freed of
the
lien[law.justia.com](https://law.justia.com/cases/florida/second-district-court-of-appeal/2024/23-1368.html#:~:text=transferred%20,subsection%20continues%3A%20If%20a%20proceeding)[law.justia.com](https://law.justia.com/cases/florida/second-district-court-of-appeal/2024/23-1368.html#:~:text=713.24%284%29%20provides%20that%20where%20,Construing%20these%20two).
Importantly, if a lien is transferred *after* a foreclosure lawsuit has
been filed, §713.24(4) provides that the lienor has one year from the
transfer to sue on the bond **“unless otherwise shortened by operation
of
law.”**[law.justia.com](https://law.justia.com/cases/florida/second-district-court-of-appeal/2024/23-1368.html#:~:text=713.24%284%29%20provides%20that%20where%20,Construing%20these%20two).
The phrase “unless otherwise shortened by operation of law” directly
incorporates the Notice of Contest
procedure[law.justia.com](https://law.justia.com/cases/florida/second-district-court-of-appeal/2024/23-1368.html#:~:text=statutes%20together%2C%20this%20court%20has,shall%20be%20extinguished).
In other words, an owner who bonds off a lien can **also record a Notice
of Contest of Lien to shorten the time to sue on that bond to 60 days**,
just as if it were still a lien against the
property[law.justia.com](https://law.justia.com/cases/florida/second-district-court-of-appeal/2024/23-1368.html#:~:text=time,3d%20at%20275)[law.justia.com](https://law.justia.com/cases/florida/second-district-court-of-appeal/2024/23-1368.html#:~:text=section%20713,the%20right%20to%20make%20a).
The Second DCA in *Hiller v. Phoenix* confirmed this interplay, holding
that a notice of contest under §713.22(2) **“allows the owner to shorten
the time period to commence an action on the security by filing a notice
of contest,”** notwithstanding the transfer to
bond[law.justia.com](https://law.justia.com/cases/florida/second-district-court-of-appeal/2024/23-1368.html#:~:text=statutes%20together%2C%20this%20court%20has,shall%20be%20extinguished)[law.justia.com](https://law.justia.com/cases/florida/second-district-court-of-appeal/2024/23-1368.html#:~:text=period%20to%20commence%20an%20action,3d%20935%2C%20937%20%28Fla).
Likewise, the Fourth DCA in *Rabil* noted that the homeowners posted a
transfer bond *and* recorded a notice of contest, and because the
contractor did not sue the surety in time, **“the lien was automatically
extinguished by operation of
law.”**[law.justia.com](https://law.justia.com/cases/florida/second-district-court-of-appeal/2024/23-1368.html#:~:text=has%20,lien%20was%20automatically%20extinguished%20by).
The procedural result is that the clerk must return or release the bond
if requested, since no timely action was brought against
it[law.justia.com](https://law.justia.com/cases/florida/second-district-court-of-appeal/2024/23-1368.html#:~:text=transferred%20,subsection%20continues%3A%20If%20a%20proceeding)[caselaw.findlaw.com](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/fl-district-court-of-appeal/1872693.html#:~:text=The%20legislature%20has%20provided%20a,LLC%2C%2084).

Several cases illustrate how this works in practice. In *Cool Guys, LLC
v. Jomar Properties* (2012), a lien was transferred to a bond during
pending foreclosure litigation. The Fourth DCA held that §713.24(4)
**“unambiguously”** requires the lienor to still sue the surety within
60 days if a contest is recorded, even though a suit against the owner
was already
ongoing[caselaw.findlaw.com](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/court/fl-district-court-of-appeal/1731298.html#:~:text=It%20is%20undisputed%20that%20Phoenix,include%20the%20requirement%20that%20an).
A pre-existing lawsuit does not excuse compliance with the shortened
deadline – the lienor must take the additional step of bringing the bond
into the action (or filing a new action on the bond) within that
period[caselaw.findlaw.com](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/court/fl-district-court-of-appeal/1731298.html#:~:text=It%20is%20undisputed%20that%20Phoenix,include%20the%20requirement%20that%20an).
In *Hiller*, the lienor’s argument that its prior lawsuit should count
indefinitely was rejected, especially given the legislature’s 2005
amendment to §713.24(4) adding the very language that mandates a timely
action against the
bond[caselaw.findlaw.com](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/court/fl-district-court-of-appeal/1731298.html#:~:text=Cool%20Guys%2C%20LLC%2C%2084%20So,%E2%80%9D).
The Second DCA noted that the trial court’s reliance on an older Supreme
Court case (*Davis Water & Waste*, 1979) was misplaced, since the
statute had changed to eliminate any “open-ended” ability to join the
surety
late[caselaw.findlaw.com](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/court/fl-district-court-of-appeal/1731298.html#:~:text=Cool%20Guys%2C%20LLC%2C%2084%20So,%E2%80%9D)[caselaw.findlaw.com](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/court/fl-district-court-of-appeal/1731298.html#:~:text=,is%20of%20course%20allowable%2C%20but).
In fact, Florida courts had decades earlier reached a similar conclusion
under the prior statute: in *Vic Tanny of Fla., Inc. v. Fred McGilvray,
Inc.* (1977), the Third DCA reversed a judgment of foreclosure because
the lienor sued the owner but failed to add the bond surety within the
then-allowed 60 days after a contest, causing the lien to
lapse[caselaw.findlaw.com](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/court/fl-district-court-of-appeal/1731298.html#:~:text=Jack%20Stilson%20%26%20Co,transferred%20security%20in%20order%20to).
This consistency in case law underscores that a lien transfer bond does
**not** protect the lienor from the effects of a Notice of Contest; the
lienor must be equally vigilant to sue on the bond in 60 days or lose
the
claim[law.justia.com](https://law.justia.com/cases/florida/second-district-court-of-appeal/2024/23-1368.html#:~:text=%28,Hall%20thereafter%20filed%20its)[law.justia.com](https://law.justia.com/cases/florida/second-district-court-of-appeal/2024/23-1368.html#:~:text=Lien%20to%20the%20bond%20and,transfer%20their%20lien%20during%20this).

**Strategic use of remedies:** Owners often employ a combination of
methods to dispose of liens. Posting a **transfer bond** is a popular
tactic because it immediately clears the title of the property (useful
for sales or
refinancing)[caselaw.findlaw.com](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/court/fl-district-court-of-appeal/1731298.html#:~:text=unjust%20enrichment)[caselaw.findlaw.com](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/court/fl-district-court-of-appeal/1731298.html#:~:text=After%20Phoenix%20commenced%20the%20action,filed%20a%20motion%20for%20leave).
But transferring alone doesn’t hurry the lawsuit; that’s where the
Notice of Contest comes in. By recording a contest notice right after
transferring the lien to bond, the owner forces the lienor to advance
its case quickly against the bond. If the lienor is dilatory, the bond
will be released in 60 days and the owner (or surety) won’t have to deal
with the claim at
all[caselaw.findlaw.com](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/fl-district-court-of-appeal/1872693.html#:~:text=The%20legislature%20has%20provided%20a,LLC%2C%2084).
Essentially, the owner can **stack** the benefits: the property is free
of the lien (thanks to the bond) and the bond itself will evaporate in
short order if the lienor doesn’t act. In *Jon M. Hall Co. v. Canoe
Creek* (2024), this sequence happened twice – the owner bonded off an
original lien and contested it, then the contractor filed an amended
(increased) lien which the owner also bonded off and contested during
the
litigation[law.justia.com](https://law.justia.com/cases/florida/second-district-court-of-appeal/2024/23-1368.html#:~:text=all%20work%20under%20the%20contract%3B,Hall%27s)[law.justia.com](https://law.justia.com/cases/florida/second-district-court-of-appeal/2024/23-1368.html#:~:text=contest%20of%20Hall%27s%20Original%20Claim,Canoe%20Creek%20moved%20for%20partial).
The contractor failed to sue the surety in time for either bond. The
Second DCA (on certiorari review) upheld the trial court’s ruling that
the lien was extinguished, finding no error in the application of
§§ 713.22 and
713.24[law.justia.com](https://law.justia.com/cases/florida/second-district-court-of-appeal/2024/23-1368.html#:~:text=,to%20provide%20construction%20and%20other)[law.justia.com](https://law.justia.com/cases/florida/second-district-court-of-appeal/2024/23-1368.html#:~:text=demonstrated%20any%20departure%2C%20we%20deny,following%20month%2C%20on%20May%2017).
The court explicitly wrote to “discuss \[the\] contention that the trial
court misconstrued sections 713.22 and 713.24” and concluded the
statutes were applied
correctly[law.justia.com](https://law.justia.com/cases/florida/second-district-court-of-appeal/2024/23-1368.html#:~:text=demonstrated%20any%20departure%2C%20we%20deny,following%20month%2C%20on%20May%2017)[law.justia.com](https://law.justia.com/cases/florida/second-district-court-of-appeal/2024/23-1368.html#:~:text=Canoe%20Creek%20was%20untimely,enforce%20any%20claim%20of%20lien).
The clear message: **owners may simultaneously use §713.24 and
§713.22(2)**, and lienors must pursue the substituted security just as
rapidly as they would a normal lien
foreclosure[law.justia.com](https://law.justia.com/cases/florida/second-district-court-of-appeal/2024/23-1368.html#:~:text=notice%20of%20contest%20of%20the,in%20Hiller%2C%20Canoe%20Creek%20transferred)[law.justia.com](https://law.justia.com/cases/florida/second-district-court-of-appeal/2024/23-1368.html#:~:text=or%20claim%20against%20a%20bond,in%20Hiller%2C%20Canoe%20Creek%20transferred).
Failing that, the lienor forfeits both the lien on the property and the
claim against the
bond[law.justia.com](https://law.justia.com/cases/florida/second-district-court-of-appeal/2024/23-1368.html#:~:text=notice%20of%20contest%20of%20the,in%20Hiller%2C%20Canoe%20Creek%20transferred)[law.justia.com](https://law.justia.com/cases/florida/second-district-court-of-appeal/2024/23-1368.html#:~:text=Hall%27s%20failure%20to%20seek%20to,why%20the%20statutory%20language%20as).

One nuance is that if a lienor files suit *before* the lien is
transferred to bond (as is often the case), and then the owner contests
the lien post-transfer, the lienor should **amend the pending
complaint** to add a count against the bond (and name the surety) within
the 60
days[linkedin.com](https://www.linkedin.com/pulse/sixty-days-means-court-reiterates-construction-lien-law-thomas-wert#:~:text=Phoenix%20did%20not%20immediately%20move,At%20the%20hearing%20on)[linkedin.com](https://www.linkedin.com/pulse/sixty-days-means-court-reiterates-construction-lien-law-thomas-wert#:~:text=against%20the%20surety%20within%2060,amended%20the%20Florida%20Statute%20concerning).
Filing a brand new lawsuit is not necessary if one is already pending;
an amendment will suffice as long as it is done timely and properly.
However, a late amendment (after 60 days) will be futile – courts have
held it cannot relate back to avoid the
deadline[linkedin.com](https://www.linkedin.com/pulse/sixty-days-means-court-reiterates-construction-lien-law-thomas-wert#:~:text=Phoenix%20did%20not%20immediately%20move,At%20the%20hearing%20on)[linkedin.com](https://www.linkedin.com/pulse/sixty-days-means-court-reiterates-construction-lien-law-thomas-wert#:~:text=against%20the%20surety%20within%2060,amended%20the%20Florida%20Statute%20concerning).
The lienor in *Hiller* learned this the hard way: its post-deadline
motion to supplement the complaint was ultimately moot because the bond
had vanished by operation of
law[caselaw.findlaw.com](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/court/fl-district-court-of-appeal/1731298.html#:~:text=During%20a%20hearing%20on%20these,Davis%20Water%20%26%20Waste)[caselaw.findlaw.com](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/court/fl-district-court-of-appeal/1731298.html#:~:text=bond%20would%20extinguish%20automatically%20by,motion%20for%20return%20of%20the).
Similarly, if multiple liens or amended liens are involved, each
transfer and contest will impose its own 60-day fuse for enforcement
against the respective
security[law.justia.com](https://law.justia.com/cases/florida/second-district-court-of-appeal/2024/23-1368.html#:~:text=against%20the%20surety%20within%20sixty,Claim%20of%20Lien%20to%20bond)[law.justia.com](https://law.justia.com/cases/florida/second-district-court-of-appeal/2024/23-1368.html#:~:text=Claim%20of%20Lien,Amended%20Claim%20of%20Lien%2C%20asserting).
Owners should be mindful to contest **each lien or significant
amendment** to avoid any argument that a new one-year period applies.
But per *Jack Stilson*, an amended lien doesn’t extend the time – a
single Notice of Contest can accelerate all related lien claims by that
lienor on the
project[floridaconstructionlegalupdates.com](https://www.floridaconstructionlegalupdates.com/an-amended-lien-does-not-delay-the-60-day-window-to-foreclose-a-lien-when-a-notice-of-contest-of-lien-is-recorded/#:~:text=This%20exact%20scenario%20was%20addressed,Contest%20of%20Lien%20is%20recorded).

In sum, Florida courts have harmonized the lien-contest statute and the
bond-transfer statute to give owners a powerful one-two punch. The case
law (from *Vic Tanny* in 1977 up through *Hall* in 2024) consistently
holds that if a lien is transferred during an enforcement proceeding,
the owner can **“shorten the time…to commence an action to enforce
any…claim against a bond”** to 60 days via a contest
notice[law.justia.com](https://law.justia.com/cases/florida/second-district-court-of-appeal/2024/23-1368.html#:~:text=section%20713,3d%20935%2C%20937%20%28Fla)[law.justia.com](https://law.justia.com/cases/florida/second-district-court-of-appeal/2024/23-1368.html#:~:text=bond%20during%20litigation%2C%20and%20the,contractor%20did%20not%20file%20suit).
If the lienor fails to meet that deadline, **the security is released
and the lien claim is
dead**[caselaw.findlaw.com](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/fl-district-court-of-appeal/1872693.html#:~:text=The%20legislature%20has%20provided%20a,LLC%2C%2084).
The judiciary’s only role is enforcing these outcomes; courts will not
intervene to give lenience because the statutes are considered
self-executing in this
regard[caselaw.findlaw.com](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/court/fl-district-court-of-appeal/1731298.html#:~:text=filing%20an%20action%20against%20a,Inc)[caselaw.findlaw.com](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/court/fl-district-court-of-appeal/1731298.html#:~:text=the%20transfer%20results%20in%20the,%E2%80%9D).
Whether via a 20-day show-cause order or a 60-day contest of lien, the
theme is the same: Chapter 713’s special procedures are intended to
**speedily discharge liens** if lienors do not timely bring them to
court[caselaw.findlaw.com](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/court/fl-district-court-of-appeal/1731298.html#:~:text=proceeding%2C%20namely%20that%20owners%20and,%E2%80%9D)[floridabar.org](https://www.floridabar.org/the-florida-bar-journal/special-statutory-proceedings-for-the-discharge-of-construction-liens/#:~:text=certain%20other%20groups%2C%20the%20special,).
As one court observed, **“a fundamental purpose”** of these provisions
is the **“speedy determination of mechanic’s lien
claims.”**[caselaw.findlaw.com](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/court/fl-district-court-of-appeal/1731298.html#:~:text=proceeding%2C%20namely%20that%20owners%20and,%E2%80%9D)
Owners can freely choose the method best suited to their needs
(administrative contest vs. judicial show-cause vs. bonding off), and
even combine them, to achieve a prompt resolution. Lienors,
consequently, must be prepared to prosecute their claims on very short
notice or risk losing the security for their claim.

## Practical Guidance for Owners, Lienors, and Lenders

Florida’s case law on Notices of Contest of Lien reveals a clear trend:
**courts strictly enforce the statute’s technical requirements and
deadlines** to the letter. Both owners and lienors can take away
valuable lessons and preventative strategies from these decisions:

  - **Owners/Developers:** A Notice of Contest of Lien is a **powerful
    and often decisive tool** to remove clouded title. To use it
    effectively, owners should ensure absolute compliance with
    §713.22(2)’s procedure. This means using the correct statutory form
    (or the clerk’s form), accurately identifying the lien and property,
    and recording the notice in the proper county. Double-check that the
    lienor’s name and address on the notice match the lien. Pay the
    clerk’s service fee and verify the **certificate of service**—the
    60-day clock doesn’t start until the lienor is
    served[jimersonfirm.com](https://www.jimersonfirm.com/blog/2024/06/contesting-construction-lien-claims-contractors-beware-of-the-notice-of-contest-of-lien-during-litigation/#:~:text=,services%20as%20provided%20by%20law).
    It’s wise to calendar the deadline a few days earlier than 60 days
    out, so that if the lienor *does* file a last-minute suit, you can
    immediately confirm whether they met the deadline. If the lienor
    fails to act by day 60, the lien is extinguished without further
    ado[caselaw.findlaw.com](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/fl-district-court-of-appeal/1460985.html#:~:text=to%20enforce%20a%20lien%20not,without%20intervention%20of%20the%20court%E2%80%9D).
    In that event, an owner should be prepared to obtain a discharge of
    lien or an order releasing any transfer bond from the court, if the
    lienor refuses to acknowledge the lien’s death. Owners who prevail
    in having a lien extinguished are generally entitled to recover
    their attorneys’ fees under the Lien Law (as prevailing parties
    under §713.29), so there is an added deterrent against lienors
    trying to prolong an invalid lien claim. In short, owners should not
    hesitate to invoke a Notice of Contest for stale or suspect liens –
    **but must do so meticulously**. Minor mistakes (wrong form,
    recording error, etc.) can nullify the effort. When in doubt,
    consulting an experienced construction lawyer or the clerk’s office
    for the proper form is advisable before recording.

  - **Contractors/Lienors:** From the lienor’s perspective, the moment a
    Notice of Contest arrives (or is even suspected – e.g., you receive
    a clerk’s certified mail), **immediate action** is required. Do
    **not** ignore or set aside the notice, even if you think it’s
    defective or believe your ongoing lawsuit covers
    it[floridaconstructionlegalupdates.com](https://www.floridaconstructionlegalupdates.com/notice-of-contest-of-lien-is-a-powerful-tool/#:~:text=Contest%20of%20Lien%2C%20do%20NOT,Lien%20based%20on%20its%20utility)[jimersonfirm.com](https://www.jimersonfirm.com/blog/2024/06/contesting-construction-lien-claims-contractors-beware-of-the-notice-of-contest-of-lien-during-litigation/#:~:text=If%2C%20as%20was%20the%20case,lien%20and%20or%20bond%20rights).
    The safest course is to assume the clock is ticking and calculate
    the 60-day deadline. Contact your attorney right away; if you don’t
    have one, this is the time to get one. If you have already filed a
    foreclosure action, discuss with counsel whether you need to **amend
    the complaint** to add parties (like a bond surety) or add counts
    (for the bond) within the 60
    days[linkedin.com](https://www.linkedin.com/pulse/sixty-days-means-court-reiterates-construction-lien-law-thomas-wert#:~:text=Phoenix%20did%20not%20immediately%20move,At%20the%20hearing%20on)[linkedin.com](https://www.linkedin.com/pulse/sixty-days-means-court-reiterates-construction-lien-law-thomas-wert#:~:text=against%20the%20surety%20within%2060,amended%20the%20Florida%20Statute%20concerning).
    Do not rely on any “relation back” argument to save a late amendment
    – Florida courts have rejected that
    approach[caselaw.findlaw.com](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/court/fl-district-court-of-appeal/1731298.html#:~:text=It%20is%20undisputed%20that%20Phoenix,include%20the%20requirement%20that%20an)[caselaw.findlaw.com](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/court/fl-district-court-of-appeal/1731298.html#:~:text=others%20who%20rely%20on%20the,file%20the%20lien%20or%20suit).
    If you have *not* filed suit yet, you should aim to file well before
    the 60th day, not at the last minute. This leaves room to correct
    any issues (such as a missing contractor’s affidavit or wrong venue)
    that could otherwise doom your case. Remember that if you’re a
    contractor, you must also serve the owner a **final payment
    affidavit** at least 5 days before filing the foreclosure complaint,
    and that too must happen within the 60-day
    window[caselaw.findlaw.com](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/fl-district-court-of-appeal/1460985.html#:~:text=On%20December%205%2C%202000%2C%20the,court%20later%20dismissed%20the%20case)[caselaw.findlaw.com](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/fl-district-court-of-appeal/1460985.html#:~:text=Pierson%20contends%20that%20its%20suit,2000%20filing%20of%20the%20lawsuit).
    Failure to do so can extinguish your lien even if the lawsuit itself
    was timely. Also, be aware that **amending your lien for more money
    will not extend your time** if a contest has been
    filed[floridaconstructionlegalupdates.com](https://www.floridaconstructionlegalupdates.com/an-amended-lien-does-not-delay-the-60-day-window-to-foreclose-a-lien-when-a-notice-of-contest-of-lien-is-recorded/#:~:text=This%20exact%20scenario%20was%20addressed,Contest%20of%20Lien%20is%20recorded).
    The 60 days runs from the date of service of the notice on your
    original lien, and an amendment won’t reset the clock. In summary,
    lienors should diarize all deadlines and err on the side of
    **over-compliance** – file suit early, join all necessary parties,
    and make sure every precondition is met. This strict approach is
    necessary because Florida courts have shown no leniency for those
    who miss deadlines or omit required
    steps[caselaw.findlaw.com](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/fl-district-court-of-appeal/1872693.html#:~:text=The%20legislature%20has%20provided%20a,LLC%2C%2084)[caselaw.findlaw.com](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/court/fl-district-court-of-appeal/1731298.html#:~:text=%E2%80%9C,Bergeron).
    Finally, always keep your **address updated** on any recorded lien
    documents. If you move or have a different mailing address than what
    was on the lien, record an amendment or at least ensure forwarding
    of mail, so that a Notice of Contest will reach you or someone who
    will alert you. Many lienors have lost rights simply because the
    notice went to an unattended mailbox or a company agent who didn’t
    forward it in
    time[caselaw.findlaw.com](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/fl-district-court-of-appeal/1872693.html#:~:text=which%20it%20did).

  - **Lenders & Title Interests:** The 60-day contest mechanism is also
    important for construction lenders, title insurers, and property
    buyers. A recorded Notice of Contest puts third parties on notice
    that a lien’s lifespan has been truncated. For example, a lender
    considering a refinance or a buyer under contract should check the
    public records for any Notices of Contest, as they indicate that a
    lien may expire sooner than the normal one year. If 60 days have
    passed since service of a contest and no lawsuit was filed, the lien
    is *dead* by operation of
    law[caselaw.findlaw.com](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/fl-district-court-of-appeal/1872693.html#:~:text=The%20legislature%20has%20provided%20a,LLC%2C%2084)
    – though it’s prudent to obtain a formal discharge or court order to
    clear the record. Conversely, if a foreclosure action is filed
    within the 60 days, the lien remains alive (attached either to the
    property or a bond) and the title stakeholders should monitor that
    litigation. The overarching purpose of the contest provision is to
    give certainty to when lien claims end, which benefits lenders and
    buyers who need clear
    title[caselaw.findlaw.com](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/court/fl-district-court-of-appeal/1731298.html#:~:text=,is%20of%20course%20allowable%2C%20but)[linkedin.com](https://www.linkedin.com/pulse/sixty-days-means-court-reiterates-construction-lien-law-thomas-wert#:~:text=,file%20the%20lien%20or%20suit).
    Construction lenders can encourage their borrower clients (property
    owners) to use the Notice of Contest to eliminate lingering liens
    that aren’t being foreclosed, thereby improving the project’s title
    status. Additionally, if a lender or subsequent purchaser finds an
    old lien that the owner neglected to contest, they themselves (as
    parties with interest in the property) may be able to record a
    Notice of Contest to start the 60-day clock (the statute allows
    **“an owner or the owner’s agent or attorney”** to record the
    notice, and an argument could be made for an owner’s successor or
    other interested party to do
    so)[jimersonfirm.com](https://www.jimersonfirm.com/blog/2024/06/contesting-construction-lien-claims-contractors-beware-of-the-notice-of-contest-of-lien-during-litigation/#:~:text=,service%2C%20the%20clerk%20shall%20serve).
    All parties relying on title should be aware that once a contest is
    recorded, the lien’s expiration date is no longer a year from
    recording but only 60 days from service. This certainty is why
    Florida’s lien law is attractive to investors – it prevents old
    liens from stagnating
    indefinitely[caselaw.findlaw.com](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/court/fl-district-court-of-appeal/1731298.html#:~:text=,is%20of%20course%20allowable%2C%20but).

In conclusion, Florida courts have consistently reinforced that **strict
compliance and diligence are paramount** under the lien contest
framework. As one court aptly noted, **“owners and lienors alike should
strictly comply with all deadlines under Florida’s lien law. Otherwise,
they may be left without the lien law’s
protections.”**[linkedin.com](https://www.linkedin.com/pulse/sixty-days-means-court-reiterates-construction-lien-law-thomas-wert#:~:text=bond%20within%2060%20days%20of,without%20the%20lien%20law%E2%80%99s%20protections)
Each decision on Notices of Contest underscores a simple practice tip:
when in doubt, follow the statute to the letter and act sooner rather
than later. Owners should use the contest mechanism confidently but
carefully to purge unenforced liens, and lienors must treat any contest
notice as an urgent call to action. By heeding the lessons from these
cases – meticulous drafting, proper service, and unwavering adherence to
deadlines – parties can avoid the common missteps that lead to lost lien
rights or protracted legal battles. In the world of construction liens,
an old adage holds true: **“Time is of the essence.”** Florida’s courts
will enforce that literally, so all stakeholders are well-advised to
plan and act
accordingly[floridabar.org](https://www.floridabar.org/the-florida-bar-journal/special-statutory-proceedings-for-the-discharge-of-construction-liens/#:~:text=The%20safest%20practice%2C%20of%20course%2C,tested%20on%20a%20substantive%20basis)[floridabar.org](https://www.floridabar.org/the-florida-bar-journal/special-statutory-proceedings-for-the-discharge-of-construction-liens/#:~:text=certain%20other%20groups%2C%20the%20special,).
