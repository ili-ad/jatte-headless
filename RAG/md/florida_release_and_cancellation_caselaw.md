# Florida Construction Lien Release and Cancellation: Case Law Analysis

## Statutory Overview: §§ 713.20 and 713.21, Florida Statutes

Florida’s Construction Lien Law (Part I of Chapter 713) provides
specific rules for waiving, releasing, and discharging construction
liens on private projects. **Section 713.20** governs **waiver and
release of liens**, prohibiting advance waivers of lien rights and
providing statutory **forms** for partial (progress) and final lien
releases[syfert.com](https://syfert.com/florida/statutes/0713.20.html#:~:text=,made%20in%20advance%20is%20unenforceable)[syfert.com](https://syfert.com/florida/statutes/0713.20.html#:~:text=WAIVER%20AND%20RELEASE%20OF%20LIEN,UPON%20PROGRESS%20PAYMENT).
A lienor cannot be forced to use a non-statutory waiver form, and any
contractual clause requiring a different form is
unenforceable[jimersonfirm.com](https://www.jimersonfirm.com/blog/resources/faqs/construction-law/#:~:text=A%3A%20Many%20general%20contractors%20and,Once).
However, if a lienor **chooses** to sign a broader release, Florida law
will enforce it according to its
terms[floridabar.org](https://www.floridabar.org/the-florida-bar-journal/floridas-unwieldy-but-effective-construction-lien-law/#:~:text=admonition%20is%20circumvented%20by%20incorporating,70)[floridabar.org](https://www.floridabar.org/the-florida-bar-journal/floridas-unwieldy-but-effective-construction-lien-law/#:~:text=additional%20work%20performed%20but%20not,70).
**Section 713.21** addresses **discharge (cancellation) of liens**,
listing the exclusive methods to remove a lien: (1) recording a
satisfaction, (2) recording a release by the lienor, (3) lapse of time
(failure to foreclose within statutory deadlines), (4) a court’s order
in an expedited “show-cause” proceeding, or (5) a final judgment in a
lien foreclosure
suit[tabbaafirm.com](https://tabbaafirm.com/blog-panama-city-law-firm-the-tabbaa-firm/construction-liens/#:~:text=Discharge%20or%20cancellation%20of%20a,21)[tabbaafirm.com](https://tabbaafirm.com/blog-panama-city-law-firm-the-tabbaa-firm/construction-liens/#:~:text=,time%20prescribed%20in%20this%20part).
Both statutes are strictly construed, and parties must **strictly
comply** with their requirements for a lien to be
enforceable[floridabar.org](https://www.floridabar.org/the-florida-bar-journal/floridas-unwieldy-but-effective-construction-lien-law/#:~:text=The%20lien%20law%E2%80%99s%20strictest%20application,to%20a%20project%20to%20ascertain)[floridabar.org](https://www.floridabar.org/the-florida-bar-journal/floridas-unwieldy-but-effective-construction-lien-law/#:~:text=attached%20to%20it,of%20the%2090%20day%20period).
Below we examine key Florida cases interpreting these provisions, common
errors by lienors and owners, and practical implications for lien
enforceability, cancellation, and reinstatement.

## Notable Case Law on Lien Releases and Cancellations

### Calixte v. Coastal Building Contractors, LLC (Fla. 4th DCA 2024)

  - **Parties:** Homeowners (Calixte and Drouillard) vs. Subcontractor
    (Coastal Building Contractors).

  - **Issue:** Whether an owner can use the **§ 713.21(4)** “order to
    show cause” procedure to cancel a lien that the owner alleges was
    **not properly perfected** (subcontractor failed to serve the
    required Notice to Owner).

  - **Facts:** Coastal (a subcontractor) recorded a lien for unpaid
    work. The owners sued to **discharge the lien under § 713.21(4)**,
    alleging Coastal never served a Notice to Owner (a prerequisite
    under § 713.06 for subs), so the lien was
    invalid[floridaconstructionlegalupdates.com](https://www.floridaconstructionlegalupdates.com/owner-cant-pursue-statutory-show-cause-complaint-to-cancel-lien-fair-outcome/#:~:text=In%20Calixte%20v,21%20is)[floridaconstructionlegalupdates.com](https://www.floridaconstructionlegalupdates.com/owner-cant-pursue-statutory-show-cause-complaint-to-cancel-lien-fair-outcome/#:~:text=under%20Florida%20Statute%20s,4%29%20through%20the).
    The clerk issued a show-cause summons, but Coastal did not foreclose
    within 20 days. Instead, Coastal moved to quash the summons, arguing
    the statute’s prefatory language allows show-cause only for a “*lien
    properly perfected*” – and the owners’ own complaint said the lien
    was not
    perfected[floridaconstructionlegalupdates.com](https://www.floridaconstructionlegalupdates.com/owner-cant-pursue-statutory-show-cause-complaint-to-cancel-lien-fair-outcome/#:~:text=In%20Calixte%20v,21%20is)[floridaconstructionlegalupdates.com](https://www.floridaconstructionlegalupdates.com/owner-cant-pursue-statutory-show-cause-complaint-to-cancel-lien-fair-outcome/#:~:text=lien%20was%20not%20properly%20perfected%2C,2).

  - **Outcome:** The Fourth DCA agreed with the subcontractor and
    **refused to cancel the lien** under
    § 713.21(4)[hammerngavel.com](https://hammerngavel.com/blog/court-refuses-to-discharge-lien-where-subcontractor-failed-to-serve-notice-to-owner#:~:text=Calixte%20v,to%20respond%20to%20the%20Complaint)[hammerngavel.com](https://hammerngavel.com/blog/court-refuses-to-discharge-lien-where-subcontractor-failed-to-serve-notice-to-owner#:~:text=The%20court%20concluded%20by%20denying,the%20petition%2C%20stating%20that).
    Because the owners’ complaint asserted the lien was never perfected
    (due to no Notice to Owner), the court held the special show-cause
    procedure was not available: “As the complaint…alleged \[the
    subcontractor\] had failed to perfect its lien… and therefore had no
    lien rights, \[the owners\] could not avail themselves of the
    special statutory procedure authorized by
    section 713.21(4)”[hammerngavel.com](https://hammerngavel.com/blog/court-refuses-to-discharge-lien-where-subcontractor-failed-to-serve-notice-to-owner#:~:text=The%20court%20concluded%20by%20denying,the%20petition%2C%20stating%20that).
    The trial court properly vacated the show-cause summons.

  - **Judicial Reasoning:** Section 713.21 begins, “A lien **properly
    perfected** under this chapter may be discharged by any of the
    following methods….” The court emphasized this plain language and
    noted that for subcontractors, serving a Notice to Owner is
    **essential to perfecting lien
    rights**[hammerngavel.com](https://hammerngavel.com/blog/court-refuses-to-discharge-lien-where-subcontractor-failed-to-serve-notice-to-owner#:~:text=The%20court%20also%20noted%20that,%E2%80%9D)[hammerngavel.com](https://hammerngavel.com/blog/court-refuses-to-discharge-lien-where-subcontractor-failed-to-serve-notice-to-owner#:~:text=lien%20rights%2C%20,proper%20remedy%20under%20such%20circumstances).
    If the lien is alleged to be invalid from the start, the statute’s
    text literally excludes it from the show-cause
    process[caselaw.findlaw.com](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/court/fl-district-court-of-appeal/116114274.html#:~:text=By%20its%20plain%20language%2C%20section,the%20warnings%20contained%20in%20section)[caselaw.findlaw.com](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/court/fl-district-court-of-appeal/116114274.html#:~:text=satisfied%2C%20the%20lien%20claimant%20has,his%20or%20her%20lien%20rights).
    The Fourth DCA cited earlier precedents holding that **mandamus**
    will lie to compel discharge of a lien when the statute’s conditions
    are
    met[caselaw.findlaw.com](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/court/fl-district-court-of-appeal/116114274.html#:~:text=Mandamus%20lies%20to%20compel%20a,4th%20DCA%201980),
    but here those conditions were not met since the lien was not
    “properly perfected.”

  - **Common Errors:** *Owner’s error:* Pleading the lien’s invalidity
    within the §713.21(4) complaint itself. This **backfired** because
    it triggered the “properly perfected”
    restriction[hammerngavel.com](https://hammerngavel.com/blog/court-refuses-to-discharge-lien-where-subcontractor-failed-to-serve-notice-to-owner#:~:text=Calixte%20v,to%20respond%20to%20the%20Complaint)[hammerngavel.com](https://hammerngavel.com/blog/court-refuses-to-discharge-lien-where-subcontractor-failed-to-serve-notice-to-owner#:~:text=,proper%20remedy%20under%20such%20circumstances).
    The owner might have been better served by using a different tactic
    (e.g. a **Notice of Contest** to shorten the lien’s duration or
    bonding off the lien) instead of the show-cause
    process[floridaconstructionlegalupdates.com](https://www.floridaconstructionlegalupdates.com/owner-cant-pursue-statutory-show-cause-complaint-to-cancel-lien-fair-outcome/#:~:text=these%20other%20standard%20options%3F%C2%A0%20And,And%20here%20is%20what%20the)[floridaconstructionlegalupdates.com](https://www.floridaconstructionlegalupdates.com/owner-cant-pursue-statutory-show-cause-complaint-to-cancel-lien-fair-outcome/#:~:text=case%20does%20not%20discuss,in%20light%20of%20this%20ruling).
    *Lienor’s error:* Failing to serve a Notice to Owner (which made the
    lien vulnerable). However, the lienor’s strategic **defense** was
    pointing out the technical statutory limitation – an example of how
    lien statutes are a “strictly construed” procedural
    minefield[hammerngavel.com](https://hammerngavel.com/blog/court-refuses-to-discharge-lien-where-subcontractor-failed-to-serve-notice-to-owner#:~:text=This%20case%20is%20a%20reminder,law%20apply%20to%20all%20circumstances).

  - **Impact:** This case underscores that owners must use the **correct
    procedure** to clear an invalid lien. An owner **cannot use
    § 713.21(4)** to summarily cancel a facially invalid or
    unperfected
    lien[hammerngavel.com](https://hammerngavel.com/blog/court-refuses-to-discharge-lien-where-subcontractor-failed-to-serve-notice-to-owner#:~:text=lien%20rights%2C%20,proper%20remedy%20under%20such%20circumstances).
    Instead, the owner’s remedies are to either: (a) file a **Notice of
    Contest** (forcing foreclosure within 60 days) or (b) file a normal
    lawsuit for declaratory relief, fraudulent lien, or quiet title
    (which takes
    longer)[floridaconstructionlegalupdates.com](https://www.floridaconstructionlegalupdates.com/owner-cant-pursue-statutory-show-cause-complaint-to-cancel-lien-fair-outcome/#:~:text=The%20owner%20could%20record%20a,the%20head%20sooner%20than%20later)[floridaconstructionlegalupdates.com](https://www.floridaconstructionlegalupdates.com/owner-cant-pursue-statutory-show-cause-complaint-to-cancel-lien-fair-outcome/#:~:text=because%20it%20is%20still%20pursuing,leaves%20the%20owner%20that%20disputes).
    The outcome caused concern among construction lawyers, as it leaves
    an owner stuck with a cloud on title (the unperfected lien) unless
    the lienor voluntarily releases it or the owner pursues these other
    methods[floridaconstructionlegalupdates.com](https://www.floridaconstructionlegalupdates.com/owner-cant-pursue-statutory-show-cause-complaint-to-cancel-lien-fair-outcome/#:~:text=lien%2C%20by%20a%20judgment%2C%20or,to%20the%20security%20of%20the)[floridaconstructionlegalupdates.com](https://www.floridaconstructionlegalupdates.com/owner-cant-pursue-statutory-show-cause-complaint-to-cancel-lien-fair-outcome/#:~:text=these%20other%20standard%20options%3F%C2%A0%20And,allege%20the%20lien%20was%20not).
    The case may prompt legislative re-examination of the “properly
    perfected” wording, but as of now, **courts strictly enforce the
    statute’s
    text**[caselaw.findlaw.com](https://caselaw.findlaw.com/court/fl-district-court-of-appeal/116114274.html#:~:text=By%20its%20plain%20language%2C%20section,the%20warnings%20contained%20in%20section)[hammerngavel.com](https://hammerngavel.com/blog/court-refuses-to-discharge-lien-where-subcontractor-failed-to-serve-notice-to-owner#:~:text=no%20remedy%20when%20a%20lien,proper%20remedy%20under%20such%20circumstances).

### Unnerstall v. Designerick, Inc. (Fla. 2d DCA 2009)

  - **Parties:** Homeowners (Christopher & Lynette Unnerstall) vs.
    Contractor (Designerick, Inc.).

  - **Issue:** What constitutes a sufficient response by a lienor to a
    **§ 713.21(4)** show-cause summons? Specifically, does filing any
    counterclaim or answer avoid discharge, or must the lienor
    specifically file a **foreclosure action** on the lien?

  - **Facts:** Designerick (contractor) installed cabinetry and recorded
    a lien for an unpaid $21,985
    balance[law.justia.com](https://law.justia.com/cases/florida/second-district-court-of-appeal/2009/2d09-1764.html#:~:text=seek%20review%20of%20an%20order,Unnerstalls%20failed%20to%20pay%20the).
    The Unnerstalls filed a complaint under § 713.21(4) for an order to
    show cause why the lien should not be enforced or
    cancelled[law.justia.com](https://law.justia.com/cases/florida/second-district-court-of-appeal/2009/2d09-1764.html#:~:text=grant%20the%20petition,discharge%20the%20lien%2C%20Designerick%20served).
    Within 20 days, Designerick **answered** the complaint and filed
    counterclaims – but those counterclaims were for **breach of
    contract and unjust enrichment**, seeking money damages, and **did
    not include a lien foreclosure
    count**[law.justia.com](https://law.justia.com/cases/florida/second-district-court-of-appeal/2009/2d09-1764.html#:~:text=balance%20of%20%2421%2C985%20due%20for,1)[law.justia.com](https://law.justia.com/cases/florida/second-district-court-of-appeal/2009/2d09-1764.html#:~:text=order%20to%20preserve%20its%20lien,See).
    The trial court found this response sufficient and refused to
    discharge the lien, since Designerick had initiated counterclaims
    within the 20-day window. The owners petitioned for a writ of
    mandamus to compel discharge of the lien.

  - **Outcome:** The Second DCA **granted mandamus and ordered the lien
    discharged**[law.justia.com](https://law.justia.com/cases/florida/second-district-court-of-appeal/2009/2d09-1764.html#:~:text=with%20the%20statutory%20requirement%20that,4).
    The appellate court held that Designerick’s counterclaims *failed to
    strictly comply* with § 713.21(4) because they did not seek to
    **enforce the lien itself** (no foreclosure
    claim)[law.justia.com](https://law.justia.com/cases/florida/second-district-court-of-appeal/2009/2d09-1764.html#:~:text=of%20action%20did%20not%20preserve,direct%20the%20trial%20court%20to).
    Under the statute, if a property owner invokes the show-cause
    procedure, the lienor must **either** (a) file a lien foreclosure
    action (in court) within 20 days **or** (b) show good cause in that
    time why a foreclosure is not yet
    filed[law.justia.com](https://law.justia.com/cases/florida/second-district-court-of-appeal/2009/2d09-1764.html#:~:text=period%20why%20enforcement%20should%20not,Dic%20Construction).
    Merely filing other lawsuits or claims for money damages is
    insufficient to preserve the
    lien[law.justia.com](https://law.justia.com/cases/florida/second-district-court-of-appeal/2009/2d09-1764.html#:~:text=Designerick%20did%20not%20request%20foreclosure%2C,the%20lien%20be%20filed%20or).
    The court directed the trial court to cancel the lien for
    noncompliance[law.justia.com](https://law.justia.com/cases/florida/second-district-court-of-appeal/2009/2d09-1764.html#:~:text=with%20the%20statutory%20requirement%20that,4).

  - **Reasoning:** Section 713.21(4) demands **strict compliance** by
    the lienor once the owner triggers the 20-day show cause
    clock[law.justia.com](https://law.justia.com/cases/florida/second-district-court-of-appeal/2009/2d09-1764.html#:~:text=that%20,the%20lienor%20in%20Mainlands%20sought)[law.justia.com](https://law.justia.com/cases/florida/second-district-court-of-appeal/2009/2d09-1764.html#:~:text=owner%20invokes%20the%20statute%2C%20a,See).
    The court cited **Matrix Construction Corp. v. Mecca**, which
    explained that an owner’s show-cause complaint creates a short
    20-day window for the lienor “to assert by counterclaim or otherwise
    a foreclosure of the
    lien”[law.justia.com](https://law.justia.com/cases/florida/second-district-court-of-appeal/2009/2d09-1764.html#:~:text=period%20why%20enforcement%20should%20not,Dic%20Construction).
    It also relied on **Ruffolo v. Parish & Bowman, Inc.**, which held
    that a lienor must strictly follow the statute to preserve its lien
    rights[law.justia.com](https://law.justia.com/cases/florida/second-district-court-of-appeal/2009/2d09-1764.html#:~:text=owner%20invokes%20the%20statute%2C%20a,See).
    Importantly, the court distinguished *Mainlands Construction Co. v.
    Wen-Dic Constr. Co.*, 482 So. 2d 1369 (Fla. 1986). In *Mainlands*,
    the lienor’s counterclaim **did** include a foreclosure of the
    mechanic’s lien, which satisfied the
    statute[law.justia.com](https://law.justia.com/cases/florida/second-district-court-of-appeal/2009/2d09-1764.html#:~:text=%28Fla,the%20lienor%27s%20motion%20to%20arbitrate).
    By contrast, Designerick’s counterclaims only asserted **personal
    claims (breach of contract, etc.) without seeking to foreclose the
    lien**, so they **failed to commence an action to enforce the lien**
    as
    required[law.justia.com](https://law.justia.com/cases/florida/second-district-court-of-appeal/2009/2d09-1764.html#:~:text=%28Fla,the%20lienor%27s%20motion%20to%20arbitrate)[law.justia.com](https://law.justia.com/cases/florida/second-district-court-of-appeal/2009/2d09-1764.html#:~:text=Designerick%20did%20not%20request%20foreclosure%2C,the%20lien%20be%20filed%20or).
    The Second DCA also cited **Brookshire v. GP Construction of Palm
    Beach, Inc.**, 993 So. 2d 179 (Fla. 4th DCA 2008), where a lienor’s
    motion to compel arbitration (instead of filing a foreclosure
    action) *did not* meet the statutory requirement and the lien was
    discharged[law.justia.com](https://law.justia.com/cases/florida/second-district-court-of-appeal/2009/2d09-1764.html#:~:text=of%20action%20did%20not%20preserve,the%20lien%20be%20filed%20or).
    In sum, only a pleading that affirmatively seeks **foreclosure of
    the lien** (whether by counterclaim in the show-cause case or a
    separate enforcement action) will prevent
    cancellation[law.justia.com](https://law.justia.com/cases/florida/second-district-court-of-appeal/2009/2d09-1764.html#:~:text=order%20to%20preserve%20its%20lien,See)[law.justia.com](https://law.justia.com/cases/florida/second-district-court-of-appeal/2009/2d09-1764.html#:~:text=Designerick%20did%20not%20request%20foreclosure%2C,the%20lien%20be%20filed%20or).

  - **Common Errors:** *Lienor’s error:* Filing an answer or
    counterclaims **that omit a foreclosure count**. Designerick
    mistakenly believed that any counterclaim within 20 days would
    suffice, but the statute requires action “to enforce the lien.”
    Florida courts consistently reject **“creative” or incomplete
    responses** – e.g. motions to arbitrate, generic answers, or claims
    for money damages – as
    noncompliant[law.justia.com](https://law.justia.com/cases/florida/second-district-court-of-appeal/2009/2d09-1764.html#:~:text=Designerick%20did%20not%20request%20foreclosure%2C,the%20lien%20be%20filed%20or)[law.justia.com](https://law.justia.com/cases/florida/second-district-court-of-appeal/2009/2d09-1764.html#:~:text=Brookshire%2C%20993%20So,and%20DAKAN%2C%20STEPHEN%20L).
    *Owner’s action:* The owners correctly utilized the show-cause
    procedure and then persistently sought mandamus when the trial court
    hesitated. The common *owner error* to avoid, as illustrated in
    Brookshire and this case, is giving the lienor the benefit of the
    doubt; instead, owners should insist on strict adherence to the
    20-day deadline.

  - **Impact on Lien Validity:** This line of cases (including
    *Unnerstall*, *Brookshire*, **Dracon Constr., Inc. v. Facility
    Const. Mgmt., Inc.**, 828 So. 2d 1069 (Fla. 4th DCA 2002)
    (bare-bones answer insufficient), and others) sends a clear message:
    in a § 713.21(4) proceeding, **any failure by the lienor to timely
    plead a proper foreclosure action means automatic cancellation of
    the
    lien**[law.justia.com](https://law.justia.com/cases/florida/second-district-court-of-appeal/2009/2d09-1764.html#:~:text=language%20of%20the%20lien%20discharge,2d%20434)[law.justia.com](https://law.justia.com/cases/florida/second-district-court-of-appeal/2009/2d09-1764.html#:~:text=with%20the%20statutory%20requirement%20that,4).
    The discharged lienor still retains the right to sue on the
    underlying contract (a **personal claim**), but **loses the
    security** of the
    lien[law.justia.com](https://law.justia.com/cases/florida/second-district-court-of-appeal/2009/2d09-1764.html#:~:text=period%29,4).
    For example, once Designerick’s lien was cancelled, it could
    continue its breach of contract suit for the $21k balance, but that
    claim would proceed **unsecured**, underlining the high stakes of
    compliance[law.justia.com](https://law.justia.com/cases/florida/second-district-court-of-appeal/2009/2d09-1764.html#:~:text=period%29,4).
    The case also underscores the strategic value of the show-cause
    procedure for owners: it can quickly eliminate stale or unsupported
    liens – provided the owner follows the statute to the letter and the
    lienor does not timely foreclose.

### Mainlands Construction Co. v. Wen-Dic Construction Co. (Fla. 1986)

  - **Parties:** Mainlands Construction (owner) vs. Wen-Dic Construction
    (contractor).

  - **Issue:** Whether a lienor’s **counterclaim for foreclosure** filed
    in response to a § 713.21(4) show-cause complaint is sufficient to
    avoid discharge, even if filed in an existing lawsuit rather than a
    new action, and whether such counterclaim must be in a particular
    form.

  - **Decision:** In *Mainlands*, the Florida Supreme Court upheld the
    validity of the lienor’s response because the lienor **did assert a
    foreclosure claim within the 20 days** (via counterclaim). This case
    established that a **counterclaim in the show-cause proceeding
    itself can satisfy** the “action to enforce the lien”
    requirement[law.justia.com](https://law.justia.com/cases/florida/second-district-court-of-appeal/2009/2d09-1764.html#:~:text=%28Fla,the%20lienor%27s%20motion%20to%20arbitrate).
    The Court treated the counterclaim seeking foreclosure as equivalent
    to commencing a separate foreclosure action for purposes of
    § 713.21(4). *Mainlands* thereby approved a **practical
    procedure**: the lienor need not file a new, separate lawsuit if a
    show-cause case is already pending; it can simply counterclaim for
    lien foreclosure in that case to preserve the lien.

  - **Significance:** The case is often cited (as in *Unnerstall*) to
    illustrate a **compliant lienor
    response**[law.justia.com](https://law.justia.com/cases/florida/second-district-court-of-appeal/2009/2d09-1764.html#:~:text=%28Fla,the%20lienor%27s%20motion%20to%20arbitrate).
    It contrasts with later cases where lienors responded with other
    claims or motions. The lesson is that **seeking foreclosure is key**
    – the form (counterclaim vs. separate suit) is less important than
    the substance of enforcing the lien. After *Mainlands*, the onus is
    squarely on lienors to include a foreclosure count or risk losing
    the lien. Owners, conversely, can feel secure that unless the lienor
    properly forecloses, the lien *must* be canceled by the court
    “forthwith” after 20
    days[law.justia.com](https://law.justia.com/cases/florida/second-district-court-of-appeal/2009/2d09-1764.html#:~:text=language%20of%20the%20lien%20discharge,2d%20434).
    This strict timeline was reinforced in *Mainlands* and subsequent
    cases as a **bright-line rule** in Florida lien law.

### Alexdex Corp. v. Nachon Enterprises, Inc. (Fla. 1994)

  - **Parties:** Alexdex Corp. (property owner) vs. Nachon Enterprises
    (contractor lienor).

  - **Issue:** Interaction between an owner’s show-cause action and a
    lienor’s **separate foreclosure action filed in a different court**;
    and whether filing in the wrong court affects the lien’s validity.
    Specifically, if a lienor files a timely foreclosure suit in
    **county court** (within one year, under § 713.22) but the amount in
    controversy exceeds the county court’s jurisdiction, can the lien
    still be discharged for lack of a proper action?

  - **Facts:** Nachon (contractor) recorded a construction lien and
    **filed a foreclosure lawsuit in county court** (which has a
    jurisdictional limit) for the amount
    due[law.justia.com](https://law.justia.com/cases/florida/supreme-court/1994/81765-0.html#:~:text=In%201991%2C%20appellant%20,that%20Nachon%20had%20not%20properly).
    Alexdex (owner) later filed a § 713.21(4) show-cause complaint in
    circuit court to discharge the
    lien[law.justia.com](https://law.justia.com/cases/florida/supreme-court/1994/81765-0.html#:~:text=In%201991%2C%20appellant%20,that%20Nachon%20had%20not%20properly).
    Nachon moved to dismiss the circuit show-cause action, pointing out
    that it had already **“timely instituted”** a lien foreclosure in
    county court (i.e. an action was
    pending)[law.justia.com](https://law.justia.com/cases/florida/supreme-court/1994/81765-0.html#:~:text=,that%20Nachon%20had%20not%20properly)[law.justia.com](https://law.justia.com/cases/florida/supreme-court/1994/81765-0.html#:~:text=the%20lien%2C%20which%20was%20filed,to%20the%20Show%20Cause%20Action).
    The circuit court nevertheless discharged the lien, reasoning that
    Nachon “had not properly responded” to the show-cause order –
    essentially because the foreclosure was filed in the *wrong level of
    court* (circuit vs. county) or not acknowledged in the circuit
    case[law.justia.com](https://law.justia.com/cases/florida/supreme-court/1994/81765-0.html#:~:text=the%20lien%2C%20which%20was%20filed,to%20the%20Show%20Cause%20Action).
    The Third DCA reversed that decision and **reinstated the lien**,
    holding Nachon’s county court action was a proper and timely
    foreclosure. The owner petitioned the Florida Supreme Court, arguing
    that only circuit courts had jurisdiction over lien foreclosures.

  - **Outcome:** The Florida Supreme Court **sided with the lienor and
    approved the Third DCA’s ruling**, holding that **circuit courts**
    *and county courts* **have concurrent jurisdiction** over lien
    foreclosure actions, up to the county court’s monetary
    limit[law.justia.com](https://law.justia.com/cases/florida/supreme-court/1994/81765-0.html#:~:text=Under%20jurisdiction%20granted%20to%20us,jurisdiction%20in%20matters%20of%20equity)[law.justia.com](https://law.justia.com/cases/florida/supreme-court/1994/81765-0.html#:~:text=district%20court%20of%20appeal%20reversed,We%20disagree).
    Importantly, the Supreme Court agreed that Nachon’s timely county
    court lawsuit **met the requirement of commencing an action** to
    enforce the lien, so the lien should not have been
    discharged[law.justia.com](https://law.justia.com/cases/florida/supreme-court/1994/81765-0.html#:~:text=the%20lien%2C%20which%20was%20filed,to%20the%20Show%20Cause%20Action)[law.justia.com](https://law.justia.com/cases/florida/supreme-court/1994/81765-0.html#:~:text=district%20court%20of%20appeal%20reversed,We%20disagree).
    In effect, the lien was **reinstated** after being wrongly
    discharged. The Court clarified that a mechanic’s lien foreclosure,
    though it involves an interest in real property, is fundamentally an
    **equitable action** and does not determine title or boundaries in a
    way that would *exclude* the county court’s
    jurisdiction[law.justia.com](https://law.justia.com/cases/florida/supreme-court/1994/81765-0.html#:~:text=district%20court%20of%20appeal%20reversed,We%20disagree)[law.justia.com](https://law.justia.com/cases/florida/supreme-court/1994/81765-0.html#:~:text=Contrary%20to%20the%20court%20below%2C,explained%20in%20Publix%20Super%20Markets).
    Thus, as long as the amount is within the statutory limit, a county
    court can hear a lien foreclosure.

  - **Key Reasoning:** The Supreme Court resolved a conflict between
    districts (some had said only circuit courts could foreclose
    liens)[law.justia.com](https://law.justia.com/cases/florida/supreme-court/1994/81765-0.html#:~:text=Under%20jurisdiction%20granted%20to%20us,jurisdiction%20in%20matters%20of%20equity)[law.justia.com](https://law.justia.com/cases/florida/supreme-court/1994/81765-0.html#:~:text=Contrary%20to%20the%20court%20below%2C,explained%20in%20Publix%20Super%20Markets).
    It interpreted the jurisdiction statutes to allow county courts to
    handle equitable lien cases within their monetary range. This meant
    the owner’s show-cause action in circuit court should have
    recognized the pending county case as a proper foreclosure. The
    decision prevented an overly technical forfeiture of lien rights due
    to a jurisdictional mix-up.

  - **Practical Implications:** *Alexdex* is a cautionary tale on
    procedural complexity. It illustrates that **owners must check if a
    lienor has already filed suit elsewhere** before pursuing a
    show-cause discharge. In Alexdex, the owner’s attempt to rush a
    discharge in circuit court failed because the lienor had in fact
    acted within its rights (just in a different
    forum)[law.justia.com](https://law.justia.com/cases/florida/supreme-court/1994/81765-0.html#:~:text=In%201991%2C%20appellant%20,that%20Nachon%20had%20not%20properly)[law.justia.com](https://law.justia.com/cases/florida/supreme-court/1994/81765-0.html#:~:text=the%20lien%2C%20which%20was%20filed,to%20the%20Show%20Cause%20Action).
    For lienors, the case was a relief: filing in the wrong court by
    mistake won’t automatically void the lien if the action was
    otherwise timely – the remedy may be to transfer the case, not lose
    the lien. *Alexdex* essentially saved Nachon’s lien from a
    procedural trap, reaffirming that **timely foreclosure within one
    year** (per § 713.22) is the paramount requirement, and courts will
    strive to uphold a lienor’s compliance with that deadline even if a
    technical misstep occurred. After this case, Florida owners and
    practitioners know to **coordinate forums**: a lienor’s proper (or
    even improperly-venued) lawsuit stops the clock on the lien’s
    expiration, and an order to show cause shouldn’t cancel a lien when
    an enforcement action is pending in any court of competent
    jurisdiction[law.justia.com](https://law.justia.com/cases/florida/supreme-court/1994/81765-0.html#:~:text=the%20lien%2C%20which%20was%20filed,to%20the%20Show%20Cause%20Action)[law.justia.com](https://law.justia.com/cases/florida/supreme-court/1994/81765-0.html#:~:text=district%20court%20of%20appeal%20reversed,We%20disagree).

  - **Related “Expiration” Cases:** Florida law also provides for
    automatic lien expiration after one year if no suit is filed
    (§ 713.21(3)). Courts strictly enforce this as well: *Scott v.
    Haufler*, 526 So. 2d 996 (Fla. 3d DCA 1988), for example, held that
    lienors who did not file foreclosure within one year “**accordingly
    \[had\] their liens… discharged**” by operation of
    § 713.21(3)[courtlistener.com](https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/7634399/scott-v-haufler/#:~:text=,3d%20DCA%201977%29).
    In **Diversified Mortgage Investors v. Benjamin**, 345 So. 2d 392
    (Fla. 3d DCA 1977), a case cited in *Scott*, the court likewise
    canceled an untimely lien. These cases, together with *Alexdex*,
    underscore the unforgiving nature of the timeline: whether by
    failing to file at all or filing in the wrong place and not
    correcting it, lienors risk **extinguishment** of the lien. The
    **practical best practice** is for lienors to file foreclosure well
    within the one-year limit (or 60 days if a Notice of Contest is
    served) and in the proper court, and for owners to use the Contest
    or show-cause remedies to hold lienors to those deadlines.

### Witters Contracting Co. v. West (Fla. 2d DCA 2020)

  - **Parties:** Witters Contracting Company (contractor) vs. West
    (homeowners).

  - **Issue:** Consequences of filing a **fraudulent, exaggerated lien**
    and refusing to release it. What constitutes a “willful
    exaggeration” under § 713.31, and what damages can an owner
    recover when a lien is deemed fraudulent?

  - **Facts:** Homeowners hired Witters to build a new home under a
    cost-plus
    contract[buildsmartbradley.com](https://www.buildsmartbradley.com/2020/07/fraudulently-filing-lien-backfires-on-contractor/#:~:text=In%20that%20case%2C%20a%20couple,an%20unpaid%20balance%20of%20%2475%2C000).
    The relationship deteriorated, and Witters made a demand for payment
    of \~$30,000, threatening to file a lien for $100,000 if not
    paid[buildsmartbradley.com](https://www.buildsmartbradley.com/2020/07/fraudulently-filing-lien-backfires-on-contractor/#:~:text=the%20pitfalls%20that%20may%20befall,abused%20the%20lien%20filing%20process).
    Shortly after, Witters’ attorney sent a letter claiming about
    $59,706 was due, and then Witters filed a **claim of lien for
    $75,000**[buildsmartbradley.com](https://www.buildsmartbradley.com/2020/07/fraudulently-filing-lien-backfires-on-contractor/#:~:text=Witters%20Contracting%20Company%2C%20to%20build,an%20unpaid%20balance%20of%20%2475%2C000).
    The homeowners sued Witters, alleging the lien was **fraudulent**
    and constituted slander of title (they also withheld payment,
    disputing the amounts). Witters countersued for breach of contract
    and quantum meruit. The homeowners moved for summary judgment,
    arguing the lien was willfully inflated. The trial court agreed –
    finding the lien was compiled with such **gross negligence or
    exaggeration** as to be willful and
    fraudulent[buildsmartbradley.com](https://www.buildsmartbradley.com/2020/07/fraudulently-filing-lien-backfires-on-contractor/#:~:text=counterclaims,the%20summary%20judgment%20motion%20finding).
    The court cited the inconsistencies in Witters’ demands ($30k, then
    \~$60k, then a $75k lien) as evidence of bad faith. It declared the
    lien fraudulent and slanderous, ordered the lien released, and
    reserved jurisdiction to award damages. After a separate trial on
    damages, the court awarded the owners **over $180,000** in damages,
    including attorneys’ fees, costs, and punitive
    damages[buildsmartbradley.com](https://www.buildsmartbradley.com/2020/07/fraudulently-filing-lien-backfires-on-contractor/#:~:text=liens%20constitutes%20a%20slander%20of,title%20upon%20Plaintiffs%E2%80%99%20real%20property)[buildsmartbradley.com](https://www.buildsmartbradley.com/2020/07/fraudulently-filing-lien-backfires-on-contractor/#:~:text=After%20a%20trial%20on%20damages%2C,The%20contractor%20appealed).
    Witters appealed, particularly objecting to the personal liability
    of its company’s owner for the judgment.

  - **Outcome:** The Second DCA **affirmed** the finding of a fraudulent
    lien and the damages against the contracting
    company[buildsmartbradley.com](https://www.buildsmartbradley.com/2020/07/fraudulently-filing-lien-backfires-on-contractor/#:~:text=On%20appeal%2C%20the%20Second%20District,personally%20liable%20for%20the%20judgment).
    The appellate court agreed that Witters willfully exaggerated its
    lien (a violation of § 713.31(2)(a)) and that this supported the
    summary judgment for the owners. The $180k judgment (which likely
    included statutory punitive damages up to the difference between the
    lien amount and actual due, per § 713.31) was upheld against the
    company. However, the 2d DCA reversed the judgment to the extent it
    made the individual owner of Witters personally liable – presumably
    because there wasn’t a sufficient basis to pierce the corporate veil
    or hold him individually responsible absent a specific statutory
    provision[buildsmartbradley.com](https://www.buildsmartbradley.com/2020/07/fraudulently-filing-lien-backfires-on-contractor/#:~:text=On%20appeal%2C%20the%20Second%20District,personally%20liable%20for%20the%20judgment).
    The lien itself was nullified due to the fraud, and the owners were
    recognized as the prevailing party entitled to fees.

  - **Legal Principles:** Under § 713.31, a lien is fraudulent if the
    lienor has willfully exaggerated the amount, willfully included work
    not performed or materials not furnished, or compiled the claim with
    such gross negligence as to amount to willful
    exaggeration[jimersonfirm.com](https://www.jimersonfirm.com/blog/resources/faqs/construction-law/#:~:text=Q%3A%20What%20is%20a%20fraudulent,the%20penalties%20for%20filing%20one)[jimersonfirm.com](https://www.jimersonfirm.com/blog/resources/faqs/construction-law/#:~:text=A%3A%C2%A0%20A%20fraudulent%20lien%20is,31).
    A “good-faith dispute” or minor mistake is *not* fraudulent, but a
    knowing overstatement is. In *Witters*, the court found the
    contractor’s shifting and inflated claims went beyond any good-faith
    dispute – they were a tactical **overreach to coerce
    payment**[buildsmartbradley.com](https://www.buildsmartbradley.com/2020/07/fraudulently-filing-lien-backfires-on-contractor/#:~:text=undisputedly%20owed%20to%20the%20lienor,abused%20the%20lien%20filing%20process)[buildsmartbradley.com](https://www.buildsmartbradley.com/2020/07/fraudulently-filing-lien-backfires-on-contractor/#:~:text=Witters%20Contracting%20Company%2C%20to%20build,an%20unpaid%20balance%20of%20%2475%2C000).
    The decision exemplifies that Florida courts will impose **harsh
    penalties for fraudulent liens**. Slander of title was effectively
    proven as well, since a fraudulent lien is an encumbrance based on
    falsehood. The damages awarded (fees, costs, punitive damages) align
    with § 713.31(2)(c), which entitles the wronged owner to recover
    **all costs of clearing the lien** (including bond premiums, if any,
    interest on deposits, etc.) and punitive damages up to the
    difference between the claimed and actual
    owed[tabbaafirm.com](https://tabbaafirm.com/blog-panama-city-law-firm-the-tabbaa-firm/construction-liens/#:~:text=Fraudulent%20Claim%20of%20Lien)[tabbaafirm.com](https://tabbaafirm.com/blog-panama-city-law-firm-the-tabbaa-firm/construction-liens/#:~:text=damages%20occasioned%20thereby,the%20lien%2C%20the%20amount%20of).

  - **Common Errors by Lienors:** *Willful exaggeration.* Some lienors
    mistakenly believe overstating a lien provides leverage; *Witters*
    shows this can backfire
    catastrophically[buildsmartbradley.com](https://www.buildsmartbradley.com/2020/07/fraudulently-filing-lien-backfires-on-contractor/#:~:text=defective%20filing%2C%20that%20party%20may,abused%20the%20lien%20filing%20process)[buildsmartbradley.com](https://www.buildsmartbradley.com/2020/07/fraudulently-filing-lien-backfires-on-contractor/#:~:text=Contracting%2C%20you%20are%20found%20to,consequences%20of%20a%20defective%20filing).
    Another error is **refusal to release** a clearly defective lien. In
    this case, even after owners’ complaints, the contractor persisted
    with its inflated lien, resulting in liability for slander of title.
    The case’s **“takeaways”** (noted by commentators) are that filing a
    lien with incorrect or inflated figures, or after one’s rights have
    expired, can subject a contractor to **substantial liability**, far
    outweighing any leverage
    gained[buildsmartbradley.com](https://www.buildsmartbradley.com/2020/07/fraudulently-filing-lien-backfires-on-contractor/#:~:text=downstream%20parties%20secure%20payment%20rights,to%20have%20abused%20the%20lien)[buildsmartbradley.com](https://www.buildsmartbradley.com/2020/07/fraudulently-filing-lien-backfires-on-contractor/#:~:text=companies%20do%20not%20fully%20appreciate,legal%20implications%20of%20filing%20liens).

  - **Impact:** *Witters* is frequently cited as a warning that
    Florida’s lien law has teeth to punish
    abuse[buildsmartbradley.com](https://www.buildsmartbradley.com/2020/07/fraudulently-filing-lien-backfires-on-contractor/#:~:text=defective%20filing%2C%20that%20party%20may,abused%20the%20lien%20filing%20process)[buildsmartbradley.com](https://www.buildsmartbradley.com/2020/07/fraudulently-filing-lien-backfires-on-contractor/#:~:text=Additionally%2C%20for%20upstream%20parties%20facing,the%20demands%20of%20the%20lienor).
    The trend in such cases is zero tolerance for fraud: courts uphold
    **voiding of the lien** (making it unenforceable) and also layer on
    **monetary consequences**. For owners, *Witters* and similar cases
    (e.g. *Viyella Co. v. Gomes*, 657 So. 2d 83 (Fla. 3d DCA 1995);
    *Delta Painting, Inc. v. Baumann*, 710 So. 2d 663 (Fla. 3d DCA
    1998)) provide a roadmap to relief when faced with egregiously false
    liens. Rather than automatically bonding off or paying a suspect
    lien, an owner can sue under § 713.31 for fraudulent lien – a
    strategy that “has some teeth,” as one construction attorney
    noted[buildsmartbradley.com](https://www.buildsmartbradley.com/2020/07/fraudulently-filing-lien-backfires-on-contractor/#:~:text=by%20filing%20a%20lien%20may,consequences%20of%20a%20defective%20filing)[buildsmartbradley.com](https://www.buildsmartbradley.com/2020/07/fraudulently-filing-lien-backfires-on-contractor/#:~:text=Additionally%2C%20for%20upstream%20parties%20facing,the%20demands%20of%20the%20lienor).
    The availability of attorneys’ fees and punitive damages gives
    owners a powerful deterrent against lienors who might be tempted to
    file inflated claims. In summary, the best practice for lienors is
    to **only claim what is due** and release any lien that turns out to
    be invalid, to avoid the fate of Witters Contracting.

## Recurring Issues and Best Practices in Lien Release/Cancellation

**1. Strict Statutory Compliance:** A clear theme in the case law is
that Florida courts demand **strict compliance** with the Construction
Lien Law’s technical
requirements[floridabar.org](https://www.floridabar.org/the-florida-bar-journal/floridas-unwieldy-but-effective-construction-lien-law/#:~:text=The%20lien%20law%E2%80%99s%20strictest%20application,to%20a%20project%20to%20ascertain)[floridabar.org](https://www.floridabar.org/the-florida-bar-journal/floridas-unwieldy-but-effective-construction-lien-law/#:~:text=attached%20to%20it,of%20the%2090%20day%20period).
Lien rights are “purely creature of statute,” so one must “allege and
prove strict compliance with every
requirement”[floridabar.org](https://www.floridabar.org/the-florida-bar-journal/floridas-unwieldy-but-effective-construction-lien-law/#:~:text=Ch,principles%20that%20culminated%20in%20the).
Common missteps that lead to loss of lien rights include: failing to
serve a Notice to Owner (as in *Calixte*), filing a lien too late or
with incorrect details, or failing to foreclose in time (as in *Scott v.
Haufler*, *Diversified Mortgage*). **Trend:** Courts do not use equity
to save a lienor who neglects the statute (indeed § 713.37 forbids
liberal construction). The flip side is that owners must also follow
statutory procedures exactly (e.g. properly serving a Notice of Contest
or show-cause summons) to accelerate lien removal. The *Calixte*
decision exemplifies that even a well-intentioned owner can be stymied
by a literal statutory
caveat[hammerngavel.com](https://hammerngavel.com/blog/court-refuses-to-discharge-lien-where-subcontractor-failed-to-serve-notice-to-owner#:~:text=lien%20rights%2C%20,proper%20remedy%20under%20such%20circumstances).
**Best practice:** Lienors should create internal checklists for all
Chapter 713 steps (Notices, recording times, service of lien,
foreclosure deadlines). Owners and their counsel should similarly diary
the **one-year expiration** and consider using the **60-day Notice of
Contest** (§ 713.22) to shorten that period when appropriate. Both sides
must respect that there is “no room for error” in lien technicalities –
errors will result in the lien being void or
discharged[jimersonfirm.com](https://www.jimersonfirm.com/blog/resources/faqs/construction-law/#:~:text=Q%3A%20What%20is%20a%20fraudulent,the%20penalties%20for%20filing%20one)[jimersonfirm.com](https://www.jimersonfirm.com/blog/resources/faqs/construction-law/#:~:text=Fraudulent%20liens%20typically%20involve%20situations,rise%20to%20a%20fraudulent%20lien).

**2. Releasing Liens Upon Payment – Proper Form and Timing:**
Section 713.21 provides that a lien can be discharged by recording a
**satisfaction or release** signed by the
lienor[tabbaafirm.com](https://tabbaafirm.com/blog-panama-city-law-firm-the-tabbaa-firm/construction-liens/#:~:text=Discharge%20or%20cancellation%20of%20a,21)[tabbaafirm.com](https://tabbaafirm.com/blog-panama-city-law-firm-the-tabbaa-firm/construction-liens/#:~:text=,person%20relying%20on%20the%20same).
A recurring issue is lienors failing to promptly record a satisfaction
after payment. While § 713.21 does not impose an explicit deadline,
failing to remove a lien that has been paid can expose the lienor to a
lawsuit (for slander of title or under the owner’s contract). Florida
encourages owners to **condition each payment on receiving a lien
release**[jimersonfirm.com](https://www.jimersonfirm.com/blog/resources/faqs/construction-law/#:~:text=giving%20a%20Notice%20to%20Owner,end%20up%20paying%20twice%20for)[jimersonfirm.com](https://www.jimersonfirm.com/blog/resources/faqs/construction-law/#:~:text=prior%20to%20each%20payment%2C%20the,from%20liability%20for%20those%20payments).
In fact, if an owner pays the contractor without obtaining releases from
subcontractors who served Notices to Owner, those payments are deemed
“*improper*,” and the owner **loses certain defenses**, potentially
having to pay
twice[jimersonfirm.com](https://www.jimersonfirm.com/blog/resources/faqs/construction-law/#:~:text=giving%20a%20Notice%20to%20Owner,end%20up%20paying%20twice%20for)[jimersonfirm.com](https://www.jimersonfirm.com/blog/resources/faqs/construction-law/#:~:text=each%20payment%20to%20the%20contractor,from%20liability%20for%20those%20payments).
*Best practice for owners:* Always require **partial lien waivers** from
all lienors up the chain for the amount of each progress payment, and a
**final release of lien** for final
payment[jimersonfirm.com](https://www.jimersonfirm.com/blog/resources/faqs/construction-law/#:~:text=Fla,If%20the%20owner%E2%80%99s%20payments)[jimersonfirm.com](https://www.jimersonfirm.com/blog/resources/faqs/construction-law/#:~:text=each%20payment%20to%20the%20contractor,from%20liability%20for%20those%20payments).
This “closes the loop” and protects the owner from liens by those who
were paid via the general
contractor[jimersonfirm.com](https://www.jimersonfirm.com/blog/resources/faqs/construction-law/#:~:text=each%20payment%20to%20the%20contractor,from%20liability%20for%20those%20payments).
If a lien is filed after an owner made improper payments (without
releases), the owner cannot use the “proper payments” defense and will
be liable – a hard lesson learned in various disputes.

For lienors, **releasing the lien upon payment** isn’t just courteous;
it’s required to avoid clouding the owner’s title. Florida law even
makes it a **misdemeanor to refuse to release a paid lien** in some
circumstances (see §713.29(3), Fla. Stat.). Many construction contracts
also impose penalties or fee-shifting if a lien isn’t removed after
payment. Thus, lienors should record a satisfaction **immediately** once
paid in full (or paid the agreed amount for a partial release). Under
§713.21(2), a recorded release should recite the official record
book/page of the original lien and be signed and notarized by the
lienor[tabbaafirm.com](https://tabbaafirm.com/blog-panama-city-law-firm-the-tabbaa-firm/construction-liens/#:~:text=,person%20relying%20on%20the%20same)[tabbaafirm.com](https://tabbaafirm.com/blog-panama-city-law-firm-the-tabbaa-firm/construction-liens/#:~:text=notarized%20signature%20and%20set%20forth,person%20relying%20on%20the%20same)
so that it can be readily indexed and verified. *Common mistake:*
Lienors sometimes send an owner an unrecorded release and assume that
suffices. An unrecorded release **does not clear the public records**
until it is actually recorded with the clerk. Owners should ensure any
release is in recordable form and follow through to see it recorded (or
record it themselves if authorized).

**3. Lien Waiver Forms – Statutory vs. Non-Statutory:** Florida provides
**simple form lien waivers** in § 713.20(4) (progress payment) and (5)
(final
payment)[syfert.com](https://syfert.com/florida/statutes/0713.20.html#:~:text=WAIVER%20AND%20RELEASE%20OF%20LIEN,UPON%20PROGRESS%20PAYMENT)[syfert.com](https://syfert.com/florida/statutes/0713.20.html#:~:text=WAIVER%20AND%20RELEASE%20OF%20LIEN,UPON%20FINAL%20PAYMENT),
and § 713.20(6) explicitly forbids requiring a lienor to sign a waiver
“different from the forms” in the
statute[syfert.com](https://syfert.com/florida/statutes/0713.20.html#:~:text=,5).
Despite this, it is *common* in practice for owners or GCs to present
**broader waiver forms** that waive not just lien rights but also any
other claims up to the date of the
release[floridabar.org](https://www.floridabar.org/the-florida-bar-journal/floridas-unwieldy-but-effective-construction-lien-law/#:~:text=admonition%20is%20circumvented%20by%20incorporating,70)[floridabar.org](https://www.floridabar.org/the-florida-bar-journal/floridas-unwieldy-but-effective-construction-lien-law/#:~:text=additions%20to%20the%20statutory%20lien,70).
As noted in one FAQ, “sneaky contractors” use non-statutory forms with
clauses waiving delay damages, unapproved change orders, or other
claims[jimersonfirm.com](https://www.jimersonfirm.com/blog/resources/faqs/construction-law/#:~:text=A%3A%20Many%20general%20contractors%20and,Once).
The statute attempts to protect lienors, but many lienors feel compelled
to sign these forms as a condition of payment. Florida law (per
§713.20(8)) says that a waiver that deviates from the statutory form is
**enforceable according to its
terms**[floridabar.org](https://www.floridabar.org/the-florida-bar-journal/floridas-unwieldy-but-effective-construction-lien-law/#:~:text=admonition%20is%20circumvented%20by%20incorporating,70)[floridabar.org](https://www.floridabar.org/the-florida-bar-journal/floridas-unwieldy-but-effective-construction-lien-law/#:~:text=match%20at%20L697%2069%20%C2%A0Fla,20%286)
– meaning if a subcontractor signs a broad form releasing “all claims”
through a date, he may later be barred from claiming extras or delay
costs accrued before that date. **Best Practices:** *For lienors:* Never
sign a waiver or release that is broader than the situation warrants.
Strike any language that waives claims other than lien rights, or insist
on using the statutory form. If pressured, remember the law is on your
side that you cannot be *required* to use a different
form[jimersonfirm.com](https://www.jimersonfirm.com/blog/resources/faqs/construction-law/#:~:text=private%20and%20public%20construction%20projects,done%20through%20a%20certain%20time)[jimersonfirm.com](https://www.jimersonfirm.com/blog/resources/faqs/construction-law/#:~:text=A%3A%20Many%20general%20contractors%20and,Once).
In negotiations, change contract clauses that demand pre-payment waivers
– modify them to say “exchange waiver upon payment,” so you’re not
releasing liens before you actually receive
funds[jimersonfirm.com](https://www.jimersonfirm.com/blog/resources/faqs/construction-law/#:~:text=)[jimersonfirm.com](https://www.jimersonfirm.com/blog/resources/faqs/construction-law/#:~:text=subcontractors%2C%20the%20releases%20will%20be,the%20nonpayment%20to%20the%20subcontractors).
*For owners/GCs:* Use the statutory forms – they are well-understood and
less likely to generate disputes or
mistakes[levelset.com](https://www.levelset.com/blog/florida-lien-waiver-mistakes/#:~:text=Florida%20Lien%20Waivers%20,just%20use%20the%20statutory%20form).
Overreaching with a complex waiver form might save you from some claims,
but it could also be deemed unenforceable or cause a subcontractor to
refuse to sign, stalling the project. Notably, if a subcontractor *does*
refuse to sign a non-statutory waiver, the owner **cannot withhold
“proper payment”** on that basis; if the owner does and a lien
results, the owner may be unable to use the “paid in full”
defense[jimersonfirm.com](https://www.jimersonfirm.com/blog/resources/faqs/construction-law/#:~:text=form%20different%20from%20the%20statutory,using%20proper%20payment%20as%20a)
(because the subcontractor was justified in refusing the overbroad
form). In summary, the cleanest approach is: **exchange a statutory-form
partial release with each progress payment, and a final release for
final payment.**

**4. Conditional Releases & Bad Checks:** A recurring practical problem
is when a lienor provides an unconditional release of lien in exchange
for a payment that later bounces (non-sufficient funds) or a payment
that never materializes (in cases of dispute). Florida courts have held
that even a **returned (bounced) check constitutes “valid consideration”
to support a lien release**, *“despite the money not actually being
transferred.”*[jimersonfirm.com](https://www.jimersonfirm.com/blog/resources/faqs/construction-law/#:~:text=Q%3A%20What%20can%20I%20do,return%20for%20a%20worthless%20check)
In other words, if a lienor signs an unconditional waiver upon receiving
a check, and that check is later dishonored, the lienor *may have
inadvertently given up the lien* without getting paid. The law does
allow lienors to **condition a waiver on actual payment**, but this must
be **expressly written** on the release
form[syfert.com](https://syfert.com/florida/statutes/0713.20.html#:~:text=,any%20such%20condition%20is%20satisfied)[jimersonfirm.com](https://www.jimersonfirm.com/blog/resources/faqs/construction-law/#:~:text=A%3A%20Surprisingly%2C%20this%20issue%20comes,is%20void%E2%80%9D%20or%20words%20of).
The standard statutory forms do *not* include conditional language by
default (they assume payment is actually received), so it’s up to the
lienor to modify the document if needed. **Best Practice for Lienors:**
When payment is made by check (or other not-immediately-cleared method),
write a clear condition on the release, such as: *“This release is
conditioned upon the check/payment referenced herein clearing the bank.
If said payment is not received or is dishonored, this release is
void.”*[jimersonfirm.com](https://www.jimersonfirm.com/blog/resources/faqs/construction-law/#:~:text=A%3A%20Surprisingly%2C%20this%20issue%20comes,is%20void%E2%80%9D%20or%20words%20of)
This kind of proviso, as suggested in Florida construction law
guides[jimersonfirm.com](https://www.jimersonfirm.com/blog/resources/faqs/construction-law/#:~:text=A%3A%20Surprisingly%2C%20this%20issue%20comes,is%20void%E2%80%9D%20or%20words%20of),
will protect the lienor. Florida’s statute §713.20(7) explicitly states
a lienor who takes a check *may* condition the release on payment of the
check, and if so, the owner may withhold corresponding funds from the
contractor until the check clears (to protect the
owner)[syfert.com](https://syfert.com/florida/statutes/0713.20.html#:~:text=,any%20such%20condition%20is%20satisfied)[syfert.com](https://syfert.com/florida/statutes/0713.20.html#:~:text=condition%20the%20waiver%20and%20release,any%20such%20condition%20is%20satisfied).
*Common mistake:* Lienors sometimes sign the release on the spot when
handed a check, without adding any reservation, trusting the payer –
only to find the payment fails. As noted, courts have little sympathy in
that scenario; the unconditional release is
binding[jimersonfirm.com](https://www.jimersonfirm.com/blog/resources/faqs/construction-law/#:~:text=Q%3A%20What%20can%20I%20do,return%20for%20a%20worthless%20check).
Owners, on the other hand, should **insist on unconditional releases**
only when they know the payment is valid (e.g., a certified check or
wire). If an owner agrees to a conditional release, they should ensure
the condition is satisfied (the check clears) to finalize the lien
discharge. Clear communication and using escrow (release delivered upon
confirmation of funds) can also avoid these issues.

**5. Fraudulent Lien Claims and Penalties:** The case law (e.g. *Witters
v. West*, discussed above) demonstrates that filing a fraudulent lien is
one of the costliest errors a lienor can make. A “fraudulent lien” under
§713.31 not only is **void/unenforceable**, but exposes the lienor to
**punitive damages, attorneys’ fees, and even criminal liability**
(filing a willfully fraudulent lien is a 3rd-degree felony in
Florida)[floridabar.org](https://www.floridabar.org/the-florida-bar-journal/floridas-unwieldy-but-effective-construction-lien-law/#:~:text=A%20lien%20is%20deemed%20fraudulent,claim%20for%20work%20not)leg.state.fl.us.
**Trends:** Courts distinguish between minor good-faith mistakes and
willful exaggeration. They have shown some leniency for *bona fide*
disputes over amount (e.g., a slight overestimation due to a pending
change order won’t automatically be deemed
fraudulent)[floridabar.org](https://www.floridabar.org/the-florida-bar-journal/floridas-unwieldy-but-effective-construction-lien-law/#:~:text=Exaggerated%20lien%20amounts%20may%20also,3d%20DCA%202008%29%2C%20reduced)[floridabar.org](https://www.floridabar.org/the-florida-bar-journal/floridas-unwieldy-but-effective-construction-lien-law/#:~:text=Ten%20years%20earlier%2C%20the%20same,Gomes%2C%20%20657).
But if the exaggeration is significant and not credibly explained,
judges readily find intent to defraud. For example, in *Witters*, the
contractor’s threat and inconsistent invoices helped prove the lien was
a sham leverage
tool[buildsmartbradley.com](https://www.buildsmartbradley.com/2020/07/fraudulently-filing-lien-backfires-on-contractor/#:~:text=In%20that%20case%2C%20a%20couple,an%20unpaid%20balance%20of%20%2475%2C000)[buildsmartbradley.com](https://www.buildsmartbradley.com/2020/07/fraudulently-filing-lien-backfires-on-contractor/#:~:text=counterclaims,the%20summary%20judgment%20motion%20finding).
**Best Practices:** *For Lienors:* Always lien for the exact amount you
genuinely believe is due under the contract, and never include
non-lienable items (like attorney fees, or work not yet done). If
there’s a good-faith dispute, document why you believe the amount is
proper. If you realize a filed lien is mistaken, **release or amend it
promptly** – do not leave a known false lien on the property. *For
Owners:* If you suspect a lien is willfully exaggerated, consider the
**fraudulent lien remedies**. Section 713.31 allows an action “in
connection with a summons to show cause under s. 713.21” or as a
standalone
suit[tabbaafirm.com](https://tabbaafirm.com/blog-panama-city-law-firm-the-tabbaa-firm/construction-liens/#:~:text=Fraudulent%20Claim%20of%20Lien)[tabbaafirm.com](https://tabbaafirm.com/blog-panama-city-law-firm-the-tabbaa-firm/construction-liens/#:~:text=or%20any%20contractor%2C%20subcontractor%2C%20or,action%20under%20this%20subsection%20in).
In practice, an owner can countersue for fraudulent lien within a lien
foreclosure action, or file an independent claim. Owners can recover all
costs of discharging the lien (attorney’s fees, bond premiums, interest)
and potentially **punitive damages** equal to the difference between the
claimed amount and the amount actually
due[tabbaafirm.com](https://tabbaafirm.com/blog-panama-city-law-firm-the-tabbaa-firm/construction-liens/#:~:text=damages%20occasioned%20thereby,the%20lien%2C%20the%20amount%20of)[tabbaafirm.com](https://tabbaafirm.com/blog-panama-city-law-firm-the-tabbaa-firm/construction-liens/#:~:text=owner%20or%20the%20defrauded%20party,due%20or%20to%20become%20due).
That threat often persuades an overzealous lienor to release or settle.
However, owners should also be cautious: courts require solid proof of
fraudulent intent, so the remedy is best pursued when the exaggeration
is egregious (e.g., billing for work not performed, or a lien amount
*far* above the contract balance with no support). In closer cases, an
expedited show-cause procedure or negotiation might resolve the lien
more efficiently.

**6. Avoiding Lien Litigation – Practical Tips:** The body of case law
suggests several proactive steps for both sides to avoid ending up in
costly litigation over lien releases or cancellations:

  - **For Owners:**
    
      - **Secure lien waivers at every payment:** As noted, obtaining
        signed releases from all potential lienors (contractors, subs,
        suppliers with Notices to Owner) each time you disburse funds is
        crucial[jimersonfirm.com](https://www.jimersonfirm.com/blog/resources/faqs/construction-law/#:~:text=giving%20a%20Notice%20to%20Owner,end%20up%20paying%20twice%20for)[jimersonfirm.com](https://www.jimersonfirm.com/blog/resources/faqs/construction-law/#:~:text=prior%20to%20each%20payment%2C%20the,from%20liability%20for%20those%20payments).
        This prevents liens from arising in the first place. Keep
        careful records of these releases.
    
      - **Use Notices of Contest and Bonds:** If a lien is recorded and
        you dispute it, you can record a **Notice of Contest of Lien**
        (per §713.22(2)) to shorten the enforcement deadline to 60 days.
        If you need the lien off title immediately (e.g., for a sale or
        refinance), consider transferring it to a **Lien Transfer Bond**
        under §713.24. Bonding off a lien doesn’t extinguish it, but it
        frees the property from the encumbrance, after which a court
        fight can happen against the security of the bond instead of the
        real estate.
    
      - **Communicate and mediate:** Many liens (especially those that
        are overstated or premature) can be resolved by communication.
        Before suing for fraudulent lien or firing off show-cause
        complaints, a practical owner will often send a **demand
        letter** citing the specific defects in the lien and asking for
        a release. Because §713.21(4) show-cause is now known to have
        limitations (*Calixte*), it may be more fruitful to negotiate or
        use the Contest procedure unless the lienor is completely
        non-responsive.
    
      - **Include lien release provisions in contracts:** Owners can
        protect themselves by contractually requiring the contractor to
        **promptly remove any liens** at its expense (for example,
        through bonding-off) and making this an obligation in the
        construction contract. This way, if a sub files a lien, the
        prime contractor is bound to address it or face breach of
        contract. While this doesn’t eliminate the lien, it gives the
        owner a direct claim against the contractor for any damages or
        delays caused by liens.

  - **For Lienors (Contractors, Subcontractors, Suppliers):**
    
      - **Meticulous compliance with notice and timing:** Serve the
        Notice to Owner (if required) by the 45th day, record the Claim
        of Lien by the 90th day from final work, and **serve a copy of
        the lien on the owner within 15 days of recording** (as required
        by §713.08). Failure to timely serve the lien can render it
        voidable if prejudice is shownleg.state.fl.us. These steps are
        the foundation of a valid lien – courts will not forgive lapses
        here.
    
      - **Use correct lien forms and content:** Florida’s lien form (in
        §713.08) must be followed – it should state the required
        information (name of lienor, name of customer, description of
        work, amount due, property description, first and last date of
        work,
        etc.)[tabbaafirm.com](https://tabbaafirm.com/blog-panama-city-law-firm-the-tabbaa-firm/construction-liens/#:~:text=A%20claim%20of%20lien%20is,or%20materials%20by%20the%20lienor)[tabbaafirm.com](https://tabbaafirm.com/blog-panama-city-law-firm-the-tabbaa-firm/construction-liens/#:~:text=A%20claim%20of%20lien%20must,state%20the%20following).
        Avoid common errors like misstating the property description or
        the last work date. Even if a lien isn’t perfectly filled out,
        minor errors might be tolerated, but anything substantive (wrong
        owner, exaggerated amount, work not lienable) can doom the
        lien[floridabar.org](https://www.floridabar.org/the-florida-bar-journal/floridas-unwieldy-but-effective-construction-lien-law/#:~:text=first%20and%20last%20dates%20of,49)[floridabar.org](https://www.floridabar.org/the-florida-bar-journal/floridas-unwieldy-but-effective-construction-lien-law/#:~:text=errors%20have%20the%20least%20adverse,49).
    
      - **Respond to owner’s show-cause or contest actions
        immediately:** If you receive a show-cause summons (713.21(4)),
        **file a foreclosure action (or counterclaim) well within the 20
        days** – ideally, get legal counsel to ensure the pleading is
        correct. If an owner records a Notice of Contest, remember that
        **your one-year window shrinks to 60 days** – do not let it
        lapse. Many liens have been lost because lienors mis-calendared
        the shortened period.
    
      - **Release the lien when appropriate:** Do not wield the lien as
        a weapon longer than necessary. If you get paid, release it of
        record. If you reach a settlement, include an exchange of a
        release. If a lien was mistakenly filed (e.g., you later realize
        it was on the wrong property or for the wrong amount), releasing
        it can avoid further liability. Florida law allows an “Amended
        Claim of Lien” in some cases if within the original deadline,
        but once the deadline passes, you cannot fix a defective lien –
        except to release it and perhaps file a new one if still within
        90 days of last work. Always err on the side of keeping liens
        accurate and current.
    
      - **Don’t overreach on lien rights:** Lien only for the **real
        property improvement** portion of the dispute. Items like lost
        profits on unapproved change orders, or contract damages due to
        project delays (unless they directly caused labor/material costs
        on the job), are generally not lienable. Filing for non-lienable
        items can make the whole lien
        fraudulent[buildsmartbradley.com](https://www.buildsmartbradley.com/2020/07/fraudulently-filing-lien-backfires-on-contractor/#:~:text=,title%20upon%20Plaintiffs%E2%80%99%20real%20property)[jimersonfirm.com](https://www.jimersonfirm.com/blog/resources/faqs/construction-law/#:~:text=A%3A%C2%A0%20A%20fraudulent%20lien%20is,31).
        Similarly, if the owner has a valid defense (e.g., the work was
        never performed or was defective and redone by others), evaluate
        whether pursuing a lien is appropriate or if a simple lawsuit
        (without a lien) is safer to avoid fraud allegations.

**7. Trends in Judicial Interpretation:** In summary, Florida courts
trend toward a **literal enforcement** of lien statutes (“strict
compliance”) combined with a readiness to penalize abuse (fraudulent
liens). However, as seen in *Alexdex* and a few others, courts will
avoid absurd results (like canceling a lien when the lienor did
everything right in the wrong forum). The overarching message is:
**follow the statute to the letter, and you will usually be protected;
deviate, and you likely lose the lien.** Procedural shortcuts by owners
(like trying to use show-cause on a non-perfected lien) are struck
down[hammerngavel.com](https://hammerngavel.com/blog/court-refuses-to-discharge-lien-where-subcontractor-failed-to-serve-notice-to-owner#:~:text=lien%20rights%2C%20,proper%20remedy%20under%20such%20circumstances),
just as procedural missteps by lienors (like answering without a
foreclosure count) lead to lien
loss[law.justia.com](https://law.justia.com/cases/florida/second-district-court-of-appeal/2009/2d09-1764.html#:~:text=Designerick%20did%20not%20request%20foreclosure%2C,the%20lien%20be%20filed%20or).
This predictability is useful: everyone knows the rules of the game.

**8. Practical Recommendations:** To avoid litigation or statutory
penalties:

  - **Educate project participants** on lien law basics. Many compliance
    errors are simply due to ignorance of the requirements. A
    subcontractor unaware of the Notice to Owner requirement might lose
    lien rights unintentionally; conversely an owner unaware of the need
    for interim releases might assume (wrongly) that paying the
    contractor alone insulates them. Early in a project, sending out a
    summary of lien protocols (who must send notices, who will be
    exchanging releases, etc.) can set expectations.

  - **Consult attorneys early** when a payment dispute arises. If an
    owner hasn’t paid, a lienor should have counsel review its lien for
    accuracy before recording. If an owner faces a lien, consulting
    counsel about the lien’s validity can determine if a quick action
    (contest, bond, or show-cause) is viable or if it’s better to
    negotiate. The cost of early legal advice is trivial compared to a
    drawn-out foreclosure lawsuit or a wrongful lien claim later.

  - **Keep lines of communication open.** Many liens get filed because
    of poor communication – a contractor isn’t sure why payment is
    delayed and files a lien out of caution, or an owner doesn’t realize
    a subcontractor wasn’t paid. Proactive communication (and proper use
    of *Conditional* lien waivers that signal payment dependencies) can
    prevent surprises that lead to liens.

In conclusion, Florida’s private-project lien release and cancellation
jurisprudence reveals a dynamic tension between **protecting owners from
invalid liens** and **protecting lienors’ rights if they play by the
rules**. Common pitfalls – untimely enforcement, improper waiver forms,
exaggerated claims, and procedural missteps – have tripped up many
parties, but they also offer lessons. The trends show that courts will
enforce the **letter of the lien law** strictly (no “fairness”
exceptions for tough luck), will punish misconduct harshly, and will
expect both owners and contractors to know and use the statutory
mechanisms at their disposal. Adhering to best practices drawn from
these cases is the surest way to avoid litigation or, if it cannot be
avoided, to emerge on the winning side of a lien dispute.
